A Vote by Standing

A political adventure of Helen Natalia Vorkosigan — without a single shot fired

I. The Committee

After the Vorheil case — thirty-one to twenty-nine, the first countess in two hundred years — Helen Natalia Vorkosigan acquired a reputation that at twenty-six is more dangerous than a duel: the reputation of a person who finds documents.

So when the Council of Counts voted to digitize its historical archive — four hundred years of minutes, petitions, and receipts, kept in cellars whose climate plant was younger than the contents by barely a century — no one was surprised that she was named secretary of the committee. “Work for an archivist with patience and no enemies,” said the sponsor. The first part was true.

The committee assembled five: two old archive clerks who knew the cellars by smell; a professor of history from Vorbarr Sultana University, arriving with the happiness of a child in a confectioner’s; Lord Aden Vornel — jurist, archival enthusiast, a man of impeccable manners who brought everyone coffee at the first session and by the second already knew how each of them took it; and Helen, the youngest by twenty years, which Vornel noted with a light, elegant irony: “The secretary’s age compensates for the archive’s. On average we are ideal.”

He was a pleasant colleague. Helen would remember that afterward, at length and carefully: he was genuinely a pleasant colleague.

For three months the work went as work: scanning teams in the cellars, registers, checksums, dust. Helen introduced one single rule of her own, learned from the Vorheil case: every volume is counted three times — against the inventory of its own century, against the inventory of twenty years ago, and by hand, sheet by sheet, before the scanner. The rule doubled the schedule and the committee grumbled. The only one who supported her aloud was Vornel: “The secretary is right. We digitize once every four hundred years — let us not be in a hurry precisely today.” Helen was grateful to him. That, too, she would remember afterward.

On the hundredth day the rule caught the first discrepancy. Volume 311 of the minutes series: by the century’s inventory — forty-two documents; by the inventory of twenty years ago — forty-two; by hand — thirty-nine. Three missing items, and in the margin of the inventory sheet, in an archive clerk’s hand, stood the explanation Helen had met once before in her life and had learned to read as an alarm: inventory omission.

Gravediggers leave receipts, she had learned from the Vorheil case. And thieves of history leave inventory omissions.

She said nothing to the committee. She simply extended her own rule backward, volume by volume, and began to count.

II. The Missing Year

In three weeks of quiet counting Helen found eleven “inventory omissions” — eleven missing documents in an archive of four hundred years, which at first glance was a brilliant result.

At second glance it was a signature.

The eleven absences were not scattered. Every one of them touched a single year — fifty-nine years back: the year of Vordarian’s Pretendership and of the Restoration Session that followed it. And not the year in general — nothing was missing from the minutes of the war itself — but one single sitting: the one at which the Council, under the presidency of the young Regent, had judged its own. Charges of treason after a civil war are not voted in secret; that was the oldest rule of the chamber: he who takes away a name takes it away standing, before all. A vote by standing, by name, with a clerk recording who rose and who remained seated.

The minutes were missing. The evidentiary annex to one of the cases was missing. The page from the clerk’s daybook for that same afternoon was missing. And the remaining eight “omissions” were documents that cited the first three.

Someone was not erasing a year. Someone was erasing one act of standing.

Helen checked what she knew of the sitting itself from the common history every Barrayaran child learns in both its versions, the schoolroom one and the family one. The Restoration Session: three days after the end of the Pretendership, a chamber full of living, angry, frightened men, six charges of treason, six votes by standing. Five names taken away and never returned. One of them — Count Voreilan, a district in the north, accused of supplying grain to the usurper’s forces; stripped of his name by thirty-three votes to twenty-seven; dead in exile two years later. The county of Voreilan, left without a line, had been awarded by the Crown to a deserving loyalist.

The deserving loyalist’s name was Vornel.

Access to volume 311 and the associated series had been had, by the cellar registers, by five people in the last twenty years. Four were archive clerks with biographies like blank pages. The fifth was a member of her own committee — the jurist who supported the triple counting, the man who knew how everyone took their coffee: Lord Aden Vornel, great-grandson of the loyalist, and since last month put forward as Keeper of the Speaker’s Circle, a post the Council would vote on in the autumn and which is not given to the grandsons of doubtful sittings.

III. Footnotes

Helen did not go to the Speaker, because she had nothing but an arithmetic of absences. Instead she did what the Vorheil case had taught her: she went after the footnotes.

A document can be stolen. Its citations cannot. For four hundred years the archive had been citing itself, and every citation was an imprint of the vanished original.

The first trail waited in the tax cellars — one floor lower and a century dustier. A tax appeal from fifty-seven years ago, filed by the widow Voreilan against the confiscation: dismissed, of course, but filed by every rule, with citations. The widow — or her advocate, working evidently without fee and without hope — cited the minutes of the sitting by page, and from the breakdown it was clear that the evidentiary annex in the Voreilan case had occupied exactly four sheets: two witness depositions and two quartermaster’s receipts for “grain delivered voluntarily to the forces of the usurper.”

The second trail was in the university library, in the memoirs of a long-dead count — read by no one, because the author had written as he had lived: at length and unforgivingly. In a footnote on page six hundred two the old man expressed bafflement that the sitting had accepted receipts “without a single signature of a receiving quartermaster — a thing even Vordarian’s thieves would not have permitted themselves,” and added, his pen pressed nearly through the paper: “but the chamber was in a hurry, and a chamber in a hurry votes with its feet, not its head.”

The third trail was the driest and the most eloquent. The register of the clerks’ office — kept then and now with identical dispassion — preserved a request from the sitting’s own clerk, filed a week later: “that there be appended to the minutes the special note of the submitting signatory Lord Vornel concerning the provenance of the material evidence.” A request marked withdrawn by instruction. With no signature beneath the instruction. Instructions that fear signatures Helen knew from the Vorheil case: they were the most sincere documents in any archive.

Here was the picture, assembled from the margins of other people’s pages: an accusation resting on two unsigned receipts, submitted by the man who after the vote received the condemned man’s county. And a clerk who had tried to leave a note for whoever might one day read — and had been made to withdraw it.

The original remained. And here the footnotes pointed in a direction it took Helen two days to laugh at: the widow’s tax appeal noted that “a copy of the minutes is preserved in the working archive of the Regency.” The working archive of the Regent, Lord Aral Vorkosigan. The cellar of her own house.

She wrote to Sergyar that same evening. Her grandmother’s answer arrived by courier, composed of three sentences, and Helen recognized the style from family legend before she saw the signature:

Helen. The Regency copies are in the gray crates, row four; the year is inscribed in my handwriting, because your great-grandfather filed documents the way he made war — conclusively and without an index. I am coming on the first liner: at ninety-three I am the last footnote with a pulse and I insist on being cited in person.

IV. The Living Footnote

Cordelia Vorkosigan arrived from Sergyar eleven days later, climbed down into the cellar of Vorbarr Sultana House refusing a hand on the stairs, and found the gray crate in fourteen minutes.

“Row four,” she said, blowing dust off the lid with an irreverence only a person who has laid that dust personally can permit herself. “I arranged these crates during the first winter of the Regency, while Aral was learning to govern an empire and I was learning to govern Aral. The second was more document-intensive.”

The Regency copy of the minutes was complete, initialed by the clerk on every page — and on the last, in a small, tight hand, stood what the official copy had never received: the special note, withdrawn “by instruction,” recorded all the same by a clerk who had evidently been one of those people who leave receipts. The note certified that the two quartermaster’s receipts had been submitted by Lord Vornel “in uncertified transcript, without production of the original,” that Count Voreilan had requested a confrontation with the quartermaster — and that the request had sunk into procedure, because the chamber had been in a hurry: three days after the end of a civil war, chambers are always in a hurry.

Cordelia sat down on the edge of the crate with the minutes on her knees and read for a while in silence. Her hands, Helen noticed, did not tremble. Her hands never trembled; that too was a family legend, and among the truest.

“I remember the sitting,” she said. “Not from inside — the women waited in the galleries in those days, which it occurred to no one to find strange, myself included, which I now find the strangest thing of all. I remember Aral that evening. He sat up there, in the library, with six folders in front of him, and said: ‘Today we took away six names. Of five I am certain.’ I asked which one he wasn’t. He answered: ‘A Regent has no right to doubts without evidence. And the evidence, if it exists, is at the bottom of somebody’s safe and will outlive me.’” She closed the minutes, very carefully. “Well. It outlived him. But it did not outlive my cellar.”

“Vornel knew,” said Helen. “The great-grandson. That’s why he cleaned the archive before the vote for Keeper: not to hide treason — there was no treason. To hide how his great-grandfather won the county.”

“The most Barrayaran crime in the world,” said Cordelia. “The murder of a dead man, so that the portrait in the drawing room stays clean.” She rose, ninety-three years old, straight as a set of minutes. “What will you do now, granddaughter? You have a report that kills one career and resurrects one name. The order of those two acts is a question of character.”

“I know,” said Helen. “That’s why I’ll take a copy to Vornel first.”

Cordelia looked at her a long moment. Then she nodded once, slowly, with that expression which in this family was handed down instead of a decoration.

“The Betan half of your blood thought of it,” she said. “But the Barrayaran half will carry it out. Go. I’ll wait here, with the crates. At my age one prefers the company of documents: they at least do not lie twice in the same way.”

V. The Copy

Lord Aden Vornel received her in his office in the Council building, between two sittings, with his usual courtesy and with coffee made exactly as she took it.

Helen laid a folder on his desk: the committee’s report, complete, with all annexes — the tax appeal, the memoir footnote, the clerks’ office register, the Regency copy certified by the last living witness of the era. On top, without comment, she placed a printout of the eleven “inventory omissions” together with the access registers.

Vornel read for forty minutes. Helen sat across from him and did not say one word, because she was her father’s daughter and knew when silence does the work better than any speech. She watched his hands turn the pages — more and more slowly — watched them stop altogether at the page with the clerk’s special note, and watched the man opposite her age forty years in forty minutes, which was just, because that was precisely how many years he had been carrying what now lay on the desk.

“I have known since I was thirty-one,” he said at last, without raising his eyes. “My grandfather told me on his deathbed, because the dying confuse confession with a gift. Since then I have kept a portrait in a drawing room I dare not look at, and a county whose birth certificate is a forgery.” He closed the folder. “I cleaned the archive, yes. Not for the post. The post was the occasion. I cleaned it because a document that exists is one day read, and I did not want to be alive on that day.” At last he looked at her. “Why do you bring it to me first, Lady Helen? Your report is complete. You could have entered it directly.”

“Because the Voreilan case was decided by a chamber in a hurry,” said Helen. “I don’t intend to correct haste with haste. You have a week before I submit it. What you do with it is your question.”

Vornel was silent. Then he stood, went to the window, looked out at the square where fifty-nine years ago half a capital had burned, and said, more to the glass than to her:

“Do you know what is heaviest, Lady Helen? That my great-grandfather was not a monster. He was a frightened man in a frightened time; he saw a county belonging to no one and lied once — once! — and then three generations of us maintained the lie, because there was no going back. Lies do not grow, as people imagine. Lies are inherited.” He turned. “I shall not need a week. I shall need three days.”

VI. A Vote by Standing

Lord Aden Vornel did not wait for the report to be submitted. On the third day he withdrew his candidacy for Keeper with a short letter, resigned from the committee, and did the one thing that could still surprise Helen: he appeared at the autumn session in person, asked for the floor first, and told the chamber, white with silence, that he was returning to the Crown the comital coronet of his line “pending determination on the merits,” because — and this sentence was quoted for years afterward — “the portrait in my drawing room weighs more than it is worth.”

The committee’s report — “concerning eleven inventory discrepancies and the restoration of volume 311” — was submitted that same forenoon, with the Regency copy appended, certified by the last living witness of the era, and with a handwriting analysis no one asked to contest.

The restoration of the name Voreilan was submitted in the afternoon. By the old order: he who returns a name returns it standing.

Helen Natalia Vorkosigan stood in the gallery, in the place where fifty-nine years ago the women had stood, and beside her, having refused an offered chair with a single word, stood Cordelia. Below, in the old chamber, sixty comital seats decided the fate of a dead man: fifty-nine men rose one by one, by name, under the clerk’s voice — and one seat, that of the line of Vornel, pending determination, remained empty, and its emptiness was counted aloud, because the chamber had finally learned that absences are documents too.

“Fifty-nine standing and one empty,” said Cordelia quietly, when the clerk announced the result. “Fifty-nine years ago I sat up here and counted who rose. Today I count again.” She paused. “The difference is that today the sum comes out.”

VII. Footnote

That evening, after the session, Helen entered the final correction in the digital archive: volume 311, restored, complete, with the Regency copy as annex A and with the clerk’s special note — at last — in the place that had been owed it for fifty-nine years.

Then, in the margin of the last page, she added a footnote — the first authorial note in the four-hundred-year archive, accepted by the Speaker without objection:

“Restored from eleven citations, one tax appeal, one withdrawn clerk’s note, and one witness with a pulse. Recorded for whoever may one day read: write your footnotes. Someone reads them.”

Cordelia flew for Sergyar the next morning. At the parting, on the dock, she embraced Helen, drew back to arm’s length, and studied her with that Betan interest which in this family passed for the highest tenderness.

“Your great-grandfather built the Imperium with fleets,” she said. “Your father holds it together with audits. And you, it seems, will repair it with footnotes. Each generation goes one level deeper into the text.” She smiled. “Keep it up. At the bottom of every text, granddaughter, there is only one thing: the names. Guard them.”

“By name,” said Helen.

“By name,” said Cordelia, and boarded the shuttle, upright, without turning — a woman of ninety-three, cited in person, with an account settled at fifty-nine years.

Dead Dwarf

An adventure of Imperial pilot Nikolai Vorsoisson — and of one very old chase

I. The Routine Object

The order arrived in the dullest formulation the Imperial cartographic service possessed: “precision survey of the Kyros system in connection with reclassification of the commercial traffic corridor, term thirty days, vessel — courier Echo, pilot — N. Vorsoisson.”

Nikki read it twice and smiled, which a cartographic order achieves once in a career.

Kyros. Every jump pilot in the Empire knew Kyros the way every Barrayaran cadet knows Hegen: a dead red dwarf two jumps from the Illyrican nexus, three bare rocks, zero colonies, zero traffic — and one ambush that had gone into the textbooks. Seven years ago, here, at the exit point of the wormhole, Admiral Elli Quinn had waited twelve hours for a stolen ship, because the only place where even the fastest hull in the galaxy is blind, deaf, and slow is the first few seconds after a jump. The thief had come out of the wormhole with an overheated implant — straight into the net. The chase had ended here, among the dead rocks, and ever since, on pilots’ courses, Kyros had been a synonym for the lesson the exit point is defensible.

Nikki had been taught that lesson on the senior jump pilots’ course, a year after the event, and he remembered how the instructor — an old jump pilot with an implant, gray at the temples — had tapped the schematic and said: “Remember this system, ladies and gentlemen. Here the fastest ship in known space discovered that speed is not a property of the ship. Speed is a property of position.”

And the ship the thief had been flying — hull forty-eight, Sail class, double phase Necklin sail, the fastest crewed thing in known space — now stood at dock with its hatches open, waiting for Nikki to load the survey drones.

Nikki Vorsoisson had been flying Echo for seven years and did not believe in fate, but did believe in symmetry: the ship was returning to the site of its own legend, with a cartographer at the console. Chases leave trails. Cartographers read them.

The survey crew was as established: himself and technician Dasa — a woman of fifty-two with a watchmaker’s hands and a dockhand’s vocabulary, who had calibrated drones for eleven expeditions and maintained that on the twelfth she would stop counting. Dasa looked over the hold, looked over the list, looked over Nikki, and said:

“Thirty days in a system with three rocks and one burnt-out sun. Do you know the good thing about dead systems, pilot? Nobody breathes down your neck. Do you know the bad thing? Exactly the same.”

“Thirty days, Dasa,” said Nikki to the console, out of a habit he had given up hiding. “We’ll measure the graveyard of your legend down to the last pebble.”

II. The Figure Eights

Measurement is a craft of patience, and Nikki loved it with a love few understood and he had long since stopped explaining.

Echo wove slow figure eights through the system — the fastest hull in the Empire moving at the pace of a grazing animal — while the drones filled in the gravimetric grid node by node, and the days went by in a rhythm that looked monotonous from outside and was full from within: morning check, circuit, calibration, evening check, log. Dasa cursed drone number four with a tenderness she reserved for only the most troublesome mechanisms. Nikki did what cartography actually exists for and what never appears in the orders: he looked.

Fifteen — now seventeen — years of service had taught him that the map departs from the territory always at the same place: where one’s predecessor was in a hurry. And in Kyros everyone had been in a hurry. The commercial shipwrights had hurried to reclassify the corridor. The old measurements had been taken “in transit,” by ships on their way elsewhere. Even the legendary ambush — the most thoroughly documented event in the system’s history — had been an eighty-hour episode viewed through gunsights.

On the tenth day Nikki wrote home, as he did every week, briefly:

Mama. I am measuring a system in which there is nothing, and this is more interesting than it sounds: nothing is the rarest object in the galaxy and no one has ever measured it properly. The ship is well. It is quiet here in a way you would like — a silence with good soil. Tell your second gardener by the rose bed that his plot is visible from orbit. I am joking. Almost. N.

Ekaterin’s answer came six days later, equally brief: Nothing, measured properly, is called a place. Measure calmly. Mama.

On the nineteenth day, in the array of the outer debris, the gravimetry snagged an object with the wrong passport.

III. The Graveyard

Kyros up close was exactly as dead as the catalogues promised: an old, miserly sun the color of charcoal, three rocks in the inner orbits, and a thin graveyard of debris in the outer ones — the remains of a protoplanetary disc that had given up halfway through the job four billion years ago.

The object with the wrong passport hung deep in the debris belt and was wrong on three counts. Albedo — too low for ice, too even for rock. Mass — forty-one kilograms, at a size that for stone would have given three times as much. And orbit — a long, clean ellipse, imperturbably stable, of the sort no fragment of a disc assumes on its own: someone had calculated it.

“Artificial,” said Dasa, bent over the spectrum. “And painted. Look at the reflectance: somebody paid for it to resemble its neighbors. This isn’t rubbish, pilot. Rubbish doesn’t camouflage itself.”

Nikki brought Echo to within three hundred meters and looked at the screen a long time without saying anything to the console, because the things one says at such moments go into the log.

The object was a sensor drone. Passive, no emissions, its battery dead for years, its hull painted in the spectrum of the local rocks — work that was not merely expensive but bespoke. The manufacturer’s markings had been erased, which was itself a marking: there was one place in the galaxy where erasing serial numbers was an industry.

Jacksonian work. Deployed — Nikki ran the orbit backward through the machine and obtained the date to within a week — seven years ago. Days before an ambush.

Someone had come to Kyros before Quinn. Had left a silent witness in the front row. And had never come back to collect it.

“Do we take it in?” Dasa asked.

“We take it in,” said Nikki. “By material-evidence protocol. Gloves, seals, continuous record.” He paused. “And, Dasa… from now on we measure everything in this system twice.”

IV. The Witness

Extracting the memory took four days and half of Dasa’s vocabulary.

The battery had been dead a long while, and by the rules of the trade that ought not to have been a problem: the solid-state memory of passive observers is built to outlive everything, including its owner — it waits only for current and patience. But the hull had spent seven years in a debris belt, and the current had to enter through contacts gnawed by micrometeorites, and the patience through three layers of protection designed by people who had wanted no one else to read the record.

“It’s encrypted like an embassy,” Dasa reported on the second day, with the respect she reserved for a worthy opponent. “But it’s encrypted for collection, not for eternity: the key is in the drone itself, waiting for a recognition signal from its owner. The owner never came. So the key is still sitting there. All I have to do is convince the drone that I’ve come home.”

“Can you?”

“Pilot,” said Dasa, “I have spent eleven expeditions convincing drones that I love them. Give me two more days.”

On the fourth day, at 2210 ship’s time, the screen in Echo’s cramped laboratory lit and a record began to run: seventy-nine hours of dead dwarf, dead rocks, dead time — and eleven minutes for the sake of which all the rest existed.

V. The Eleven Minutes

The eleven minutes Nikki watched fourteen times. Then once more with Dasa, frame by frame. Then once more alone, in the middle of the night, because there are records a man must watch without witnesses in order to check his own eyes.

Here: the wormhole’s exit point blooms in the familiar five-space grimace and hull forty-eight comes out of the jump — his ship, seven years younger, with its serial numbers imperfectly erased and a thief with an overheated implant at the console. Here: Quinn’s net closes, clean, by the textbook, the fleet’s vessels light their beacons, and the chase ends in one hundred forty seconds, exactly as the reports said, word for word. Nikki had read those reports on the pilots’ course. Now he was watching them — from a vantage no one had watched them from.

And here is what appeared in no report at all.

Forty light-minutes out, in the debris, on the record of the passive witness, sat a ship. Dark, cold, engines at zero — invisible to everyone at the exit point, busy with their ambush. And in the seconds when hull forty-eight was emerging from the wormhole with its sail at full thrust — in the only seconds in the universe when the double phase sail of a Sail-class ship works at its limit before anyone’s eyes — the dark ship touched it once with a narrow-beam active scanner: a single pulse, two point one seconds long, calibrated not for hull, not for cargo, not for crew.

For field geometry. For the shape of the Necklin field itself under full load.

Then the ship waited out the ambush, waited for the fleet to depart with its prize and its thief, discreetly gathered distance — and jumped. The drone stayed behind, waiting for a collection signal that never came, because professionals do not return to a crime scene that has already paid them.

Nikki sat a long while before the frozen final frame. The buyer who does not exist, he thought. That was the name of the chapter in the legend: a buyer with a false name who had paid the penalties without blinking, who had ordered a handover at a dead dwarf — and who had evaporated when the net snapped shut. For seven years everyone had read that chapter the same way: the buyer took fright and did not come.

The buyer had come. The buyer had left with his purchase. He had never wanted the ship — the ship was too large, too hot, too stolen. He had wanted two point one seconds of truth about a sail. And the theft, the chase, the whole celebrated hunt had been merely the way to make the fastest ship in the galaxy pose for him: at full thrust, on a predictable course, directly before the lens.

“Pilot,” said Dasa quietly, over his shoulder, “do you know what we’re looking at?”

“We’re looking,” said Nikki, “at the most expensive photograph in the history of shipbuilding.”

VI. Whose Trail

The letters to Professor Riva and back took nine days each way, and the correspondence that developed over the following month Nikki kept for the rest of his life.

Her first letter consisted of one sentence: “Send me the raw pulse, not your conclusions — at ninety-three I prefer to make my own mistakes.”

The second was technical and ended with a paragraph Dasa printed out and stuck on the laboratory wall: “Commend your technician for the extraction. A drone that has waited seven years for an owner is like a dog on a grave — it opens only to someone who speaks to it with patience. It is evident that your person spoke with patience.”

The third was longer: “The pulse is what you suspect: a lifting of field geometry, competently executed. Whoever commissioned it knew that the documentation of a sail is half a sail — the real merchandise is the behavior of the field under load, and that no blueprint gives you: it is raised over years of adjustment, like a voice.” And then, in the handwriting her comconsole reproduced from dictation: “Check the Vervani yard Tanel. Two years ago they announced a two-masted courier of ‘proprietary design.’ They published their field spectra at a conference, out of vanity. Send me those as well.”

The fourth letter was Nikki’s and contained Tanel’s spectra, taken from the conference’s open proceedings, and one question he was a long time formulating: how does one prove that two fields are the same field, rather than two solutions to one problem?

The fifth was the shortest, and Nikki reread it standing:

“I compared them. Agreement to the seventh decimal, including in the noise. My boy, independent discoveries resemble each other in their ideas. In their noise, only thefts resemble each other.”

VII. The Map Remembers

Colonel Elin Venek heard out Nikki’s report in an office on Solstice without interrupting once — she had built a career on not interrupting — then ran the drone’s record to the eleven minutes, watched them twice, and said:

“For seven years this case has sat in my boot like a pebble. Accounting caught the false buyer in three days, the fleet caught the thief in three seconds — and the principal had no face, no motive, and no trail, and we all agreed to believe he had taken fright.” She tapped the screen. “And he turns out to be the only one in the whole story who finished his job. Myself included.”

“Everyone included,” said Nikki. “A chase is fast work, Colonel. Quinn had twelve hours and caught what one catches in twelve hours. I had thirty days and a gravimetric grid. Speed wins chases. Slowness wins truths.”

“Tell that to the industrial arbitration tribunal,” said Venek. “They will require a witness who can sound like a proverb.”

The rest was done by lawyers and diplomats, slowly and expensively, as they do: the suit of the Illyrican shipwrights against Tanel, supported by the record of a passive witness, by an expert opinion signed Riva, and by agreement to the seventh decimal in the noise, dragged through three instances and ended with the revocation of licenses, with penalties that made an entire sector flinch, and with a proverb that entered arbitration practice under the name the Kyros principle: independent developments are proved by their noise.

Dasa received a commendation in orders and a leave which, by her own account, she intended to spend “somewhere with far too many people, for a change.” At the parting on the dock she shook Nikki’s hand and said:

“Twelfth expedition, pilot. I said that on the twelfth I’d stop counting.” A pause. “I lied. For the likes of us, counting is the engine.”

And Nikki Vorsoisson finished the survey on schedule, because a cartographic order takes no interest in legends, and on the final working day entered into the Imperial chart of the Kyros system one additional position: a long, clean ellipse with an object of forty-one kilograms, status “material evidence, recovered,” coordinates to the last decimal.

Maps, an old instructor had once told him, do not remember who was in a hurry. Nikki now knew it was the opposite: maps remember precisely that. The trail of the fast one is left for the slow one — which is why you fly in another’s wake only until you learn to read your own.

He closed the chart, patted the console of the ship that seven years ago had posed here, unknowing, for an invisible lens, and laid in a course for home.

“Come on, boy,” said Nikki. “We’ve solved your legend. As of today you’re the fastest piece of material evidence in the galaxy.”

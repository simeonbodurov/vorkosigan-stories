The Garden of Drafts

A Cetagandan story — of one family on Rho Ceta and the neighbors one does not look at

I. Tala’s Catalogue

The Veris family lived in the third house on the irrigation row, in the orchard district of Lanna on Rho Ceta, and kept its distance from the great house behind the hedge, because that is how sensible people behave.

The father maintained the district’s canals — quiet work, important and invisible, exactly his measure. The mother kept the accounts of three orchard cooperatives. And their children were two: Yun, eight years old, who wanted to be the pilot of anything that flew, and Tala, twelve, who did not want to become anything, because she already was something — a cataloguer.

Tala’s catalogue ran to forty pages and contained, at the opening of this story, thirty-two night moths of Lanna district: each with a drawing, with measurements, with hours of flight, and with a name Tala gave it herself, because the official names were numbers, and a number, she said, is not a name. Her father regarded the catalogue with the quiet pride of a man who has counted cubic meters of water all his life and has raised a child who counts beauty.

“Only one rule,” he said, every spring, when the night hunts began. “Hunt wherever you like, except toward the great house. One does not look at the great house.”

The great house belonged to the retired ghem-General Taven — hedgerows, terraces, an old park, and a gate that did not open. The general appeared in the village twice a year, on the holidays, with his clan’s face paint impeccably applied, and people bowed to him exactly as much as was required and not one glance more. Of his wife only one thing was known, and it was spoken in whispers: that she was haut-born. A true haut, from the Celestial Garden, given to the general forty years ago for services one does not ask about. A haut who has come down among the ghem is haut no longer — but the eyes, the old people said, remain. That is why one did not look at the great house.

Tala observed the rule for eleven years and four months. Then, one evening at the end of the blossoming, moth number thirty-three landed on her net.

The moth had a wingspan the width of both her palms, gray-blue, soundless — and across its wings, when it spread them, ran glowing signs. Not markings. Signs. Ordered, repeating, like a letter in a language Tala did not know, written in firefly light upon a living wing. And when the moth took off, it did not flutter wherever its eyes led, like any respectable moth, but set off in a straight line, confidently, like a courier with an errand — across the irrigation row, over the canal, straight toward the hedge of the great house.

Tala stood a long time in the dark with a pencil in her hand and a catalogue that had no column for impossible things.

Then she did what cataloguers have done since the beginning of the world: she went after the specimen.

II. Beyond the Hedge

She took Yun too, because Yun had seen her going out and the price of his silence was complicity.

The hedge had a gap by the old elm — every child in the district knew it and none had ever used it — and beyond the gap was a park, and beyond the park, behind a second fence of living bamboo, was a garden such as existed nowhere.

The beds glowed. Not all of them and not brightly — a stalk here, a shrub there, a soft greenish light without a single lamp. Over the central lawn wove moths like number thirty-three — dozens, and the signs across their wings ran in time, as though they were all reading one page. A vine along the wall opened and closed its flowers at even intervals, patiently, like a metronome. In a shallow pond swam fish that at every turn changed color by a half-tone, up and down some scale of their own. And at the far end, under an awning, stood worktables, glass vessels, rows of pots with numbered labels — and this was the moment when Yun whispered “a laboratory” with delight and Tala whispered “we’re running” with all her common sense.

Common sense was ten seconds late.

“Stay on the path,” said a voice behind them. “The beds don’t like being trodden on.”

Ghem-General Taven stood three paces away, without paint, in a gardening shirt, with pruning shears in his hand — and that was somehow more frightening than any paint, because a face without paint among the ghem means you are not a guest. Yun froze. Tala, the catalogue pressed to her chest like a shield, did the only thing that occurred to her: bowed exactly as much as was required and said in a voice that trembled only at the end:

“Forgive me, sir. I was following a specimen.”

The general looked at her for a long moment. Then his gaze fell on the catalogue, on the open page with the drawing of number thirty-three — the measurements, the flight time, the track sketched in dashes across the canal — and something moved in his face that in a ghem-general might mean anything at all.

“You were following a specimen,” he repeated. “With measurements.” He turned toward the house and raised his voice only as much as was necessary. “Naina. Come and see something. We have… colleagues.”

III. Tea with a Haut

Lady Naina ghem Taven received them on the terrace, with tea that smelled of rain, and for the whole of the first hour Tala could not decide where to look, because one does not look at a haut, and not looking at Lady Naina was physically impossible. She was old — she had to be old, forty years on Rho Ceta — but the age upon her was as ordered as everything else: not beauty taken away, but beauty completed.

She turned the pages of Tala’s catalogue one by one, unhurried, and on the thirty-third page she stopped.

“You have named her,” she said. Her voice was quiet and very precise. “In my registers she is draft eleven-nine: eleventh line, ninth attempt. And with you she is—” she read the name Tala had written beneath the drawing “—Letter-Carrier.” Silence. “For forty years no one had given them names. Only numbers. Myself included.”

Then, because the children had come too far to be sent back with only tea, she told them — as much as one tells children, that is, the truth without the numbers that frighten.

In the Celestial Garden, in the Star Crèche, she had been of the gardeners’ circles: not of those who work upon the human thread — few are admitted to that — but of the artists of the living, who make moths with poetry on their wings and vines that measure time, for the Emperor’s festivals. Every line has drafts: attempts, unfinished, imperfect. And the rule of the Crèche is simple and old: when a line closes, the drafts are collected. Collected, said Lady Naina, is the word that is used. The other word is shorter.

“When I was given to the general,” she said, without bitterness, as one gives a date, “my lines were closing. All of them. I asked for one single marriage right: to take my garden. Not the perfect specimens — those remained with the Emperor. The drafts. No one objected, because no one writes ‘drafts’ in a ceremonial inventory — they wrote ‘garden gift, one hundred eleven pots.’” She looked out at the lawn, where the Letter-Carrier and her sisters were reading their glowing page. “One hundred eleven drafts, children. Forty years. Everything here is imperfect and everything here is alive, and by the laws of the Crèche the second does not excuse the first.”

“So it’s—” Yun began.

“Illegal,” said General Taven calmly, pouring tea. “For forty years now. Drink, while it’s hot.”

IV. Lessons in the Garden

The sensible thing after such an afternoon would have been never to go back. Tala lasted six days.

On the seventh Lady Naina was waiting for her at the gap in the hedge — not at the gate: at the gap, which was itself a whole sentence — with gardening gloves in her hand, child’s size.

“A cataloguer without access to the collection is half a cataloguer,” she said. “Come on odd-numbered days, after school. You will come in this way: the gate is watched from the road, but nobody watches the elm except us. Your brother may come on the even days — the general needs a listener, and I need a rest from military history.”

So began the lessons the Veris parents did not know about, which weighed on Tala every evening and did not weigh on her a single afternoon.

Lady Naina taught her the discipline of a collection: how to keep a register that will outlive its keeper; how to describe a living creature without killing it in the description; why every draft is inspected by hand once in ten days (“the machine measures indices; the hand measures mood”). Tala transferred the names from her own catalogue into the great register, one by one, and Lady Naina entered them without comment — and then, Tala noticed, began to use them.

And on the even days Yun sat with the general under the awning and received his own education: orbital maneuvers drawn with a stick in the sand of the path; the history of three campaigns told from the quartermaster’s side (“war, boy, is deliveries with fireworks”); and once, only once, an answer to the question Yun asked with all the effrontery of eight years old — what the general had done to be given a haut for a wife.

General Taven was silent for a long while. Then he said:

“I saved something the Empire could not afford to lose, in a manner for which it could not afford to reward me publicly. So they rewarded me in a way that would keep silent along with me.” He raked the sand smooth with the stick. “They thought they were giving me an ornament, boy. They gave me a gardener. To this day the Empire does not know who saved whom in this house.”

In the evenings, going back through the gap, Tala sometimes stopped and looked back at the soft light above the beds. She was twelve and a half and already understood enough to know that she was carrying a secret with an adult’s weight. She did not yet understand — that would come a month later, with one official envelope — that the secret was carrying her as well.

V. The Census

The rule “one does not look at the great house” Tala now understood entirely. She also came to understand the second rule, the unwritten one that holds for all secret gardens: that the most dangerous thing in them is not inspectors, but guests with good intentions.

Because three months earlier, before the gap in the hedge, before the terrace and the tea, Tala had done something entirely innocent: she had sent a photograph of an unknown night moth — distant, blurred, taken above the canal — to the district’s school natural-science competition. And the photograph had won first place, and had gone into the provincial almanac, and the almanac had gone where all almanacs go in an empire which, after the Harvest affair and after one scandalous trial in the capital, had begun, quietly and methodically, a census of everything living that lacked a license.

The inquiry arrived at the Veris house under the seal of the new provincial administrator: “concerning a published biological object of undetermined registry origin — the location of the observation to be specified.”

That evening the kitchen was the quietest in the family’s history.

The father read the inquiry four times. He was a man who for thirty years had maintained canals in such a way that no superior ever learned his name, because on Rho Ceta the names of small people are learned only on bad days — and now he held a bad day with an official seal in his hands. The mother did arithmetic in her head — Tala could see her doing it — of what an answer in one direction cost a family from the irrigation row, and what an answer in the other cost. A precise woman, an accountant: she was also calculating the third thing no one said aloud — what silence costs, if it comes out later.

“We’ll write the truth,” the father said at last, in a flat voice. “Photographed above the canal, direction undetermined. That’s the truth. Nearly.”

“It isn’t,” said Tala.

And since she was already too far gone to be sent back, she told them everything: the moth, the gap, the garden, the tea, the lessons, the names in someone else’s register. Her parents listened with faces that passed through fear, through greater fear, and finally — Tala had not expected this — through something like grief: the grief of people whose child has stepped across into a world they had guarded her from all her life, and has brought back from it not disaster but a friendship, which is more complicated than disaster.

“I gave them away,” Tala finished. “I’ll warn them.”

“No,” said the father, and stood. For the first time in her life his voice filled the entire kitchen. “You will go to bed. The head of the family gives the warning. That is the order of things.” He took the inquiry from the table, folded it, and stowed it in his inside pocket, next to the heart, where dangerous documents are carried. “And the order of things, daughter, is the one thing small people have more of than great ones. We keep to it even when it is frightening. Especially when it is frightening.”

And Tala’s father, irrigation technician Veris, who for thirty years had not glanced toward the great house, went and knocked at its gate in the middle of the night.

VI. The Letter

What the two men said to each other that night Tala learned later, in pieces, and from both sides, and the two versions agreed in everything except one: each maintained that the other had conducted himself more honorably.

General Taven heard the father out standing, to the end, without inviting him to sit — not from arrogance, but because the father had refused: “I am here on duty, General. Family duty.” Then the general did something that in neither version sounded plausible and was for that very reason true: he bowed. To an irrigation technician. With the bow a ghem gives an equal in honor.

“Your daughter,” he said, “has kept another’s secret with an adult’s weight for three months and betrayed it only to those to whom she owed it: to you. Your house has raised a person who knows what she owes to whom. In my trade that is called impeccable service, and in forty years I have not met a great deal of it.” And he added: “Tomorrow this ceases to be your concern. Forty years I have waited for this census. I knew it would come: empires always finish reading their lists. My plan has been the same from the beginning and lacked only one thing. A witness with measurements. Now I have that too.”

In the morning General Taven, in full face paint applied as if for a parade, sat down and wrote a letter to the capital — to an old colleague with whom, many years ago, he had shared one very long night, and with whom he had shared ever since the right to write directly. Pensioners with face paint, he explained to Yun as he sealed the envelope, still retain the right to one letter a year that gets read.

“Who’s it to?” Yun asked.

“To a man,” said the general, “who after a certain affair reads everything about illegal organisms personally. And who, unlike administrators, knows the difference between a weapon and a garden.”

And to the Veris family, that same day, was handed a reply for the administrator, composed by the general and signed by the father, accurate to the last letter: the biological object was observed above the canal; its origin is the Taven estate; all further questions to the owner of the estate, who assumes full responsibility.

“The truth, the whole truth, and the address of the truth,” said the general. “The best protection for your family is my name on the problem. It has been on it all along in any case.”

VII. The Archive

The woman who came down from the shuttle three weeks later arrived without escort, without arms, and without notice, and nevertheless all of Lanna knew within the hour, because such eyes do not walk about incognito: white clothing, a gait that hurried nowhere, and an age discernible by nothing except the weight of the gaze.

Haut Pel Navar walked the garden of drafts for two hours, alone, with Lady Naina two paces behind her — the two of them speaking a language of pauses that Tala did not so much hear as watch — and at last stopped by the pond with the fish singing their scale in half-tones.

“The eleventh line,” she said. “I remember its closure. I voted for it.” A pause. “The regulation says that a draft is an unfinished work and that the unfinished is collected. Lately, however, the Crèche has twice established that a work left alone long enough completes itself. It creates precedents for us. It is becoming a habit with them.” She turned and looked not at the general, not at Lady Naina, but at Tala, standing beside her parents at the edge of the lawn. “Are you the cataloguer?”

Tala bowed exactly as much as was required.

“Lady Naina knows their numbers, haut. I know their names.”

“That is exactly how the work divides in an archive,” said haut Pel Navar.

The decision that left for the Celestial Garden that same evening was short. The garden in Lanna district was declared an external archive of the Star Crèche — “a living repository of closed decorative lines” — with Lady Naina ghem Taven as keeper, for life, and with an assistant cataloguer appointed on the recommendation of the consort herself: Tala Veris, twelve years old, probationary until her majority, and with the first official salary in the history of the irrigation row, payable in seed.

There was also an addendum, unrecorded in the decision, pronounced on the terrace at the leave-taking, when haut Pel accepted a cup of tea — which, Lady Naina would explain afterward, had not happened on any terrace outside the Celestial Garden in eleven years: the consort asked Tala’s father what he did, heard out the answer about the canals, nodded, and said:

“Irrigation. So you too keep alive things that others designed, and answer for it when they dry out.” A pause, precisely measured. “A widespread profession in this empire. More widespread than is admitted.”

And the district’s rule changed by only one word, which is the most any rule can ask. Once a year, on the first evening of the blossoming, the gate of the great house opened and the children of Lanna walked the paths between the glowing beds — to watch one hundred eleven drafts, imperfect and alive, read their page in the dark.

One looked at the great house now. Only with one’s head up.

The Faces of Vashnoi

A diplomatic adventure of the Emperor and his Auditor

I. The Letter from the Centenarian

The letter arrived at Vorbarr Sultana House by the oldest channel available: on paper, with a wax seal, delivered in person by Consul Lord Ivan Xav Vorpatril, who held it between two fingers, the way one holds something that may be either an invitation or an explosive device, and who in his experience with that family found the difference cosmetic.

“From Tej’s grandmother,” said Ivan. “Moira ghem Estif. She asked me to hand it to you ‘by hand, no chancery, no log.’ At a hundred and twenty-six one is entitled to whims. This one doesn’t smell like a whim to me.”

The letter was short and its calligraphy was a century old — a school no longer taught even on Eta Ceta.

Count Vorkosigan. I am the last living person to have seen Vashnoi with roofs on it. I lived on Barrayar eleven years as a young ghem-wife, three of them in the consulate five streets from the market square you reopened last spring. I have read about the beds with the names. The Empire I left documented everything it touched: censuses, cadastres, facial registries. The archive of Vashnoi district was not burned — what is burned is not archived, and Vashnoi was archived down to the last child, eight months before the bombs, at the satrapy’s scheduled review. You have the names. We have the faces. An old girl believes it is time the two halves met — and knows who in the Celestial Garden believes the same. If you answer, answer me. The official channels will kill this child in its cradle.

Miles read the letter three times. Then he laid it on the table beside his cup and said to Ekaterin, who was watching him over her own tea with the patience of a woman who had counted such silences for thirty years:

“The faces. We have a grave, we have a garden, we have names read aloud. And they are sitting on a hundred and five years of photographs. Every face from every bed.” He looked up. “And somebody there knows it, and wants to give them back, and that somebody isn’t an archivist, because archivists send copies, and between these lines it says in person.”

“Between the lines,” said Ekaterin, who had once stood in a Cetagandan conservatory facing those very lines, “it says Pel.”

II. The Impossible Invitation

Emperor Gregor Vorbarra heard out the report in his private study, standing at the window, and was silent afterward exactly as long as Miles had expected: a long time.

“Give me the political arithmetic,” he said at last. “Aloud. I want to hear it from you before I have said it myself.”

“A haut on Barrayaran soil for the first time since the withdrawal,” said Miles. “Not a diplomat, not a trade attaché of ghem caliber: a haut, and from the Star Crèche at that. Carrying the occupation archives of the city the occupation killed. Half the Council will see penitence and demand that it kneel. The other half will see an intelligence operation. The veterans’ societies will see an insult wrapped as a gift. And Cetaganda, if the invitation is official, will simply not permit it — haut do not travel to barbarian memorials; that would concede there is something to travel for.” He drew breath. “You cannot invite. She cannot ask. No one can apologize, because empires have no mouths, only archives. The arithmetic, Sire, comes to zero.”

“The arithmetic always comes to zero,” said Gregor. “Which is why I keep an Auditor who counts differently.” He turned from the window. “Tell me, Miles. I can see you found it on the shuttle.”

“Vashnoi does not belong to the Imperium,” said Miles. “Vashnoi is mine. Personally. A grant made to a three-year-old child, executed by every rule, with a bitter joke in place of a motive. For fifty-seven years it has been the most useless property on Barrayar, which is precisely why no one has ever troubled to nationalize it.” He spread his hands. “A private estate, Sire. Count Vorkosigan invites a private person to visit his private property — an elderly lady, an old family connection through the Vorpatril in-laws, with a companion. No state visit. No protocol. No Empire — neither one of them.”

“A companion,” Gregor repeated, with an expression in which thirty-six years of self-command restrained something suspiciously close to a smile. “Haut Pel Navar, consort of the Star Crèche, as companion to a grandmother of a hundred and twenty-six.”

“Elderly ladies do not travel alone, Sire. This is an acknowledged fact throughout the galaxy.”

“And I?” Gregor asked quietly. “Because I am not going to miss this, Miles. Not this. But an Emperor on the lanes of Vashnoi turns the private into the official by the mere act of breathing.”

“An Emperor — yes,” said Miles. “But as I recall, there is in the ImpSec files an inspector of grain deliveries by the name of Greg Bleakman, with an impeccable record and a superior who owes him years of unused leave.”

III. A Protocol for a Visit That Is Not Happening

The protocol for a visit that does not happen proved to be the most complex document ever written in Vorbarr Sultana House, and on the third day Miles surrendered and sent for a specialist.

Lady Alys Vorpatril Illyan arrived at ninety-four, straight as a protocol — which had also been the meaning of her life — heard out the task, was silent for forty seconds, and delivered the diagnosis with a relish she had not felt in a decade:

“So you want from me a ceremonial for an event which is officially a tea party, factually an act of state, and emotionally a funeral one hundred and five years late. Involving a sovereign who is not present, an apology that is not spoken, and a guest empire that does not exist.” She set down her cup. “Miles, my dear, this is the finest present you have ever given me. Give me a fortnight and Simon for a courier.”

Alys’s protocol ran to a bare four pages and was a masterpiece of negative space: who does not stand where, who does not bow to whom, which cameras are not present, and which word — regret — is spoken by no one, because once spoken it would demand an answer, and answers were the business of empires, not of guests.

In parallel ran the other channel, the invisible one. The chief of ImpSec, General Duv Galeni — a man whose own biography ran through the Solstice Massacre and who for that reason understood the matter better than any other living Barrayaran — held with Ghem-General Dag Benin a series of consultations, entered in both archives as “technical exchange on quarantine standards.” The two services, whose predecessors had killed each other for three generations, agreed routes, frequencies, and lists with the patience of old chess players, and at the end of the last session Benin, by the testimony of the minutes that were not kept, had said: “General, if this goes well, no one will ever know how much work it was. I propose a toast to our profession.” And Galeni had replied: “To the profession. And to archives that this time give back instead of taking.”

One thing remained: the Council of Counts, which must not learn beforehand and must not be lied to. The formula was found by Gregor, with that reflex Miles had for forty years called governing by footnote: in the court’s weekly register, between the repair of a roof and a prize awarded to a choir, a line appeared — “Count Vorkosigan receives private guests at his estate of Vashnoi; the Crown is informed.” Informed. Not “approved,” not “authorized.” Anyone who wished to ask could ask. No one realized there was anything to ask until it was too late for asking.

IV. The Guest

They landed at first light, on the field pad by the northern fields, in a civilian cutter under Escobaran charter: two women, one old and one ancient, and eight crates of archive.

Moira ghem Estif came down first — a hundred and twenty-six years, upright with that haut carriage which does not concede to gravity because it has not invited gravity into the conversation — set foot on Barrayaran soil for the first time in more than a century, surveyed the horizon where there had once been roofs, and said only:

“It is lower than I remember. Cities hold the horizon up.” Then she turned to the second woman descending behind her: “Mind the third step, my dear. On this planet everything is two centimeters more obstinate than you expect.”

Haut Pel Navar — at ninety, in white without a single mark of rank, which was itself the highest rank — took the third step, then the soil, and stood motionless for one long moment, facing the sunrise, like someone checking whether worlds weigh differently. If Alys’s protocol had provided anything for that moment, it was a page left blank.

Ekaterin met them. That too was a page of the protocol — perhaps the most important one: not the Count, not a service, not an office, but the mistress of the garden, with work gloves tucked into her belt, as years ago in a conservatory on the other end of the war. The two women, the white and the silvered, looked at each other, and Pel Navar spoke the first haut words on Barrayaran soil in a hundred years:

“You once showed me a garden grown upon bad memory. I have come to see what grows upon memory returned.”

The circuit lasted until noon. They walked the lanes that were streets, past the beds that were homes, and Moira ghem Estif walked more and more slowly — not from her years but from the reading: she read the names of the beds and stopped at every third or fourth, and once said, “there was a bakery here, a one-eyed man kept it, he carried half the street on credit,” and Ingi, the senior ranger accompanying them with the map of the old grid, checked and confirmed in a suddenly dry voice: bed “Weavers’ Street 11, Darev family, bakers.” The centenarian remembered the right houses in the right places. Archive walked beside archive.

And two paces behind the group, with the documents of an inspector of grain deliveries and the expression of a man on long-overdue leave, walked a tall quiet man named Greg, whom no one introduced to anyone. Pel Navar looked at him once, at the greeting — a single look with eyes in which twelve centuries of genetics missed nothing — and did not look at him again, which was the most perfect diplomatic act of the day.

V. The Empty Frame

The man was waiting for them at the entrance to the market square, where the lane narrowed and he could not be walked around, and he held in both hands a portrait frame. Empty.

His name was Grozdan Ilev, he was seventy-one, third generation of those who had not come back: his grandmother, then a girl from the riverside villages, had gone to the market at Vashnoi that morning with her mother, three sisters, and a brother, and had come back alone, because they had sent her home for a forgotten parcel. All her life, they told it in the district, she had kept an empty frame on the wall — her family had been of the kind for whom no photograph had ever existed: poor, riverside, never inside a photographer’s. Names in the bed they had. Faces from nowhere.

The old man did not shout. He did not bar the way with menace. He simply stood, the frame before his chest, and when the group halted he addressed Miles — only Miles, pointedly — in a voice worn thin by seventy years of patience:

“My lord Count. I hear they’re bringing faces. Good. Only mine have no faces in their archives either, because mine were the sort empires don’t photograph — they photograph them only on the labor-levy lists. So I’m standing here to ask: when you hand out the faces of the city, what do you give us, the riverside folk? Another empty frame?”

Alys’s protocol had no page for this. Miles felt the day tilt — and then haut Pel Navar did something no haut had done in the history of two worlds: she stepped forward, in front of her host, broke her own invisibility, and spoke directly to a Barrayaran peasant.

“A name,” she said. Not an order; an archivist’s requisition. “The grandmother’s. Maiden name. And the village.”

“Ileva, Stana. Dolno Kraiche,” said the old man, too astonished not to answer.

Pel turned to the crates the porters were just then carrying into the pavilion, said four words to her assistant, and waited, motionless, offering neither explanation nor hope — haut do not promise. The search took eleven minutes, during which no one on the square spoke. Then the assistant returned with a thin plate, and Pel handed it to the old man with both hands, by a custom that was neither Cetagandan nor Barrayaran but simply correct:

“Satrapy review, market day, entry register of the south gate. The Empire photographed everyone entering the city, sir. The riverside folk as well. The riverside folk especially — we counted them as labor, which is why we kept their faces.” A pause, precisely measured. “The shame of the reason is ours. The faces are yours.”

On the plate, in a grainy frame from an entry register of a hundred and five years ago, stood a woman and five children with market baskets, frozen one step from the gate of a city that had three hours left to it. The girl at the end of the row was looking straight into the lens, frowning against the sun.

Grozdan Ilev looked at the plate for a very long time. Then he unfolded the empty frame, laid it on the ground beside the bed of the street he was no longer barring, and said, addressing no one in particular, which meant he was addressing everyone:

“She looked like my mother. She said so all her life, and we had no way to see it.”

VI. The Reading

There was no ceremony — Alys’s protocol had forbidden one explicitly, because ceremonies have hosts and guests, and this day must have no sides. There was only what the garden-city already did without guests: in the early afternoon the readers came, the schoolchildren from Hassadar, one per street, to read the names aloud, as on every first Saturday.

The difference was that this time beside each child walked a porter with a plate from the eight crates, and after each name, for the first time in a hundred and five years, came a face.

Pel Navar asked for a street. The protocol did not give her one — Ingi gave it to her, with the map: Weavers’ Street, eleven beds, reader — a girl of about ten with very serious braids. The haut walked beside the child the whole afternoon, bed by bed, and when the girl read out a name, Pel turned the plate face toward the bed — toward the home — and held it so as long as was needed before they moved on. She did not say one word through all eleven beds. Haut do not apologize. Haut, Vashnoi district discovered that afternoon, can instead stand three hours under a foreign sun and show the dead that their faces have been kept.

And among the people along the lanes — hundreds had come; rumor in a district travels faster than a cutter — walked an inspector of grain deliveries doing what emperors cannot and people can: he stood in the queue at the pavilion, leafed through the registers on equal terms with everyone, gave up his place to a pregnant woman, and for half an hour held the ladder for the archivist sorting plates by street. An old farmer beside him, having waited his own turn for forty minutes, said without looking at him, “Big day, eh?” — and Greg Bleakman, inspector, answered with the purest truth uttered by the Crown in decades:

“The biggest I’ve been at.”

VII. The Presence

That evening, with the guests’ cutter already gone and Yadwiga’s flowers lighting their soft green night shift over the Mine clearing, the Emperor and his Auditor sat on a bench at the edge of the square — an inspector and a count, by no protocol whatever — and watched the last people disperse from the pavilion with plates under their arms.

“The Council will know within three days,” said Miles. “Half will demand an explanation. A quarter will demand my resignation. One or two will demand war, from inertia.”

“And all of them will be too late,” said Gregor, “because within three days five hundred families in this district will have on their walls a face that for a hundred years was only a name. One does not vote against that, Miles. It is one of those things that become irreversible in the instant they happen.” He was silent a moment. “Do you know what I understood today, in the queue? Empires apologize with archives. People apologize with presence. You and I have spent our whole lives translating from the one into the other.”

“She never once said I am sorry,” Miles observed.

“No,” Gregor agreed. “She stood three hours in the sun showing one street of dead people their own faces. I have been Emperor for forty-five years, Miles. I have heard every possible I am sorry.” He rose from the bench and tugged his inspector’s jacket straight. “That today was the first one I believed.”

They walked down the lane toward the shuttles — past the beds, past the names, past the flowers glowing in the dark that no one owned any longer — and at the very edge of the square Gregor stopped once more, by the bed of Weavers’ Street, where beside one name there now stood a small propped plate with a girl frowning against the sun.

“Tell Ekaterin,” said the Emperor, “that her garden has grown. It bears past now, as well.”

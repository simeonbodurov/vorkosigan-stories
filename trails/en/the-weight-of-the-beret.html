The Weight of the Beret

An adventure of Simone Vorkosigan — first journey alone

I. The Send-Off

At sixteen, Simone Vorkosigan set off for Sergyar alone, which cost her father approximately as much effort as it cost her nothing.

“One courier,” said Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan at breakfast, in the tone of a man making a final reasonable concession. “Discreet. You won’t even notice him.”

“I’ll notice him,” said Simone.

“Half a courier.”

“Da.”

“Fine.” Miles sighed and pushed a small comm unit with a single button across the table. “This, then. You press it only if. I’m not asking whether you’ll carry it. I’m asking whether you’ll carry it somewhere it can be pressed from.”

“I’ll carry it,” said Simone, and stowed it in her inside pocket, next to the ticket. It was a fair trade and they both knew it: the button instead of the shadow, weight instead of surveillance.

Her mother said nothing about couriers. Ekaterin Vorkosigan merely checked the packing list — once, calmly, the way one checks a seam — and, at the bottom of the pack, under the spare pullover, found the familiar soft lump.

“You’re taking it,” she said. It was not a question.

“I’m taking it,” said Simone.

The gray beret had traveled with her for three years, ever since it came out of Grandmother Naismith’s old trunk and somehow failed to go back in. Simone did not wear it on her head — almost never, almost nowhere. She carried it the way one carries a stone from a summit: so as to remember you were there, and not to talk about it.

The evening before departure she made a round of the house to say goodbye, which at sixteen is done in secret, under the pretext of hunting for a charger. She stopped in the library, where her father was ostensibly reading reports and had in fact laid out on the side table, as if by accident, three charts of the jump routes to Sergyar with the emergency stations marked. Simone studied them gravely, said “good charts,” and Miles beamed like a man whose gift has been accepted without being identified as a gift.

Selig wrote from the Academy that same evening: Three jumps, one transfer, zero mountains. Even you can manage. If something does go wrong — count. Brothers of eighteen are insufferable. Simone kept the letter.

The route really was simple: shuttle to orbit, liner through three jumps, transfer at Waypoint Station, and a final leg to Sergyar, where Grandmother Cordelia would be waiting on the dock — ninety-five years old, Vicereine in retirement, who met shuttles in person because, in her own words, “protocol dies last, but it does die.”

As far as Waypoint everything ran on schedule. Simone ate two lunch menus, won three games of tactics off an elderly gentleman who afterward stared at the board for a long time, and watched two jumps through the viewport with that expanded, ceremonial attention with which one regards a thing described all one’s life at the family table. Then the schedule ended.

II. Ring D

The impact woke her in the third hour of the layover — not a crash, but worse: a short metallic sound somewhere deep in the station’s bones, followed by a silence in which the whole transfer lounge stopped breathing at once.

Then the sirens spoke, and after them a voice that was trying to sound calm and frightened people more than the sirens did:

“Micrometeorite strike in Ring D. The section is being sealed as a precaution. Passengers in transfer zone D-three, please remain calm. Repeat—”

The bulkheads closed with a soft, final sigh. Zone D-three — one lounge, two corridors, a sanitary facility and a baggage alcove — became a small self-contained world with twenty-eight passengers on the manifest, two station staff, and air that everyone abruptly began to be aware of.

Simone sat by the viewport and did what she always did before deciding whether to be afraid: she counted.

Twenty-eight passengers on the departure list. Plus the two staff — an ensign-stationer named Garin, young, with a collar that had suddenly grown tight, and a middle-aged technician with bitten nails. Thirty.

Plus the little boy.

The boy was about seven, asleep on a suitcase beside an elderly woman whose hands were gathered in her lap like a bundle, and he was on no list at all — Simone had seen them boarded at the last minute from the platform dock, with a hand-written pass that had stayed with the dockhand outside. Madame Veren and her grandson Teo, traveling to Sergyar to join her daughter.

Thirty-one, Simone told herself. We start out thirty-one.

Ensign Garin stood up in the middle of the lounge, raised his hands for quiet, and got more of it than he wanted.

“Ladies and gentlemen. The situation is under control. The damage is localized in an adjacent sector. The tug with the repair crew arrives in—” he glanced at his wrist and made his first mistake, because forty people watched his face do arithmetic “—fourteen hours. Until then the section is autonomous. There is air. Please be patient.”

“And heating?” someone asked from the back, because the floor was already saying it for him: the zone’s heat exchange was on emergency ration and the temperature was descending by one patient degree at a time.

“We’re working on it,” said Garin, which in every language in the galaxy means no.

III. The Counter

The first two hours were the loudest. One man insisted the bulkhead be opened because he had connections; the bulkhead remained unpersuaded. A woman cried for ten minutes and then stopped, because crying in a sealed room is like yawning — it spreads, and she was clever enough to work that out herself. Then the cold took the floor and people went quiet in the corners — and the corners, Simone noticed, were the problem. The lounge had a warm end, by the interior bulkhead, and a cold one, by the hull; the fastest had collected in the warm end and the slowest had been left in the cold: Madame Veren with Teo, a very old man with a cane, a young family with a baby that no longer cried but whimpered in economical portions.

Simone waited another ten minutes for some adult to do something. The adults were doing what adults do in a sealed room: watching the ensign, while the ensign watched the door behind which a different, more senior adult was supposed to arrive.

In the eleventh minute Simone stood up, took a notebook out of her pack — paper, a mountain habit — and went first of all to the baby.

“Rotation,” she said to the room, in a voice that neither pleaded nor apologized but simply announced a timetable. “The warm end changes every hour. First hour: children, the elderly, and the baby. There will be a list. There will be names.” She opened the notebook. “I’m Simone. Who’s by the viewport? Name, please.”

“Little girl,” began a large man in a merchant’s coverall, in the tone that begins sentences that end badly, “here we wait for official instructions, not—”

“The instruction is fourteen hours and one degree an hour,” said Simone, without lifting her eyes from the notebook. “You’re welcome to wait for it in the warm end when your turn comes. There will be turns. Name, please.”

The man opened his mouth. From the baggage wall another voice spoke — low, rusty, amused:

“Give her your name, son. It’ll go faster.”

The dockhand was about sixty, with the shoulders of a man who had loaded things all his life, and sat on a crate with the patience of the crate itself. His name was Ansen, he had worked Waypoint for eleven years, and he did not explain why he stationed himself beside Simone’s list like a wall behind a blueprint, but after him the queue of names formed on its own.

By the fifth hour zone D-three had: a list of thirty-one names (Teo appeared with an added +1, seven yrs, asleep); a warm-end rotation observed with that grim satisfaction with which people observe fairness once they have seen it written down; a water ration distributed by the technician with the bitten nails, who turned out to be iron once given a task; and one claustrophobic accountant from Komarr whom Simone set to keeping a duplicate of the list, because a man who is counting has no hands free for trembling.

Ensign Garin accepted all of this in the only intelligent way available: he declared it his own instruction. Simone nodded at him gravely. In the notebook, under staff, she had written beside his name: will turn out well, if he survives today.

IV. The Long Night

The hours from the sixth to the eleventh stayed in Simone’s memory as one long, cold, strangely warm thing.

At the sixth hour the temperature was fifteen degrees and the large merchant — his name was Brant, he shipped spare parts, and he was ashamed with a delay — came to the list and asked to be recorded as a volunteer for the permanent cold end. “I’ve got an insulated coverall,” he told the floor rather than Simone. “The others haven’t.” Simone entered it without comment, which was the greatest courtesy she could do him, and moved the young family with the baby into the middle band permanently.

At the seventh hour it emerged that the old man with the cane was having a birthday. His granddaughter gave it away in a whisper, like a military secret. The lounge, which had needed an excuse not to think about the air, sang him “Many Years” in four false voices and one true one — the accountant from Komarr turned out to be a tenor — and the technician produced a packet of dry biscuits from somewhere and declared the cake symbolic. The old man blew out a pocket lamp instead of a candle and said it was his best birthday in twenty years, because at the others nobody had counted him so carefully.

At the eighth hour Teo woke, established that the world had shrunk to one lounge, and accepted this with the pragmatism of seven: he asked whether there were other children, was given the baby by way of answer, and appointed himself its guard. Madame Veren thanked Simone three times for the blanket from the rotation and three times got her name wrong — Mona, Sima, Mila — and all three times Simone said “that’s right,” because names matter when you are writing them down, not when frozen grandmothers are saying them to you.

At the ninth hour Ansen talked about the Dendarii.

He talked ostensibly to Teo, who had dragged the baby over to his crate, but he was speaking to half the room: eleven years of ground crew, docks and cargo ramps, Admiral Quinn who remembered the names of the third-shift technicians too. And one photograph in the flagship’s mess, an old one, from before his time: a small admiral in a beret, tilted to a regulation that existed in no regulations.

“He was their first,” said Ansen. “And they say he counted everybody. Not off a list. By face. He’d go down into the hold after an action and count.” He shrugged. “I don’t know if it’s true. I know it got told, and that from the telling we worked better.”

Simone sat two paces away with the notebook in her lap and her pack against her leg, and said nothing. But she noticed — out of the corner of her eye, the way important things are noticed — that the pack’s top pocket had come open in the cold and a corner of gray cloth showed. And she noticed that Ansen had noticed it. The dockhand said nothing. He only looked at the cloth, then at her, then at the list in her lap, and nodded once, to himself, like a man for whom two parts of an old blueprint have just fitted together.

At the tenth hour the baby finally slept deeply and the lounge, by unspoken agreement, went over to whispering. At the eleventh the tug came on the comm: running two hours ahead of schedule.

Nobody applauded, because the baby was asleep. Thirty people raised their fists in complete silence, and Simone wrote in the margin of the notebook: the quietest cheer in the history of the Imperium.

V. Thirty

The tug arrived two hours early and ruined everything.

“Evacuation by groups,” announced Garin, his collar tighter now. “The shuttle takes fifteen at a time. The zone is then vented for repair — full preservation, power and atmosphere to zero. Personal effects in hand, baggage stays for cargo.”

The first group — fifteen, children and the elderly first — went through the flex tube under the shuttle’s counter. Simone sent Madame Veren with them, and the family with the baby, and the old man with the cane and his granddaughter, and ticked each name in the notebook as it left. The second group shuffled after them. Simone walked last, out of mountain habit: the leader closes the column.

At the hatch stood an officer from the tug with a manifest, counting aloud with professional boredom:

“…twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty. End of list. Thirty on the manifest, thirty aboard. Close it up.”

“Thirty-one,” said Simone.

The officer looked at her the way one looks at a discrepancy in bookkeeping.

“The manifest says thirty, miss. Twenty-eight passengers, two staff. All ticked.”

“We boarded thirty-one.” Simone produced the notebook. “Teo Veren, seven years old, hand-written pass from the platform dock. The pass stayed outside. The child is inside.”

“The child is with his grandmother.” The officer nodded toward the shuttle’s cabin, where Madame Veren sat with her lap full of a bundle… which on second look proved to be a coat.

Madame Veren raised eyes to them clouded by fourteen hours of fear and eighty years of life, and said with the absolute certainty of confusion:

“Teo went ahead. With the first group. To his uncle.”

“There’s no uncle aboard,” said Simone. The cold she felt had nothing to do with the temperature. “He was asleep in the baggage alcove. Behind the netting, in the warm by the sheepskins. I logged him there at twelve-forty, at the tenth hour, when he was minding the baby and then went to ‘guard the luggage.’” She turned to the officer. “The zone is being preserved. When?”

The officer looked at his wrist and this time his face did not do arithmetic — it went white.

“The venting is running on schedule… nine minutes.”

VI. Nine Minutes

What happened in the next nine minutes the reports described variously.

The tug’s manifest noted “re-inspection of compartments at passenger request.” Ensign Garin’s log, more honest, recorded: Passenger S. refused closure of the hatch, positioned herself in the flex tube and stated that the shuttle departed thirty-one or did not depart. I aborted the venting with my emergency code. Correctly.

Garin’s code stopped the countdown at the forty-first second of the ninth minute — but stopping a venting does not put the atmosphere back into the pumps: the zone was already at a third of pressure and falling by inertia, and the emergency power was dying sector by sector, like a house through which someone walks turning off lamps.

Three of them went in: Garin with the code and a lamp, Ansen because he knew the alcoves of Ring D better than their blueprints, and Simone because the notebook was in her hand and in the notebook, with the coordinate precision of a mountain child, it said: baggage alcove, second section, behind the cargo netting, right, by the gray sheepskins.

They walked through a room dying on schedule: frost creeping over the walls in visible tongues, air that could be felt thinning, silence with a new and evil quality. Garin shouted the child’s name; his voice carried less and less far, because thin air does not carry. Ansen did not shout. Ansen went straight to the second section, moved the cargo netting aside with one hand, and reached in among the gray sheepskins exactly where the notebook said.

Teo was asleep. The deep, careless sleep of seven years old, in the warm nest between two sheepskins, chosen with the unerring instinct of a child, which in another six minutes would have become the quietest place in the sector — permanently.

The dockhand carried him out through the flex tube wrapped in one of the sheepskins, and the boy woke only at the hatch, looked at the intent faces around him, and said reproachfully:

“I was guarding the luggage.”

“You guarded it,” said Ansen, in a voice of absolute seriousness. “It’s all present. We checked it against the list.”

The shuttle accepted its thirty-first passenger in complete silence, and then the roomful of people who for fourteen hours had grumbled about lists and rotations and the little girl with the notebook did the most sensible thing of the day: they did not applaud, because there were now two sleeping children aboard. Only Brant, the large merchant in the coverall, rose silently and stayed standing while Simone came down the aisle. After him the others stood too.

Simone sat down in her place, opened the notebook, and ticked the final line in an even hand. We started out thirty-one.

VII. Arrival

The tug’s formalities took an hour. Ensign Garin filled out the report himself, and when he came to the line identifying data of passenger S., he took her documents, read them, blinked once — slowly, like a man reordering the last fourteen hours — and said only:

“Ah.”

“Yes,” said Simone.

“Is that why—” he did not finish.

“No,” said Simone. “The counting isn’t from the name. The counting is from a mountain. The name was just watching.”

Garin thought about that, and then did something Simone entered in the notebook that same evening, under staff, as a final assessment: he struck out the official phrase with the assistance of a passenger and wrote in its place, legibly, at the insistence and by the list of passenger S., without whose counting the outcome would have been otherwise. Then he signed it. People who correct their own reports turn out well. He would.

Ansen found her at the hatch for the final leg, before separation for Sergyar. He stood with his arms folded and looked at her pack — more precisely at its top pocket, buttoned now.

“Eleven years’ ground crew with the Dendarii,” he said without preamble. “Under Admiral Quinn. There’s a photograph in the flagship’s mess, an old one, from before my time. A small admiral in a beret of exactly that cut.” He paused. “I won’t ask where you got it, girl. I’ll only tell you that I knew you by the counting before I ever saw the beret. That one in the photograph, the old hands said, counted everybody. By name. Such things get handed down, it seems. Not by blood. By habit.”

“By habit,” Simone agreed.

“One thing more, since I’m old and may.” Ansen nodded at the pocket. “Stones from summits are carried in a pack. Berets are worn on the head. You’ll understand the difference when you need it.”

On the dock at Sergyar, exactly where the flex tube ended, stood a ninety-five-year-old woman, upright, with white hair and eyes the color of thinned sky, which found Simone in the second row of disembarking passengers and narrowed at the corners — first with joy, and then, noticing something, with that particular Betan interest which in this family was handed down instead of a coat of arms.

Simone had taken the beret out on the shuttle, had looked at it for the whole last leg, and had put it on only in the flex tube — not as a stone from a summit, but as a hat: jauntily, a little askew, exactly as it sat in a photograph in a mess she had never seen.

“It suits you,” said Grandmother Cordelia by way of greeting.

“It’s heavy,” said Simone.

“I know,” said Cordelia, and hugged her. “That’s why it’s worn on the head, love. The spine takes it there.”

That evening Simone wrote a letter home, short, because important letters are short:

Mama, Da. The trip went with one delay and one station I’ll tell you about in person. We started out thirty-one. We arrived thirty-one. Kiss Selig for me and tell him I counted — and tell Da the button wasn’t needed. I carried it somewhere it could be pressed from. That turned out to be enough.

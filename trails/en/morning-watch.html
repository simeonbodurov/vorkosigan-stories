Morning Watch

A Cetagandan story — of two answers to one question

I. The Proposal

Three years after the forest of Mu Ceta, the question came back to Ghem-General Dag Benin through the dullest channel imaginable: a budget memorandum.

The memorandum came from the colonial administration and concerned the other free population — the one the court had been whispering about even before Harvest: sixteen hundred giant mantids on a distant colonial world, four hundred eleven of them wild-line and twelve hundred of the new generation, free by a decision Benin himself had recommended and the Empire had accepted with its characteristic gift for converting a dead end into a precedent. For three years the population had lived beside the fortified colony in a balance no one dared call peace for fear of jinxing it: the mantids guarded their breeding clutch on the plateau, developed their language, and every morning at first light formed up — sixteen hundred motionless silhouettes against the sunrise, the most beautiful page of the whole history, the one thing they had kept from their service.

The memorandum proposed closing the page. The wording was impeccable: “optimization of security expenditure,” “consolidation of protected populations,” “relocation to the closed reserve on Mu Ceta, which possesses established infrastructure.” Translated from administrative: the plateau is open, the reserve is fenced, let us gather them in one place and guard one fence instead of two.

And between the lines, where Benin read best, stood the real reason: two months earlier a “survey cutter with damaged navigation” had put down on the plateau. The mantids had surrounded it — merely surrounded it, in a silent ring of forty specimens — and the cutter had lifted at a speed inconsistent with damaged navigation. Analysis of its track led through three false registrations to a free station, where the trail went cold in the Jacksonian manner.

Someone had come to look at the merchandise. The Raal model was sealed, the archives locked under the Star Crèche, the Harvest program burned to the foundations. In the entire galaxy the last living carriers of that genetics walked free on one open plateau. The market, Benin knew from thirty years’ service, does not leave such things walking for long.

He took the memorandum where all questions with a thread were now taken — to the Star Crèche. Haut Pel Navar read it, set it down, and said:

“The administration proposes that we relocate sixteen hundred persons because it would be cheaper to guard them so. Tell me, General, what I got wrong in that sentence.”

“The word relocate,” said Benin. “And the word persons was not in the memorandum.”

“The second error is theirs,” said haut Pel. “The first we shall correct ourselves. One’s own are not relocated, General. One’s own are asked.” She turned to the window, to the gardens. “Send them an embassy. And send the right one.”

II. The Embassy

The right embassy consisted of two: Sai Vetra, voice of the work, the only person in the Empire holding a post invented personally by a consort — and Serp.

Extracting the alpha from the reserve took the Crèche’s jurists a month. The formulation they finally settled on — “an unfinished work accompanying its own voice, with the status of delegate” — went through eleven drafts, two of which Vetra kept for her archive, because in them one could see an empire learning, word by word, to speak of something for which it had no words. The personnel officers no longer gasped. They merely filed.

The task itself was explained to Serp in one evening, on the bank of the great river, in signs: far — others — like you — question. Serp regarded her a long while in the “thinking” posture — forelimbs folded, head low — then made the sign he had built in his second year in the forest, from kin and river: the other kins. And added his own question, the old one, the unwearing one: After?

“We are going to find out what their after is,” Vetra said aloud. “And to ask whether they want ours.”

The Crèche transport flew eleven days. Serp spent most of them motionless before the viewscreen, three meters forty of patient chitin against the stars, and Vetra, who kept her journal on this voyage too, wrote: “Day three. He asks about the distance every day. He has built a sign for far that we did not have: river, repeated three times. He measures the world in rivers.”

“Day seven. I showed him a star chart. He looked a long time at the point of Mu Ceta, then at the point of our destination, then at the distance between them. He made the sign one’s own over the first point and a question-sign over the second. I explained, insofar as signs allow: others live there, from the same beginning, but by another road. His answer kept me awake: he pointed at himself, pointed at the screen, and made river-three-times plus kin. Free translation: we were far ones too. I do not know which of us is teaching the other a language.”

“Day eleven. We land tomorrow. I have asked the pilot to set down before first light. If what they say of the formation is true, Serp must see it from the first second. We owe him that. We owe it to them as well — that their first guest of their own kind should see them at their best sentence.”

III. The Formation at First Light

The plateau received them at first light — deliberately. When the ramp came down on the stone it was still dark, only the eastern ridge outlined with that gray edge which precedes all sunrises on all worlds, and in the gray, across the whole width of the plateau, stood sixteen hundred silhouettes. Motionless. Ordered. Facing east.

Vetra had read the reports, had watched the recordings, had written analyses of “ritualized residual behavior.” The recordings did not prepare her. A recording shows a formation; in the flesh one saw something else — that the formation breathed: a very slight, simultaneous sway of sixteen hundred bodies, in a rhythm no one was setting, because the rhythm was common.

She heard Serp come out of the transport behind her and stop.

She turned. The alpha of the forest stood on the ramp looking at the formation with his head tilted, and from the chitin, from the stance, from the folded raptorial limbs, Vetra — who knew his language better than any living human — read something she had not seen in him since the parade ground of Base Yugen: Serp had come to attention.

Then the sun cleared the ridge. The light ran across the plateau like a tide, reached the first rank, and the formation dissolved — not all at once but by companies, rank after rank, with that dry, manifold sound of steel set in motion — and toward the ramp there detached a single specimen: old, enormous, his chitin bleached by years of wild winters, scarred in the way that is left not by surgery but by living. One of the four hundred eleven. Of the wild line that had survived everything without a single operator.

The two alphas halted ten paces apart.

The colony commandant — a weary colonel of the Rau circle who for three years had written reports to the capital beginning “the situation remains unchanged,” and had learned to consider that sentence a success — stood beside Vetra and watched what she watched.

“Three years I’ve observed them every morning,” he said quietly. “I thought I’d seen everything. I had never seen two generals greet each other.”

And the two alphas began to speak.

IV. Two Languages

The languages had diverged. Vetra established it on the first day with linguistic precision and a lump in her throat: the forest and the plateau had started from different beginnings — the forest from forty-one operator gestures, the plateau from nothing, from bare chitin and necessity — and in three years and in thirty they had arrived at different grammars.

The forest spoke with the forelimbs: fast, economical, operator-fashion — a language of conversation, of two, at close range. The plateau spoke with the whole body: postures, intervals, position in space — a language of many, at distance, of formation. In the forest the sentence was a gesture. On the plateau the sentence was a placement: who stands where in relation to whom and to what. Vetra filled eighty pages of journal in five days, and on the sixth she wrote the sentence later quoted in three institutes: “They have not developed two dialects. They have developed two solutions to the question of what a language is — and both are correct.”

Serp learned the foreign grammar at a rate that reduced the journal to abbreviations. By the fifth day the two alphas were already building bridge-signs: the forest offering river and home, the plateau offering its own. One of them, the plateau’s most frequent, Vetra could not translate for a long while: a stance with the raptorial limbs opened, turned outward, toward the horizon. Not a threat — the limbs faced outward, but the bodies stood between the horizon and something else.

It was only when the old alpha led Serp — and Vetra, at a respectful distance — to the shallow basin at the heart of the plateau that she understood.

In the basin, beneath overhanging rock shelves, sheltered from the winds, lay the clutch: hundreds of egg capsules in ordered rows, each a hand’s breadth from its neighbor, guarded by a standing ring of watchers who relieved one another without command, by an internal clock that Vetra observed for two hours and never once caught in error.

The sign meant watch. And the formation at first light, sixteen hundred bodies between the sunrise and the basin, every morning, for three years, meant what Vetra wrote in her journal with a shaking hand and what afterward the whole Celestial Garden read:

“They have not kept the formation out of nostalgia for the service. Their formation is a sentence, and the sentence reads: this is guarded. The plateau has no fence. The plateau has a morning watch.”

That evening she put to the old alpha, through Serp, the Empire’s question: the other place — forest — river — fence — safe — question. Will you come?

The old alpha regarded the signs. Then he turned toward the basin, toward the rows of capsules beneath the shelves, and answered with three postures, which Serp rendered into the language of the forest and Vetra into human, though translation was hardly needed:

Here. The kin is here. After is here.

V. The Basin

They stayed another eight days — officially “for completeness of the report,” unofficially because neither Vetra nor Serp wished to leave, and the old alpha gave no posture indicating that the guests had outstayed.

Vetra spent the days in the basin, by a permission no human had ever received: to sit at the edge of the watchers’ ring and record. She learned the rhythm of the reliefs — by the sun, not by hours. She learned that the capsules were turned every third day, each by its own watcher, in an order that was never confused. She learned that when the wind swung to the north, the whole ring unfolded a quarter turn before the first cold breath reached the stone — they read the weather by pressure, with their antennae, an hour ahead.

And on the fifth day she learned why the wild line had survived where everything else of the Raal model had died or gone feral to beasthood. The old alpha showed her — through Serp, with the patience of a teacher before a capable but slow pupil — the place at the northern end of the basin: three rows of old, empty, carefully preserved capsule beds, worn smooth by decades of wind. The first clutch. The one the four hundred eleven had kept through the first wild winter, without operators, without rations, without orders — when no one had told them they must, and no one had told them how.

“I asked him how they decided,” Vetra said to the commandant that evening, the journal still open in her lap. “That first winter. No language yet, nothing. How do a thousand starving abandoned combat organisms decide to guard eggs instead of eating them.”

“And what did he answer?”

“He answered with the posture watch. Only that.” Vetra shook her head. “I think my question was a human one. For them there was no decision. There was a clutch. The rest is grammar.”

Serp spent his days elsewhere — in the ranks. He walked among the plateau’s companies, learned their postures, showed them the forest’s gestures, and all day long ten or fifteen of the young generation gathered around him, the twelve hundred born free, who had never seen an operator and regarded the guest from Mu Ceta the way one regards a distant relative with an interesting accent.

And at the morning formation, from the third day onward, Serp took his place at the end of the first rank — a guest’s place that no one had shown him and no one disputed.

VI. The Night

The Jacksonians came on the ninth night — Benin would note afterward in his report, with his driest quartermaster’s irony, that the market has an unerring instinct for moments when there are witnesses on the ground holding the highest possible credentials.

Two cutters without lights, a landing in the dead zone behind the northern ridge, eleven men with thermal optics and heavy stunners, and a route plotted by someone who had learned his lesson from the “survey” flight: not toward the adult specimens — toward the basin. Toward the clutch. An egg capsule does not resist, does not weigh much, and carries within it the entire Raal model, clean, intact, ready for the incubator of any free station. Eleven men could carry out forty capsules in an hour and convert three generations of someone else’s history into start-up capital.

The colony’s seismic sensors caught them in the second minute after landing. Vetra, roused by the alarm, arrived at the command post with the journal still in her hand and found there the commandant, the duty officer — and Dag Benin, arrived the previous evening on an inspection whose timing no longer looked accidental.

“We observe,” said Benin, without turning from the screens. “We do not intervene unless required. I want this buyer whole, with his route, his connections, and his expression. And besides—” he paused, watching the thermal image of the plateau “—I suspect our intervention will not be required.”

The Jacksonians got to within four hundred meters of the basin. The watch let them come that far.

Then the plateau moved. Not a rush — a movement, by companies, by a habit no wind had unravelled: four hundred silhouettes came out of the darkness simultaneously, from four directions, in a perfect enveloping order, and stood around the eleven men in a ring of thirty meters’ radius. No one attacked. The ring simply stood — three and a half meters of chitin for every two meters of arc, raptorial limbs folded, heads tilted in the “sighting” posture — and contracted by one pace a minute, patiently, unambiguously, like a tide.

In the command post no one spoke. On the thermal image one could see how, out in the dark beyond the ring, the remaining twelve hundred took up a second, outer rank — not facing the prey, but facing outward, toward the ridge, toward the cutters, toward every direction from which someone else might yet come. The morning watch, deployed in a night order, by a sentence no one had taught them.

Eleven heavy stunners were laid on the stone, without a single shot fired, before the ring had closed to twenty meters. The Jacksonians, one of them said afterward at interrogation, did not surrender out of fear. They surrendered because there was something worse than fear in that contracting formation: there was order. “You understand that it isn’t a beast hunting you,” the man said. “You understand that an army is arresting you. A beast, you fight.”

At first light, with the cutters already under the seals of internal security and the eleven under escort, Vetra stood at the edge of the plateau and watched the formation take up its morning position: sixteen hundred bodies between the sunrise and the basin, and one more — Serp, at the end of the first rank, standing to his full three meters forty.

“The buyer’s trail?” she asked Benin, without turning.

“Eight names, three registrations, one free station,” said Benin. “We shall walk it to the end, but we both know what we shall find: a man who decided that because something walks free, it belongs to no one.” He was silent a moment. “The Empire thought the same for a long while, Ghem-Lieutenant. The difference is that the Empire occasionally learns.”

VII. The Answers

The Star Crèche’s decision came out a month later and was short by their standards: three pages.

The plateau of the colonial world was declared a closed reserve of the Crèche — the second in their history — and its population recognized as “a work that has completed itself”: a formula polished by the jurists after a precedent involving a singer from the outer worlds, of which the court spoke with admiration. The grounds fitted into a single sentence, dictated personally by haut Pel Navar, and Benin read it three times before initialing it:

“One’s own is known by this: that when you ask it, it answers — and when you attack it, it does not answer with more than is necessary.”

There would be no consolidation. The Empire would guard two fences — or more precisely one, because the plateau, Benin’s report observed, had been guarding itself, better than any infrastructure, at zero budget and with a single technology: a morning watch, three years, without one missed sunrise.

Vetra and Serp departed on the fourteenth day. At the leave-taking — if the silent arrangement of two peoples of chitin facing one another may be called a leave-taking — the old alpha of the plateau came out before the formation and made toward Serp a long, slow sequence of signs in the bridge language: his own sign watch, then the forest’s sign home, then a pause, then the two together, fused into something new that neither world had built before.

Serp repeated it once, precisely, with folded limbs. Then he turned to Vetra and showed it to her — slowly, three times, as she had once shown him one’s own on a parade ground — to be sure that the voice of the work had understood it and would carry it where it belonged.

Vetra entered it in the journal that same evening, with the translation, about which this time there were no reservations whatever, and the entry reached the Celestial Garden, reached the white room without a single object, where a haut read it and stood motionless a long while before forwarding it upward, to the highest possible address, without comment, because comment was not needed:

“A new sign, built jointly by the two populations. ‘Watch’ + ‘home.’ Meaning, by the unanimous interpretation of both kins: that which you guard while it guards you. Proposed by them as the common name of the two reserves. I recommend acceptance.”

It was accepted.

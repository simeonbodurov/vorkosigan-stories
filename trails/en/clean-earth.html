Clean Earth

An adventure of Lady Ekaterin Vorkosigan — in Vorkosigan Vashnoi, twenty-five years after the flowers

I. The Last Flower

Twenty-five years after the first experimental plot, Ekaterin Vorkosigan’s dosimeter measured the air above the dead city and found nothing in it worth saying.

“Read it out loud,” said Enrique Borgos. He stood beside her on the hill above the old riverbed, without a protective suit, in his shirtsleeves, and that in itself was more eloquent than any instrument: the man who for a quarter of a century had never set foot in the zone without three layers of protection had come today in his shirtsleeves, deliberately, as to a festival. “I want to hear it with my ears.”

“Background of a clean forest,” Ekaterin read. “With deviation within instrument tolerance.”

“Background of a clean forest,” Enrique repeated, slowly, as though testing the words for acidity. Then he took off his spectacles and polished them for a long while with his back to her, and Ekaterin politely watched the river until he had finished.

One hundred five years since the Cetagandan bombs had turned Vorkosigan Vashnoi — the district’s first city, the pride of three generations — into glass and silence. Eighty of them the zone had stood locked: stakes, trefoil signs, dosimeters at every post, and one word with which the district frightened its children. And then Enrique’s bugs had come — ungainly, glossy, with a small glowing patch on the back — and for twenty-five years, generation after generation, they had grazed the poison out of the earth, stalk by stalk, isotope by isotope, and carried it on their backs to the collection points, glowing in the dark the brighter the fuller they were.

This spring the sectors had run out. The last — the center of the city itself, where the market square had once been — had given clean samples three quarters running, and today the final generation of bugs was coming in from the field forever: full, heavy, burning down to a soft green, on their way to the vitrification that would lock their cargo in glass for ten thousand years.

Ekaterin watched the ranch technicians working the plots with their collection boxes and thought of a sentence she had spoken twenty-five years ago, to a tired ranger, in a much younger world: “Even if it only becomes a park — a park where people can walk without protection, camp without dosimeters…”

Well, she thought. The park had become possible. What remained was the hardest part: deciding what should grow on the clean.

II. Three Proposals

The hearing in Hassadar lasted six hours, and at it, by Ekaterin’s count, three truths were spoken, each of which was correct and none of which was sufficient.

The builders spoke first, with a model. New Vashnoi: a city at the crossing of two monorails, fifty thousand jobs, “the return of the district’s heart.” Their speaker was young, well prepared, and clever enough to finish not with the numbers but with a sentence Ekaterin wrote down: “A district cannot forever carry a ghost capital in the middle of its map.”

The veterans and the old families spoke after them, without a model. An eighty-year-old woman rose on their behalf, upright as a pole, introduced herself only as “Mara Kosoeva, the zone took six of mine,” and said four sentences: beneath every future street lies a street; beneath every excavation, someone’s home; land that has taken so many dead is left to silence; and whoever wishes to build, the district is wide. Then she sat down. The silence after her lasted longer than the speech.

And the farmers from the surrounding valleys, fewest in number, spoke last and most briefly. Their elder, a tenant with hands like roots, said: land cleaned for a quarter century, bug by bug, is left neither to concrete nor to silence — clean earth is plowed. Otherwise what was it all for. And he added, without looking at anyone: “The dead of Vashnoi were people of our sort. People of our sort did not honor empty land.”

Count Miles Vorkosigan — to whom Vashnoi belonged personally, by a grant made to a child more than half a century ago with a bitter joke about a city that glows in the dark — heard out all three sides without interrupting, which at his age he had at last mastered, and in the end did the wisest thing Ekaterin had ever seen him do with power: he did not use it.

“City, grave, or field,” he said. “The district council has heard three answers. I have heard three halves of one. I charge Lady Vorkosigan with finding the whole.” And he added, for her alone, on the way out, with that smile which thirty years had not altered: “You cured me of a garden once. Cure me of a city as well.”

III. The Keeper

The man who knew the zone better than any living person was waiting for her at the north post: a tall man in his forties, with white hair and white lashes, with skin the Vashnoi sun had never managed to touch, and in the uniform of a senior district ranger.

“Lady Vorkosigan,” said Ingi, and bowed forest-fashion, with the head, not the waist.

“Ingi,” said Ekaterin. “I remember you at fifteen, with a pony.”

“I outlived the pony,” said Ingi. “Vadim retired last autumn, you know. He said the zone was no longer for guarding but for showing, and that was work for a more talkative man.” He smiled with the corner of his mouth. “Then he proposed me, which Martya called the only joke Vadim ever made in his life.”

They walked all day, and Ingi showed her the zone the way one shows a house one grew up in.

He showed her where the soil was sweet and the willows had come on their own — whole willow galleries along the old canals, self-settled the moment the bugs had passed, as though the trees had been queuing. He showed her where the old streets could still be read beneath the grass: straight, obstinate, more durable than their houses — he walked them without looking, from the memory of his feet, because he had grown up playing on the dead boulevards of a city whose name the adults did not say aloud. He showed her the corner where each spring his patrols now chased off not poachers but newlyweds, come to be photographed “in the forbidden” — and added, with ranger’s melancholy, that next year, when the status lapsed, he would have only their bad taste to chase off, and against that there is no service.

“And here—” he said toward midday, at a crossroads distinguishable only by shorter grass “—was the bakery on Titmouse Street. I know it from Vadim, he knows it from his grandfather. They say that in the mornings the smell reached the river.” Ingi was silent a moment. “I always slow down at this spot. All my life. From a habit that isn’t mine.”

Ekaterin walked, listened, and made notes in her book that were not for the report: the willows came on their own; the streets can be read; the smell reached the river; habits that aren’t ours.

IV. The Mounds

Toward evening, without asking, Ingi took her where Ekaterin had known he would take her: to the clearing with the small mounds, beneath the old trees — the graveyard of the children the zone had taken, before the coming of the woman who at least buried them with names.

The mounds were overgrown with low, thick grass and tended with a care that did not force itself on the eye and was for that very reason visible from everywhere. The newest was twenty years old and its stone read only MA ROGA.

“Vadim and I got it approved,” said Ingi quietly. “She died in the home in Hassadar, peacefully, insofar as she was ever capable of peace. But she had told Yadwiga: ‘don’t put me in the clean soil, my soil is here.’” He paused. “The court had given her the zone as a sentence. She made it into a home. Which of the two won, I wouldn’t undertake to say.”

They stayed on the clearing until dark and then longer, because Ingi, with a certain solemnity, announced that under the sector’s new status a thing unthinkable for twenty-five years was now permitted: fire. The first authorized campfire in the Vashnoi zone in one hundred five years. He laid it ranger-fashion, small and correct, and the two of them sat by it, while around them in the dark the last working bugs still glimmered here and there across the plots — sparse now, going out, like the last windows of a neighborhood going to bed.

“When I was small,” said Ingi, watching the fire, “Ma used to tell us the glow was the souls of the city. Not to bother them, they were doing their work. Then I grew up, got my training, and I know what it is: isotopes in collector tissue, half-life, dosimetry.” He nudged a twig. “And watching them go out, I think her way again. The souls are finishing their work. They’re going home.”

“And Yadwiga?” Ekaterin asked.

“Yadwiga is at the ranch, with Martya and Enrique, where she always was. She minds the goats and the new hatchings.” Ingi looked off into the dark. “She knows the bugs are being taken in, my lady. She asks the same thing every day. None of us has the heart to answer her.”

“What does she ask?”

“She asks,” said Ingi, “where they’ll plant the flowers now.”

V. The Ranch

The butter-bug ranch met Ekaterin with the smell of hay, of reagents, and of maple syrup — the last a new production line about which Mark, the business partner, maintained in his reports that it “at last makes the insect an unambiguous asset.” Martya Koudelka-Borgos met her in the yard, an apron over her lab coat, embraced her Koudelka-fashion — once, hard — and said:

“You’ve come for Yadwiga. Quite right. Only first you’ll eat, because in my house politics is not conducted on an empty stomach. Enrique! Out of the tank, we have district authority!”

Over lunch Enrique talked for forty minutes about vitrification, drew the molecular cage of glass on a napkin, knocked over the salt cellar, apologized to the salt cellar, and finally, with no transition whatever, said with complete clarity:

“Do you know what’s absurd, Ekaterin? For a quarter of a century I worked to make the bugs unnecessary. I succeeded. And now the whole project sits in my throat right here—” he pointed “—because the last generation goes into the glass with full bellies, like workers nobody thanks. I know it’s sentiment. I am a scientist; I recognize sentiment by definition. But Yadwiga—”

“Yadwiga,” said Martya quietly, “has called summer ‘the season of the flowers’ for thirty years. Come and see her.”

Yadwiga was forty and was still Yadwiga: a broad face, eyes that looked straight, five fingers on her right hand, the scars of the old surgeries faded into stories. She was sitting on the goat-pen fence, the goats listening to her — they had always listened to her — and when she saw Ekaterin she slid down and performed the curtsey Martya had taught her twenty-five years ago and which she executed with unvarying, concentrated pride.

“Milady. You’ve come about the flowers.”

“I’ve come about the flowers,” said Ekaterin.

She sat down beside her on the fence — at sixty and with an Auditor for a husband one learns to sit on fences — and told her the last part of the plan, the part that had not been written for the council but had been commissioned from Enrique up on the hill, while he was polishing his spectacles.

The new hatching was ripening in the laboratory: bugs with no work. No collector gene, no appetite for isotopes, no cargo — Enrique had called them, in the documentation, a “decorative line,” grumbling that he had worked wonders for a quarter century and would go down in history for night-lights. The only thing left from the old breed was the glow: soft, green, warm, but no longer a sign of a belly full of poison — simply light, because Ekaterin had insisted that the garden of Vashnoi should also have a night shift.

“Flowers, then,” said Yadwiga slowly, when she understood. At forty she spoke as she had at fifteen: straight to the substance. “Flowers that don’t sting and don’t get taken in.”

“Flowers that stay,” said Ekaterin. “But someone has to mind them, Yadwiga. To know their clearings, to count the hatchings, to let them out in spring. There’s a post for it in the plan. I’m looking for someone who loved them back when everyone else was afraid of them.”

Yadwiga was silent a long while, looking at her hands. Then she raised her head and stated her single condition:

“The first ones I’ll let out on the Mine clearing. By the mounds. Ma always said it’s darkest there.”

VI. The Plan

Ekaterin wrote the plan for twelve days, tore it up twice, and in the end it fitted, as real plans do, on one page.

The fields — outside. The wide ring around the city would be plowed and leased to the valleys: clean earth is plowed, the farmers had been right, and the first harvest of Vashnoi in one hundred five years would be worth more than all the speeches put together.

The city — neither concrete nor silence. The old street grid, which the grass had failed to erase, would be cleared and preserved exactly as it was: the streets became lanes, the squares became meadows, and the plots of the former houses became flower beds. No new buildings save one: an archive pavilion on the market square, where the city’s last census, saved long ago in the district registers, would be laid out name by name — and every bed, every lane, would bear the name of the street and of the families who had lived on it. A garden-city, Ekaterin told the council. A grave one enters without fear, fields that feed, and names read aloud, because people are not remembered by the headcount. People are remembered by name.

The council passed the plan with almost no debate, which in a district council happens once a century. The builders got the monorail and the new city — thirty kilometers to the south, on clean ground, with no one’s bones in the foundations; their young speaker came to Ekaterin afterward and said he took back his sentence about the ghost capital, because a ghost that feeds the countryside and is read out by name is called something else. The veterans got the silence — the kind one can enter: Mara Kosoeva asked for one thing only, a bed on Titmouse Street, and got it. The farmers got the fields.

And Count Miles Vorkosigan received a single sheet for signature and signed it, by the secretary’s testimony, without reading it to the end — which had not happened to him in forty years — because halfway down the page he had stopped, looked a long time at the heading of section three, “Named Beds,” and said: “The rest I know. The rest is her.”

VII. The First Sowing

The ceremony in the spring was short, because Ekaterin had designed it as a garden and not as a parade: no platforms, no salutes, one single row of chairs for the oldest.

In the morning the farmers from the valleys plowed the first furrow of the outer ring — led by the oldest tenant, with a horse-drawn plow, at his own insistence, “because the machine doesn’t remember and the horse does.” Then, on the market square, they opened the archive pavilion, and the first readers — schoolchildren from Hassadar, one per street — set off along the lanes to read the names of the beds aloud, and the reading, Ekaterin had calculated, would take until evening, which was precisely the intention.

At midday the shuttle from the ranch arrived as well: Martya with thermoses, Enrique with three boxes carried like relics, and Yadwiga in a new apron, with the old curtsey and the expression of someone who has waited all her life for precisely this appointment without knowing it existed.

Count Miles Vorkosigan made no speech. He stood beside his wife at the edge of the square, watching his city fill with living people for the first time in one hundred five years, and said only, very quietly, for two:

“All my life I’ve said I own a city that glows in the dark. It’s over. I don’t own it anymore.”

“No,” said Ekaterin, and slipped her hand under his elbow. “Now it glows on its own.”

And at dusk, when the readers reached the last streets and the zone’s first festive evening settled over it, on the Mine clearing, by the small mounds, a forty-year-old woman with five fingers on her right hand opened the first box of the new hatching — carefully, the way one opens something precious — and the flowers of Vashnoi rose into the dark: soft, green, harmless, without cargo and without sentence, and settled over the grass and over the stone with the name MA ROGA, and glowed there all night, because there was no longer anyone to take them in, and no longer any reason.

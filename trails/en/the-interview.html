The Interview

A diplomatic comedy — Barrayar lands on Beta Colony, live

I. The Condition

The invitation to the first state visit in history by a Barrayaran Emperor to Beta Colony arrived with one single peculiar clause, and the clause caused more panic in the Council of Counts than three border incidents combined.

Beta accepted the visit. Beta also accepted the treaty — the package on medical and jump technologies the two diplomacies had been working on for four years. But the Betan parliament would ratify nothing until the Betan public approved of the partner, and the Betan public, the note stated with disarming directness, “does not trust a leader who will not answer questions live. We have a constitutional allergy.”

The condition: one hour on No Airlock — the most-watched current affairs program on the planet. No questions in advance. No forbidden topics. No editing.

“The Emperor,” declared one Count in the Council, the color of a beetroot, “is not a showman!”

“The Emperor,” said Gregor Vorbarra from the throne, with that calm the experienced had learned to recognize as his most dangerous condition, “has received a stipulation no Vorbarra in history has received: to be heard before being judged. Usually the order is the reverse. I accept.” And he added, before the chamber could draw breath for objections: “Lord Auditor Vorkosigan will prepare the visit. He is the only living Barrayaran raised by both civilizations, which makes him our translator. The Council is free to worry in an organized fashion.”

The Council worried in an organized fashion for three weeks. Miles worried in a disorganized one, which was more effective.

II. The Fourteen Forbidden Topics

“I am a translator between civilizations,” Miles explained to Ekaterin as he packed his console for the fifth preparatory session. “The trouble with being a translator between civilizations is that both are native to me, I owe both of them, and each considers the other a failure of upbringing.”

The preparation proceeded on a Barrayaran plan that disintegrated on a Betan schedule. The palace chancery compiled, from the inertia of the trade, a list of fourteen topics “the raising of which is incompatible with the dignity of the Crown”: hereditary power, Komarr, Escobar, the position of women, hermaphrodites, the military budget, the history of the mutations, and seven more, arranged in alphabetical order of horror. Miles sent it to the producers of No Airlock as a “working point of departure.”

The list came back in eleven hours stamped ACCEPTED, with a covering letter: “Thank you for your assistance. We accept the document as the program’s preliminary running order. Your arrangement is excellent.”

“They’re going to ask everything on the list,” Miles told Gregor at the next session, brandishing the letter. “In order. I gave them the agenda. Personally. Thirty-eight years of service, to hand the opposition our own list of vulnerabilities with an alphabetical index.”

“I know,” said Gregor. “I was counting on it when I let you send it.” He turned a page in the folder of Betan constitutional practice he was reading for pleasure, which in itself alarmed Miles. “A list of fourteen forbidden topics is a confession of guilt on fourteen counts, Miles. A list of none is a position. Now they will ask everything, I will answer everything, and no one will be able to say that Barrayar concealed anything at all. You did not betray our vulnerabilities. You published the agenda of our honesty.”

Miles looked at him for ten seconds.

“When exactly,” he asked, “did you learn to do that to me as well?”

“I grew up with you,” said the Emperor. “Something sticks.”

III. Customs

Beta Colony met the imperial delegation with that particular Betan hospitality which does not distinguish an emperor from a tourist, because distinguishing would be ill-mannered.

The customs officer at the shuttleport checked the documents of His Imperial Majesty Gregor Vorbarra with the same expression with which he checked everyone’s, asked whether he was carrying biological materials to declare, wished him “a productive stay,” and called the next in line. The delegation’s protocol attaché developed a mild nervous tic that would accompany him for the rest of the visit.

Next in the queue was a ninety-five-year-old woman with silver hair, arrived on the same liner from Sergyar, in civilian clothes, with a single bag.

“Purpose of visit?” asked the officer.

“Coming home,” said Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan. “I was born two levels beneath this terminal. I’m not part of the delegation, young man, don’t look at the list. I’m local.”

The officer’s console made a noise. He peered at the screen, then at her, then at the screen again, with the growing expression of a man who has found a fossil in the system.

“Ma’am… there’s a flag here. Unresolved proceedings. From—” he counted in his head and disbelieved the result “—over half a century ago. ‘Failure to appear, resisting detention, and—’” his voice thinned slightly “‘—assault upon an attending psychiatrist.’”

“She started it,” said Cordelia brightly. “She was putting sedative in my tea. Does the flag say that? It ought to. I filed an objection. Through a third planet, but I filed it.”

The incident reached the President of Beta in eleven minutes and was resolved by a decree that Betan law faculties afterward examined with relish: full amnesty by limitation, accompanied by an official note that “the Colony withdraws its therapeutic claims upon Madame Vorkosigan, acknowledging the therapy unsuccessful for reasons beyond the patient’s control.” Cordelia asked for a copy, framed.

“Our secret weapon,” Miles reported to Gregor that evening, “went through customs, detonated it, and is now signing autographs for customs officers. I didn’t invite her, incidentally. Nobody invited her. She simply… coincided.”

“I know,” said Gregor. “I wrote to her when we were arriving.”

IV. The Mehta File

Priya Mehta, host of No Airlock, had behind her eleven seasons, three toppled ministers, one resigned president, and a nickname her colleagues pronounced with respect and her guests with a swallow: the Decompression. Her method was public and therefore unavoidable: she did not attack. She asked, listened all the way to the end, and asked the next thing that followed from the answer — a process that looked gentle and worked like a vacuum: people depressurized themselves.

Miles went to the preliminary meeting intending to negotiate the format and came back two hours later with the expression of a man returning from an exercise with live ammunition.

“Did she try the questions on you?” Gregor asked.

“She interviewed me,” said Miles grimly. “We were ostensibly settling the lighting. Twenty minutes later I caught myself explaining why I lied on my entrance examinations at seventeen and what I actually think of the Lord Protocol’s ceremonial. She wasn’t even taking notes. She remembers.” He poured himself a drink. “And another thing. I pulled her file. Familiar surname, I thought, a Betan classic, coincidence. It is not a coincidence. Her grandmother is Dr. Mehta, Gregor. The psychiatrist who after Escobar was assigned to… treat my mother. For my father. The one with the tea.”

There was a pause of historical density.

“So tomorrow,” said Gregor slowly, “the granddaughter of the woman who tried to talk Cordelia Naismith out of Barrayar will try, before an entire planet, to talk Beta Colony out of Barrayar.”

“Symmetry,” said Miles. “I hate symmetries. Ezar loved them, and look where it got the dynasty.”

“It got it,” Gregor observed, “here: to a studio on Beta, live.” He finished his glass and rose. “I’m going to bed. Tomorrow I have a conversation with our family psychiatrist’s granddaughter. I want to be rested: among us depressives, freshness is counted in percentages.”

V. On the Air

The No Airlock studio was round, with no dais, with two identical chairs — the Betan scenographic creed that no one stands above anyone — and with an audience of four hundred citizens chosen by lot, including, by inexorable Betan statistics, three veterans of Escobar of the appropriate age, whose names the production announced in advance, because on Beta surprises on the air were considered manipulation.

“Good evening,” said Priya Mehta to the camera. “Tonight, with no airlock, is a man whose office my civilization abolished three hundred years ago as a design error. Gregor Vorbarra, Emperor of Barrayar, Komarr, and Sergyar. Emperor — first question. You received three planets by inheritance, at the age of five, without a single vote cast. Why should anyone in this room respect that?”

“There is no reason,” said Gregor.

The studio went very quiet. Mehta, for the first time in eleven seasons, waited.

“Hereditary power is not an argument, it is a circumstance,” Gregor continued in his level, unraised voice. “No one owes respect to my circumstances. I did not choose my work — it was handed to me at a funeral. The only thing I have chosen is every morning since: to do it in such a way that people who also did not choose where they were born find it easier than the day before. Respect, if it appears at all, is for the second list. The first is luck, and bad luck at that.”

“‘Bad luck,’” Mehta repeated. “Three planets.”

“Madame Mehta,” said Gregor, “you have spoken with three hundred holders of power. How many of them would tell you on the air that a throne is bad luck?” A pause, precisely measured. “Now consider what it means that the only one who says so is the hereditary one.”

The hour ran on, and Miles, behind the cameras between the protocol attaché with the tic and the Betan producer, watched something happen that his preparation had not foreseen because it was unforeseeable: Gregor was not defeating the Decompression. Gregor was assisting it. Every one of the fourteen questions he opened further himself, deeper than they asked — on Komarr he spoke with the casualty figures unrounded; on the women of Barrayar he said, “we are two generations behind and I know the names of the people delaying us”; on the mutations, “our shame is documented; denying it would be a second shame.” The depressurization for which the program was named proved pointless with a man who entered the studio already open.

At the fifty-first minute Priya Mehta set her question cards on the floor beside her chair — a gesture that in eleven seasons had meant one thing, improvisation — and asked the last one.

VI. The Escobar Question

“Your father,” she said, “commanded the invasion of Escobar. In this room sit three people who fought in that war. Millions watching have parents who fought in it. What do you say to the Betan veterans of Escobar, Emperor?”

“The first thing I will say to them,” said Gregor, “is that the woman who raised me is one of them.”

He let the sentence travel all the way — to the room, to the cameras, to the three faces of the appropriate age in the third row.

“Cordelia Naismith commanded a Betan ship in that war, on your side, against my father’s fleet. Then her life took the strangest turn in modern history and she became the woman who stood by my bed when I had nightmares, and who explained to me — at seven years old, with Betan directness, without one word spared — what my father had done and why it must never happen again. Everything I know about conscience, I know in Betan, Madame Mehta. With her accent.” He looked at the audience. “I cannot give your veterans anything back. I can only tell them that the raising of the next Barrayaran Emperor was entrusted to a comrade-in-arms of theirs — and that she took no prisoners.”

Priya Mehta glanced at the director’s booth, then at the audience, and played the card she had been holding since the start of the season:

“Then let us ask her whether she agrees with that assessment.” She turned toward the third row. “Madame Vorkosigan. The chair is yours, if you wish it.”

Four hundred people and four cameras turned toward the silver-haired woman in civilian clothes, seated between a retired dome engineer and a student of xenobiology. Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan rose unhurriedly, came down to the round stage, settled into the offered chair with the ease of someone who has sat in hotter seats, and said:

“Mind the tea, Madame Mehta. Family joke. Your grandmother will understand it — do tell her the treatment didn’t take, but the patient bears no grudge: the diagnosis of ‘dangerously in love’ proved accurate; only the prognosis was pessimistic.” She waited for the room’s laughter to pass. “You asked whether I agree with the assessment. Not entirely. He left out the most important part. I did not choose Barrayar over Beta, children. I am the double agent of common sense: for sixty years I have been smuggling Betan ideas into Barrayaran heads and Barrayaran seriousness into Betan conversations, and I intend to die at my post. That treaty your parliament is afraid to ratify — that is simply my latest consignment. Sign for it on delivery.”

VII. The Ratings

No Airlock with Emperor Gregor Vorbarra became the most-watched broadcast in the channel’s history, surpassing — by inexorable Betan statistics — even the emergency coverage of the Silica dome failure, “which,” the producer noted in an interview, “was a disaster with fatalities, so the comparison is improper, but we are making it with pride.”

The Betan press produced a term that within a week entered the dictionaries of eight planets: Barrayaran honesty — a style of public answer in which the speaker supplies more incriminating information than was requested, thereby rendering the attack logistically impossible. Parliament ratified the treaty by a majority that on Beta was otherwise assembled only for resolutions against noise. And the closing shot of the program — the Emperor, the Decompression, and a ninety-five-year-old woman, all three laughing at a remark the microphones did not catch — circled the networks of the Union under a caption neither side disputed: “Negotiations concluded. The viewers won.”

At the shuttleport, on departure, Priya Mehta caught up with the delegation with a professionally impenetrable face and handed Cordelia a card.

“One interview,” she said. “Whenever you wish. The format is yours, the topics are yours, the length is yours. I have never offered that to anyone.”

“Your grandmother once offered me the same,” said Cordelia, pocketing the card. “With sedative in the tea. Come to Sergyar without tea, girl, and I’ll consider it. I have things to tell that your courage will have to train for.”

In the shuttle, already in the jump couch, Miles turned to his Emperor:

“Summary for the record. I sent the opposition our list of vulnerabilities, you executed it point by point before four hundred million viewers, my mother was amnestied for assaulting a psychiatrist, and as a result we have a ratified treaty and a new idiom in eight dictionaries. I don’t know how to file this, Gregor. We have no column for it.”

“File it,” said the Emperor, closing his eyes for the jump, “under successful translations.”

Second Voice

An adventure of two empires and one singer — with an old agent and one uncancelled deal

I. The Invitation

Three years after dock eleven-lower, Volna Kri was singing at the Escobaran opera — third cast, small roles, her own name on the bill — and it was the finest time of her life, because it was the first that belonged to her.

Her life had a schedule, which she adored with all the passion of a person whose schedule had once been a column in someone else’s catalogue: rehearsals in the morning, lessons in the afternoon — she who could sing four and a half octaves in her sleep took lessons from an old baritone, because no one had ever taught her to sing with other people, only instead of them — and evenings on which she sometimes went onstage for eight bars, and the eight bars were hers.

The invitation arrived on a Tuesday, on a sheet that was not paper, in a calligraphy that was not a font: every letter drawn by hand, with a brush, by someone who had studied the art for forty years. The Festival of Ten Thousand Voices, at the foot of the Celestial Garden, Eta Ceta, invited “the performer Volna Kri, independent,” to present “vocal art of the outer worlds” in the program of the Feast of the Long Autumn.

Cetaganda did not invite outside performers. Cetaganda, so far as Volna knew from her former owner’s catalogues, considered outer-world vocal art a charming ethnography. And an invitation in hand calligraphy signified a rank that would have made her Escobaran colleagues weep.

She read it a long time. Then she set it on the table beside the mirror and said to the empty dressing room, very quietly:

“Ah.”

Because twenty-six years in the column marked equipment, acoustic teach a person one thing above all others: when the gift is too beautiful, read the wrapping.

Captain Mirko Zarov arrived on Thursday — of course on Thursday — leaned against the dressing-room frame in the manner in which he had once leaned against a cargo lock, and said without preamble:

“Don’t sign it.”

“Hello to you too,” said Volna. “Still guarding me, Captain? I thought the deal was concluded.”

“A deal is a deal,” said Zarov. “And deals, as I learned from a certain singer, have fine print. I came to read you yours.”

II. The Fine Print

Zarov’s account took twenty minutes and one napkin, on which he sketched the diagram with an eyebrow pencil, there being nothing else to hand.

House Vale had lost article seven, but not the documentation. The “Coda” series — genomic maps, vocal apparatus, auditory range from eight hertz to seventy kilohertz, absolute acoustic memory — sat in the House’s archives like an unsold catalogue. And four months ago the House had sold it: quietly, in pieces, through three intermediaries, to a buyer on Eta Ceta. Ghem-lord Taren, collector of “biological instruments,” a man with eight names along the chain and not one true one in the contract.

“Do you know what he collects?” Zarov drew a cage on the napkin. “Birds. Two hundred thirty-one songbirds from eighty worlds, each designed by someone for someone. He keeps them in a hall with perfect acoustics and plays them recordings of their own voices, so they sing against themselves. He says that way you can hear the design.” He set down the pencil. “That is the man who bought your blueprints, Volna.”

“Only the documentation is half a commodity,” said Volna slowly. She knew this lesson from its inner side. “An article of the Coda series is tuned over years. The voice isn’t in the genomic maps.”

“The voice is in you: twenty-nine years of epigenetics, training, habits of the vocal apparatus. Which is why the contract carries a clause on ‘demonstration of a matured article in an operational environment.’ Jacksonian law: the demonstration completes the sale.” He tapped the napkin with the pencil. “The Festival is the demonstration. The invitation asks you for the series’ certified program — the one from Vale’s catalogue, note for note. Sing it on a stage with full-spectrum recording and Taren receives the final component of the goods, Vale receives the balance of the money, and you receive the honor of having sold yourself, in hand calligraphy.”

Volna looked at the napkin for a long moment.

“And if I refuse?”

“Then Vale is in default to a buyer who does not forgive, and the only way for the House to deliver the component becomes… more old-fashioned.” Zarov did not elaborate. It was not necessary. “I have a disappearance ready. New name, new sector, six hours’ notice.”

“I ran once,” said Volna Kri. “I liked where. I didn’t like how.” She picked up the invitation and turned it between her fingers, beautiful and poisonous. “No, Captain. I’ll accept. I’ll go to Eta Ceta and sing them something.” She smiled, and in the smile there showed for a moment that girl from the bar called Low Orbit who had torn a sound curtain apart in the harmonics. “After all — who sets the program?”

III. The Other Side of the Stage

Ghem-General Dag Benin, chief of Imperial internal security, learned of the full-spectrum recording array in the Festival hall eleven days before the opening, during a routine inspection that had long since ceased to be routine — since the Harvest affair his service examined every transaction in outer-world biogenetics with the patience of a man once scalded.

The array was mounted beneath the stage, disguised as acoustic calibration, paid through three foundations, and set to a range that had nothing to do with music: eight hertz to seventy kilohertz, full capture, storage density of the sort spent on intelligence data, not concerts.

His analyst — a young ghem-major with the patience of an archaeologist — assembled the chain in a night and laid it out on Benin’s desk like the genealogy of a crime: ghem-lord Taren; three intermediaries; House Vale; the Coda series. An outer-world bioengineering development on a human base — vocal apparatus, auditory tract, cortical acoustic memory — bought by a ghem in pieces, around the Star Crèche’s monopoly on everything that touches the human thread.

“The buyer’s file?” Benin asked.

“A collector of his grandfather’s school.” The analyst opened a second folder. “The hall of birds is documented: two hundred thirty-one, all acquired ‘as objects of art,’ which is formally accurate, because the sellers were careful to sell the cage and let the bird go along ‘as an appurtenance.’” The major paused with relish. “With the singer the design is evidently identical: one buys the documentation, and the performer comes as an appurtenance to her own recording.”

Benin read the file twice and caught himself feeling something between professional alarm and simple human boredom. The Empire did not learn. The Autumn Council had invested human reason in chitin; ghem-lord Taren was investing a fortune in a foreign larynx. The same crime each time, dressed up now as doctrine, now as connoisseurship: the conviction that the human thread is a material.

He could have stopped the concert with a single signature. Instead he ordered tea, let it cool exactly as long as required, and wrote two instructions. The first — surveillance of the array, no intervention: an undemonstrated sale is a rumor, a demonstrated one is material evidence. The second was an invitation, dispatched through protocol channels to the Escobaran delegation: the Empire would be pleased to welcome to the boxes the attaché for cultural affairs, Mr. Verek.

“If we are going to watch the same trap,” Benin told his analyst, “let the two sides of the trap at least know each other by sight.”

IV. The Lotus Hall

Eta Ceta received Volna Kri with a courtesy calibrated to the micron: an apartment with a garden, an interpreter with the manners of porcelain, and a master of ceremonies — ghem-lord Nial, a man whose face paint resembled a page of score — who mentioned in his third sentence, in passing, how pleased the Festival was that her program would include “the classic certified repertoire of the celebrated series, in its authentic form.”

“In the most authentic,” said Volna, and smiled smile number three from the catalogue, which meant the article confirms.

The hall took her breath away at the first rehearsal. Built three hundred years ago in the shape of an opening lotus and tuned so that a whisper from the stage reached the last row as a whisper and not as a voice — the Cetagandans considered amplification barbarism, and they were right, which was the most irritating thing about them. Volna stood at the center, sang a single test phrase from the certified repertoire — technically flawless, level, by catalogue — and heard the hall return it: precise, obedient, dead.

Then, ostensibly to check the acoustics, she released two notes of her own. Lead voice and second, answering, one against the other — the double vocal apparatus working as a conversation.

The lotus woke. Three centuries of acoustic architecture took up the two voices, separated them, turned them along the petals of the ceiling and returned them interwoven, and Volna stood in the middle of her own sound understanding, with a clarity that brought her near tears, that this hall had been built three centuries ago for exactly what she intended to do in it — only none of its builders had known.

At night, in the apartment with the garden, she finished the composition. She worked in the dark, without an instrument, off her absolute acoustic memory — the one thing from the catalogue she was grateful for — and built it bar by bar: a theme written by other hands and an answer raised by her own; article and human, quarrel, contest, and a final unison that she rewrote eleven times, because the unison between what they made of you and what you have become is the hardest interval in music.

She called it Second Voice. On the third night, in the garden, she sang it through once, whispering, to an audience of pond fish.

At that same hour, four levels beneath the lotus stage, technicians of “acoustic calibration” were running final tests on an array that was waiting for an entirely different program.

V. Ten Thousand Voices

On the evening of the Feast of the Long Autumn the lotus hall held three thousand people and more face paint than the entire theatrical sector of Escobar. In the third box on the left sat a man with eight names. In the fifth on the right sat a man with the alias Verek and the expression of a cultural attaché such as no culture had ever seen. And in the imperial box, half-hidden, sat a woman in white of whom it was whispered that she had lately been visiting the most unexpected places, ever since she had laughed aloud in a barbarian garden.

The announcer named the program as agreed with the organizers: “certified repertoire of the Coda series, article seven” — a formulation that for ninety-nine percent of the hall was exotica and for three boxes was a signature on a contract.

Volna Kri came out in clothing without a single Jacksonian seam, stood at the center of the lotus, and waited for the full silence the hall had been built for. Then she said, aloud, unamplified, and three centuries of architecture carried the words to the last row:

“The program has changed. Tonight you will hear a composition by Volna Kri, performed for the first time. It is called Second Voice.” She found the third box on the left with her eyes and bowed to it personally, courteously, as to an admirer. “Catalogue repertoire, honored sirs, is performed by articles. I am here by an invitation addressed to a performer. Independent.”

In the third box someone leaned sharply toward a comm. In the fifth box the cultural attaché Verek crossed his legs and settled in more comfortably, like a man at the beginning of a long-awaited spectacle.

And Volna sang.

She sang her own music for forty minutes — written at night in Escobaran dressing rooms and finished in a garden on Eta Ceta, upon the skeleton of everything the double vocal apparatus could do and that no catalogue had ever asked of it: two voices at once, lead and second, theme and answer, article and human, conversing with each other, quarrelling, reconciling in a final unison after which the hall with three hundred years of acoustic culture fell into a silence of a quality Volna had never heard — the silence of an audience refusing to spoil the ending by breathing.

Beneath the stage the full-spectrum array recorded every frequency up to the seventieth kilohertz. Not one of them corresponded to the certified repertoire. No demonstration of an article took place — what took place was the premiere of an author, which in the legal sense is precisely the opposite.

VI. The Interval

The interval at the Festival of Ten Thousand Voices lasted, by tradition, twenty-nine minutes. Taren needed the first four to understand, and Internal Security the next six to knock.

Into the third box on the left came four men in the service’s colors, with a warrant whose subject was described in a sentence polished by the best jurists of the Celestial Garden: “acquisition of a work bearing the human thread from an unlicensed subject.” Ghem-lord Taren heard out the text with a face on which, for the first time in forty years, the paint was doing all the work by itself, and made the only move available: he asked whether he might see out the concert. He was told, with impeccable courtesy, that the concert would be seen out without him, and that the recording — he flinched at the word — would be furnished to him in the capacity of material evidence number one: a full-spectrum array installed by the buyer, at the buyer’s expense, which had captured in perfect quality the goods on sale refusing to be goods.

“The cleanest case of my career,” Benin said later. “The accused financed the expert examination in advance.”

In the corridor behind the boxes, in the course of the interval, an unminuted meeting also took place. The ghem-general in the face paint of internal security and the cultural attaché with the impossible expression passed each other, stopped, and looked at one another with the mutual recognition of two trades that are the same trade, worn by two empires.

“Mr. Verek,” said Benin. “I trust you are enjoying the program.”

“Extraordinarily,” said Zarov. “Particularly the change to it. I hear it was submitted at the last moment.”

“Changes to the program,” said Benin, “are the performer’s reserved right. We entered it in practice recently. On a specific occasion.” He paused, precisely as long as required. “Do convey to Escobar that their singer has excellent… advisers on fine print.”

“Escobar,” said Zarov, “will deny ever having heard of fine print. But I’ll convey it.”

The two bowed to each other by half a centimeter more than protocol required and went their separate ways, each to his own box, pleased in the manner of professionals when the trap has closed upon the correct party.

VII. Encore

Volna Kri survived the reception afterward with a professional smile and one memorable encounter: the woman in white approached, without escort, looked at her with eyes in which twelve centuries of genetics had left nothing to chance, and said:

“The second voice. The one that answers. That one was not designed, was it?”

“No, haut,” said Volna. “That one I raised. It was I who answered what was designed.”

“Then the work has completed itself,” said haut Pel Navar, as though entering a formula in a register. “We had a similar case recently. It is becoming a habit with them.” And at the corner of the perfect mouth something passed that in another person would have been a smile. “Sing long, performer. The Empire has collected beauty for a thousand years and is only now learning that the finest specimens are not owned.”

“And the birds, haut?” Volna asked, before prudence could catch up with her. “Two hundred thirty-one, in a hall with perfect acoustics.”

Haut Pel Navar regarded her for one long moment.

“An informed performer,” she said. “As of tomorrow the birds are the Crèche’s concern. The hall, I imagine, will stand empty.” A pause. “Unless someone is found to sing in it. I shall consider it. The Empire owes art one acoustic.”

At the exit to the shuttles the cultural attaché Verek was waiting for her, hands in his pockets, wearing at last an expression that was his own.

“House Vale is in default to a man who is already under arrest,” said Zarov. “The deal collapses at both ends. Clean.” He paused. “You knew Benin was watching, didn’t you. That’s why you came. Not for the honor of the invitation — for the witness with the warrant.”

“I knew someone was watching,” said Volna. “On Cetaganda someone always is. I was only counting on the watcher being one of the sort who read the fine print.” She offered him her hand, Escobaran fashion. “It appears I need one of those at every stage. You were the first, Captain. Thank you for coming to read me mine.”

Zarov shook her hand.

“A deal is a deal,” he said.

In the shuttle, without audience, without arrays and without boxes, Volna Kri leaned against the viewport, watched the lotus of the hall dwindle, and sang herself one last encore: short, quiet, with harmonics below eighty hertz and above twelve kilohertz — where the curtains run — so that in the whole shuttle it was heard only by her own ears, designed once for someone else’s catalogue and serving now only her.

And in her new contracts, from the following season onward, there stood one additional clause, non-negotiable, on the first page, before the fee:

The program is set by the singer.

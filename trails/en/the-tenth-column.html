The Tenth Column

An adventure of the Emperor and his Auditor — with one empty sarcophagus and one column more

I. Three in the Morning

The summons found Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan in bed, dozing over a report on customs tariffs, and its wording — “at the Auditor’s discretion, immediately” — was an imperial oxymoron Miles had been translating without error for forty years: come at once and pretend you had a choice.

So at ten past three in the morning he entered the Imperial study with the Imperial Auditor’s mantle thrown over a dressing gown — a combination Gregor’s secretary registered with a single lift of one eyebrow worth an entire disciplinary report — and found the Emperor standing at the window with a glass of whisky in his hand.

The whisky was the bad sign. Gregor Vorbarra drank whisky standing, in the dark, on average once every five years, and each of those occasions had a name in the textbooks.

“The restoration of the old wing,” said Gregor without preamble. “They were shoring the foundations beneath the chapel. Behind a load-bearing wall which by every plan for three centuries is solid, there is a cavity. In the cavity there is a crypt. In the crypt there is a sarcophagus.”

“The Imperial crypt is under the new wing,” said Miles. “And it is inventoried. Nine sarcophagi, by register.”

“Precisely why you’re drinking what I’m drinking.” Gregor nodded at the second glass, poured and waiting, which meant the conversation had been planned for a long time — that is, for a whole forty minutes. “A tenth sarcophagus, Miles. Walled in, no entrance, no corridor — the wall was built over it. And it is empty. Nearly.”

He handed over the plate with the holoimages. Miles paged through them: a narrow crypt, a single sarcophagus of black granite, no arms, no dates. On the lid — a bronze plate with an inscription, deeply carved, in a hand Miles had seen on documents in the family archive often enough to feel his fingers go cold.

Here lies the lawful heir. Let him never become Emperor.

“Ezar’s hand,” said Miles.

“My grandfather’s hand,” said Gregor. “Do you know how many conspiracy theories circulate about the Vorbarras and empty graves?”

“All of them,” said Miles. “The answer is always ‘all of them.’” He reached the last image: the interior of the sarcophagus. Not entirely empty. A small hermetic capsule of the type in which medical archives keep tissue. And a metal tag with a serial number, of the kind carried by— Miles enlarged it. “Gregor. That’s a registration plate from a uterine replicator. Third generation. Manufactured more than half a century ago.”

“I know,” said the Emperor, and drank. “The laboratory has been working since midnight. The result came in an hour ago. Hence the drinking.”

II. The Grave of a Claim

The result fitted on one sheet and weighed as much as a dynasty.

The tissue in the capsule contained a complete human genome matching to the last marker the archival genome of Crown Prince Serg Vorbarra — Gregor’s father, killed at Escobar fifty-six years ago — and carried the standard marker of replicator cloning of that era: not a child of Serg, but a copy of him. Plus a date of decanting, pedantically engraved on the tag to Imperial laboratory standard: seven months before Escobar.

“A clone of my father,” said Gregor levelly. “Commissioned, gestated, and decanted alive while my father was still alive. And then my grandfather walled up his empty grave beneath the chapel and built a wall over it.” He set down the glass. “Explain this to me, Auditor. Because I have been explaining it to myself all night and my explanations are not fit for the record.”

Miles looked at the inscription for a long time. Ezar’s hand. The Emperor who — the family had it from Cordelia, word for word, from a deathbed — had seen what was growing in his son, had weighed him against a planet, and had sent the Escobar war to finish what a father’s hand could not. And seven months earlier that same Emperor had commissioned a copy.

“Insurance,” said Miles slowly. “You were four, Gregor, with uncertain health and a mother in a court full of knives. Ezar was dying and knew it. If he had lost you as well, the dynasty ended — or passed through Serg, which Ezar had already resolved not to allow. So he ordered a… reserve. A blank sheet with the same blood.” He tapped the holoimage of the inscription. “And then you lived, Vordarian was still in the future, and Ezar was left with a baby in a replicator that was simultaneously the most lawful heir by blood — a copy of the Crown Prince, the senior line — and the last creature in the universe he would let near the throne. Not because of the baby. Because of the original.”

“And instead of resolving it in the old way—”

“He buried the claim,” said Miles. “Literally. A sarcophagus for the right, not for the man: the tissue sample and the tag inside, the inscription on top, a wall in front. A legal burial, executed in granite. And the man—” Miles paged back, to the list of items from the cavity. One single other object: a bill of lading from a civilian liner, fifty-six years old, for “household baggage, one item,” route Vorbarr Sultana–Escobar, no names, paid in cash, with a customs exemption signed by nobody. “The man was carried out. And I can see whose work it is. No signature, no trail, a document of perfect tedium. That is Captain Negri’s hand, Gregor. The absence of a signature is his signature.”

“Escobar,” said Gregor. And then, very quietly: “My grandfather sent my father’s copy to live on the planet where he sent my father to die.”

“Symmetry,” said Miles. “Ezar thought in symmetries.” He hesitated, then said it, because someone had to: “That inscription is not for the clone, Gregor. That inscription is the epitaph Ezar could not carve on his son’s grave.”

III. The Leak

Three days later the news leaked, and it leaked with holoimages.

The investigation cost Miles one afternoon: the restorer of the old wing, a man with suddenly expensive habits and a nose broken in a “fall from scaffolding” two days before the leak — a fall which had evidently taught him nothing — had sold the images to a broker. The broker, at two removes, proved to be paid by the circle around Count Vormoncrief: an alliance of old lines for whom “conservative” was by now too progressive a word.

In the Council of Counts there broke out that particular Barrayaran fire which burns without flame, with only smoke and procedural motions. Count Vorhalas — eighty years old, straight as statute and hereditarily disinclined to forgive the Vorkosigans anything whatever — moved for “a genetic audit of the dynastic line in the interest of the Crown”: a formulation polished to sound like loyalty and cut like a knife. For if Ezar had made a clone once, who could vouch for the rest of the pedigree? And behind Vorhalas, who at least believed in his own stone integrity, stirred the circle of Vormoncrief, which believed in nothing but arithmetic: if the senior line had a living bearer, he was either a weapon, or merchandise, or a grave — preferably in that order.

“And the best part,” said Miles to Ekaterin that evening, over coffee that had long since stopped working, “is that half the chamber watched me do the arithmetic. Me. Because obviously anyone of my height and my lineage dreams of a throne designed for people with a whole spine. Forty years of service, and they still find it easier to believe in my ambition than in my loyalty.”

“They don’t find it easier,” said Ekaterin. “They find it more convenient. Ambition makes you theirs, to scale.” She refilled his cup. “Who leaves for Escobar first — you or the news?”

“I do,” said Miles. “Tomorrow, with an audit of the consular accounts for cover. The departure isn’t the problem. The problem is that this morning the Emperor asked to see my itinerary. With that look.”

“Which look?”

“The look of a man who remembers that there is in the ImpSec files an inspector of grain deliveries with unused leave.”

IV. The Inspector and the Antiquarian

The argument ran across two time zones and Miles lost it while still in orbit, because it was an argument with a man who had been accustomed since the age of five to losing everything except arguments.

“You are the Emperor,” said Miles for the fourth time, in the cabin of the civilian liner, watching Gregor Vorbarra hang in the closet the wardrobe of Greg Bleakman, inspector: three changes of clothing in the color of conscientious tedium. “Your face is on every coin in real space.”

“On the coins I am wearing a crown,” said Gregor with that calm which was more dangerous than any anger. “Without a crown I am a tall man with a tired look, and Escobar imports those wholesale. Tested, Miles. Three times now.”

“That is exactly what worries me. Inspector Bleakman is accumulating alarming seniority.” Miles sat down. “Tell me the real reason. Not the one for the record — I know that one: the leak came from the court, therefore the court is a sieve, therefore the fewer who know where you are, the more alive the subject stays. It’s true and it’s conveniently true. The real one.”

Gregor closed the closet. Stood a moment with his hand on the door.

“The tissue in the capsule,” he said, “is genetically my father. Which makes any child of his, if he has children—” He did not finish the sum aloud. It was not necessary; both of them had done it in the first minute over the laboratory sheet. “I am sixty-one, Miles. I have a wife I love, a son who will be better than I am, daughters, an empire, everything. And one single account that has never balanced: my family is everything after the throne. Everything alongside the throne. Nothing before it, nothing outside it. And now, on one planet, there lives — perhaps — a person with my father’s face who has never heard the word Vorbarra spoken toward him.” He turned. “I will not interfere. I will not speak. I only want to see. Emperors do not inherit families, Miles. They inherit crypts. Allow me three days of being one of the other people.”

Miles looked at him for a long moment. Then he sighed and took out his console.

“Greg Bleakman and his cousin Miles Naismith, antiquarian,” he said. “We deal in old books. That much, at least, I shall enjoy playing.”

V. The Woman in the Library

Negri’s trail proved exactly as good as Miles had feared: perfect. For fifty-six years it had lain cold as professional honor — no documents, no names, no end. What warmed it was older than any intelligence service: bookkeeping.

The bill of lading for “household baggage” had an insured value. The insurance had an assessor. The assessor, long dead, had an archive, sold to a historical society. And in the archive, among a thousand tedious lines — an inventory: “crate, children’s effects; blanket, wool, handmade, mountain stitch.” Mountain stitch. Dendarii. Someone — Negri? a nurse? Ezar himself, in his single inexplicable line? — had sent the baby into exile with a blanket from the Vorkosigans’ own mountains.

From there it was patience and registries: a foundling of the appropriate week, adopted by a childless bookbinder’s family in the city of Valdez; a boy named Sevan Arda, raised over a bindery; apprentice, master, at last a restorer of rare books with a quiet reputation in three sectors. Married late. One daughter. Died six years ago, at fifty, of the most banal thing in the world — his heart — without one day of knowing what he carried.

And the daughter worked twelve minutes’ walk from their hotel, in the conservation department of the Central Library of Valdez, and her name was Alia Arda, and she was twenty-six.

They saw her on the third day, from the reading room, through the glass wall of the restoration hall: a young woman in an apron and cotton gloves, bent over an opened folio with that complete, happy concentration which cannot be acted. Dark hair, carelessly pinned. Narrow, focused brows. And when she raised her head toward the light to examine a leaf against the lamp, Miles heard the man beside him — a tall man with a tired look, an inspector of grain deliveries — stop breathing for three full seconds.

“She has my father’s eyes,” said Gregor very quietly. “From the portraits. From my one memory.” A pause in which something rearranged itself permanently. “Miles. She is genetically my sister.”

“Yes,” said Miles. “And she is a book restorer in the city of Valdez, her name is Alia, her colleagues are fond of her, she is waiting on approval for a conservation fellowship, and she hates politics — that last from three independent sources.” He looked at his friend sidelong, carefully. “She has the right never to learn one word of the previous sentence, Gregor. She has the right to the thing neither of us ever had: not to be what we are.”

VI. The Midnight Quarrel

They quarrelled at midnight, in the hotel room, in whispers — the worst kind of quarrel.

“One conversation,” said Gregor. “A reader and a restorer. Half an hour over some book. Nothing more, never again.”

“You’re lying to yourself,” said Miles. “I know, because I have had a lifelong mastery of that particular lie. Half an hour will not be enough; you will want another, then a letter, then an ‘anonymous patron,’ and then one day she will assemble the facts — she is clever, remember? — and understand. And in the second she understands, her life ends and her biography begins. You cannot give her the truth, Gregor, without taking her life. The two do not come apart.”

“You don’t know what it is.” Gregor’s voice was level and for that reason terrible. “A cage from the age of five. Every face around you either a guard, or a threat, or an account. Knowing that anyone you love becomes a target by the very act of being loved. My father is a monster in the textbooks, my mother is a photograph, my grandfather is an inscription on an empty grave. And suddenly — a sister, Miles. Alive. With an apron and gloves. The first person of my blood who is neither above me, nor below me, nor after me on the throne. Simply… mine. And you tell me to look through a pane of glass and walk away.”

“I tell you,” said Miles, “that love which cannot keep silent is not love but need. I learned that the hard way and late.” He gestured at the window, the city, the lit facade of the library in the distance. “She is free, Gregor. The only person of Ezar’s blood in the entire universe who is free. Your grandfather walled up a crypt to achieve it. Negri erased himself to achieve it. A bookbinder raised her over a workshop without knowing what he was keeping. Four dead men built that freedom for her, and you want to pull it down for half an hour over a book.” Miles dropped his voice all the way. “I am your Auditor. I audit. The account does not balance.”

A long silence. Then Gregor sat down on the bed, suddenly not an Emperor but only a tired man of sixty-one, and said:

“When did you become the wiser of the two of us?”

“Never,” said Miles. “I have simply already committed this particular idiocy, and you have not. Division of labor.”

VII. The Tenth Column

Both their plans went stale the following morning, when Miles, on a routine walk past the library, counted too many boring men.

Four. Deployed by the textbook: café, tram stop, bench, and a parked van of “climate maintenance” with an engine nobody was maintaining. Middle-grade professionals, hired at two removes at least — which meant the Vormoncrief circle had reached Alia along its own line: the restorer with the nose had evidently sold not only pictures but also the direction of Miles’s archival requests. Alive or controlled, the senior line was merchandise. Dead, it was a closed risk. And the four were waiting for the end of the working day.

“Options,” said Miles to Gregor, in the reading room, over an open atlas, in the voice of an antiquarian discussing a binding. “One: we call the local police — and her name enters a report that is read tomorrow in three capitals. Two: I call ImpSec — and we officially admit what we are protecting. Three: we call no one, and this evening they take her from the tram stop.”

“Four?” asked Gregor, because he knew him.

“Four is the tenth column,” said Miles. “Every operational plan counts the opponent’s options. These four have counted them: nine outcomes, nine responses, nine variants, all covered. You win by giving them a tenth — which is not a variant but a mirror. You don’t defeat them. You make them cancel their own task.”

The afternoon passed in preparations, each innocent by itself. Miles, a charming antiquarian, secured a meeting with the head of conservation to “assess a donation” — forty minutes in which he learned the layout of the restoration wing to the centimeter and left the library a gift of a crate of old books, delivered by a courier firm whose van remained parked at the service entrance (“awaiting the handover protocol”). Two retired sergeants of the Valdez police, hired through a security agency as “inventory clerks,” sat down to take inventory in the two reading rooms with a bearing legible at fifty meters. And at 1610, by an impossible coincidence, the municipal system sent all subscribers in the district a notice of “preventive maintenance on the gas network” with a request to ventilate — and the library, by its regulations, began an orderly evacuation: calm, cheerful, over the public address system, staff leading.

At 1615 the watcher in the café reported to his team — Miles did not hear the report, but he watched it being born — that the subject was leaving two hours early, in a dense group of colleagues; that since morning the building had contained at least two people with military bearing; that an unloaded van of unclear status had been standing at the service entrance for three hours; and that the whole opera smelled of somebody else’s operation, begun before theirs.

And at 1617, on their operational frequency — which Miles had found in four hours, because middle-grade mercenaries economize on cryptography — there arrived a short unsigned message, composed by a man who had spent forty-one years signing orders and knew how the real ones sound: Operation compromised by a second party. Subject under foreign surveillance. Abort and withdraw by the reserve route. Payment stands.

The last sentence was Gregor’s. “‘Payment stands,’” he had said while dictating it, “is the difference between an order you obey and an order you are glad of.”

At 1622 the “climate maintenance” van pulled away. By evening all four boring men had left Valdez by four different conveyances. And Alia Arda went home with her colleagues, complained about gas inspections, and lay down to read, without learning — not then, and not ever — that a battle had been fought and won around her that afternoon, and that the evacuation by regulation had been the one time in her life an emperor held the door for her.

VIII. Ezar’s Answer

They returned to Barrayar separately and with different faces: Inspector Bleakman rested, the antiquarian Naismith with a crate of Escobaran books, and an ImpSec courier who had left a day earlier with a report that set General Duv Galeni running three quiet investigations at once. The Vormoncrief circle learned what an audit means on Barrayar: no arrests, no scandal — only one Imperial Auditor (not Miles; Miles was “on leave”) installed in the Count’s district accounts with the patience of a geologist. Nothing kills a conspiracy more surely than an audit of its funding.

With Vorhalas, Gregor proceeded otherwise. He invited the old Count — alone, without escort, without protocol — into the old wing, to the opened crypt, and left him ten minutes alone with the black sarcophagus and the bronze inscription. No one ever learned what Vorhalas thought during those ten minutes before the handwriting of the Emperor he had served as a young man. But when he came out, he withdrew the motion for a genetic audit with seven words that entered the Council’s minutes and the textbooks of political dignity: “The late Emperor has answered my question.”

The palace’s official statement was written by Miles and was his finest work in the genre, because it consisted almost entirely of truth: during restoration a sealed crypt had been discovered containing a cenotaph — an empty sarcophagus dedicated by Emperor Ezar to the memory of Crown Prince Serg, whose body, as every Barrayaran schoolchild knows, was never returned from Escobar. A grieving father, a secret monument, walled up out of sorrow. The inscription — “a private dynastic text” — remained under seal out of respect for the dead. The Imperium accepted the story instantly and gladly, because it explained everything, moved everyone, and threatened no one; the journalists got a tragedy, the historians a footnote, the tourists a legend. And its most useful quality was simple: one cannot conspire against the empty grave of a dead prince.

The genetic sheet, the replicator tag, and Negri’s bill of lading went into a single sealed envelope with an Auditor’s seal and an endorsement Miles formulated personally: “To be opened only by the reigning Emperor, only in a matter touching the succession, only after reading the enclosed note.” The note was three sentences long and began with First read to the end, then decide — because Miles knew heirs.

IX. The Sealed Thing

“You might have let me choose,” said Gregor.

They were sitting in the study, the evening after the statement, with a glass each of the same whisky, sitting this time, which on Miles’s scale meant civilization had retreated about a meter from the edge.

“You chose,” said Miles. “In Valdez, at 1617, when instead of stepping through a pane of glass you dictated a withdrawal order in a voice that made me want to come to attention. I merely narrowed the options to the correct ones. That is my job — the word says so: an auditor, one who counts.” He turned his glass. “Besides, I have deprived you of nothing. The envelope exists. The truth is one seal away. I have simply put one night’s reflection and three sentences between you and it. That is what a crown weighs on paper.”

“And if my son opens it one day?”

“Then he will read the note first,” said Miles. “It tells him that on Escobar there lives a free person whose freedom was built by Ezar, by Negri, by a bookbinder, and by his father, and that every dynasty rests not on those who sit on the throne but on those who were left not to sit. If Xav is the son I know, he will seal the envelope again and pour a whisky.” Miles shrugged. “If he isn’t — three sentences would not have stopped him anyway.”

Gregor laughed — for the first time in three weeks, briefly and truly. Then he rose, went to the bookcase behind the desk — the private one, the small one, with the things that did not belong to the Imperium — and settled on the shelf a thin old volume in a freshly restored binding: Barrayaran poetry from a century ago, bought for a modest sum from the conservation department of an Escobaran library, restored, said the small label on the back, by restorer A. Arda.

“You are a dreadful psychologist, Miles,” said the Emperor, his back to him, aligning the book with the others.

“But I am a splendid Auditor,” said Miles. “My accounts balance. Even when I weep over them.”

X. The Column

Six months later Alia Arda of the city of Valdez received a letter informing her that her application for the fellowship in rare-book conservation had been approved — full, three-year, at the finest institute on Beta Colony, funded by a small private foundation no one in the trade had ever heard of, called the Tenth Column.

In the envelope, besides the contract, there was an unsigned card, handwritten, in a hand at once commanding and tired:

Keepers of books outlive emperors — ask the archives whose names they remember. If nonetheless you should one day wish to change trades: a throne weighs roughly as much as an incunabulum. It is merely less convenient to restore, and must never, under any circumstances, be leafed through.

Alia read it twice, decided that the patrons of small foundations are charming eccentrics, and glued the card to the inside of her suitcase, for luck.

The suitcase left for Beta Colony via the Escobar nexus, on a liner where no one looked at her longer than politeness required, because she was simply a young woman with eyes out of an old portrait, of the kind the galaxy imports wholesale.

And the wall in the old wing was rebuilt, thicker than before, and the chapel above it became once more the quietest place in Vorbarr Sultana — not counting one private bookcase two floors up, where among the reports and the contracts stood a small volume of poetry that the Emperor of Barrayar never leafed through and never moved, and which, strictly speaking, was the only monument in the Imperium dedicated to a living person who was permitted not to know it.

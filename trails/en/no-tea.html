No Tea

An adventure of the journalist Priya Mehta — on Sergyar, with a mountain in the middle of it

I. The Declaration

The customs officer at the Kareenburg orbital terminal read the declaration twice, then raised his eyes to the passenger with the professionally neutral expression of a man who has seen everything, but not this.

“‘Biological materials to declare: none. Tea: not carrying. Deliberately.’” He blinked. “Ma’am, there is no ‘tea’ field on the form.”

“I added it,” said Priya Mehta. “I have grounds. The recipient will understand.”

The recipient was waiting at the end of the flex tube — ninety-five years old, upright, silver-haired, with eyes that surveyed the journalist from head to foot with the benevolent mercilessness of a scanner.

“You’ve come,” Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan established. “Without tea?”

“Without tea,” Mehta confirmed. “Search my luggage. I’ve declared its absence in writing.”

“In writing.” Cordelia took the declaration, read the added field, and for the first time in their acquaintance smiled with her whole face. “Girl, that is the most Betan apology I have received in seventy years, and I have received Betan apologies with notarial certification. Accepted. The interview is tomorrow at ten, on my veranda, on my terms: you ask what you like, I answer what I like, and nobody touches the teapot but me. The shuttle down is at six. I’ll fly you myself — on this planet they still let me drive, chiefly because nobody dares tell me otherwise.”

They went down at dusk. The new capital, Gridgrad, stayed far to the east — a bright spill across the plain — and the shuttle banked toward the old plateau, where at the foot of a dark, broad-shouldered mountain glimmered the lights of the first city of Sergyar: Kareenburg, the capital in retirement, named by the woman at the controls more than half a century ago.

“Beautiful mountain,” said Mehta, for something to say.

“Beautiful,” Cordelia agreed. “And it wakes once every three hundred years, by the geological estimates. That’s why we moved the capital: I signed the relocation before I retired. Only the old families stayed down there — three hundred eighty-one souls who refused to budge. First settlers. Here the word is a rank.”

“And does the mountain know about the geological estimates?”

Cordelia looked at her with sudden respect.

“You ask the right questions, Madame Mehta. Write that one down. By tomorrow it may have an answer.”

II. The Mountain

The answer came at eleven minutes past four in the morning, with a tremor that pulled Priya Mehta out of bed in the residence’s guest room, and with a distant, low sound she had never heard before and recognized instantly, with that instinct older than civilization: a mountain clearing its throat.

At five the volcanological station declared an orange code: a lateral vent opening, an ash column, probability of a pyroclastic flow down the northwest slope — the slope above the old city — “on a scale of hours to a day.” At ten past five the colonial administration in Gridgrad convened an emergency session by comm. At five forty Priya Mehta, camera on her shoulder and twenty years of reflexes, was already sitting in the corner of the operations room of the old city hall, where no one had time to throw her out, and recording.

The record of the next two hours later entered the curricula of three planets, in a section Beta called “Comparative Crisis Management” and Barrayar called simply “Cordelia.”

The problem was legally perfect. Three hundred eighty-one residents, all with first-settler status under the colonial charter — a status Cordelia herself had once written in to protect the pioneers from future bureaucracies, and which now protected them from evacuation. Compulsory removal required either a red code (and the volcanologists, honest to the last, were giving only orange), or a court order (eight hours of procedure before a bench in Gridgrad), or the personal consent of every resident. The young district prefect — a capable boy, desperately observing every letter — had already sent three appeals for voluntary evacuation. The old city had answered the first with silence, the second with a counter-petition, and the third with a sunrise in which its chimneys were smoking breakfast, as though the mountain above them were not smoking its own.

“They’re not mad,” the chairman of the old city’s neighborhood council was explaining over the comm, an eighty-year-old man with a face of the appropriate granite. “We’ve gone down into the shelters eleven times in fifty years on your orange codes, Prefect, and eleven times the mountain thought better of it. Our graveyards are here. Our years are here. When it goes red — we come down. Sooner — no.”

“The red code,” the volcanologist said quietly, to the room only, “may come forty minutes before the flow. The flow reaches the city in eleven. The arithmetic doesn’t work, Prefect.”

There fell that particular silence of a meeting that has exhausted its procedures and knows it. And in that silence a ninety-five-year-old woman who until then had listened without interfering rose from her corner and said:

“Right. That’s enough Beta for today. We’re switching to Barrayaran.”

III. Barrayaran Fashion

“Madame Vorkosigan,” the prefect began, with respect and horror in equal parts, “you have no standing authority—”

“I have none,” Cordelia agreed. “I have something better: I have nothing to lose. Now listen to the allocation, children, because the clock is running. The Betan solution to this problem is four more meetings and one posthumous commission. The Barrayaran solution is: one sufficiently old person goes down into the city, assumes the entire responsibility personally, and does not leave without the people. Afterward the procedure punishes the old person, if there is anyone left to punish. A dreadful system of government. An excellent system for rescue.” She took up her cane, which she used chiefly for pointing. “Prefect: I want every shuttle and ground vehicle on the plateau at the southern exit of the old city within the hour, engines running. Don’t ask under which article. I requisition them under article me. You’ll get receipts — Barrayaran ones, with the eyes. Volcanologist: you come with me, with the portable station, and every ten minutes you tell me the truth aloud, as it is. Madame Mehta—”

“I’m coming,” said Mehta, already on her feet.

“Of course you’re coming. You’re my record.” Cordelia headed for the door with a cane that never touched the floor. “There’s a Barrayaran saying: what is not recorded is not saved. Today we’ll test it.”

They came down into the old city at twenty past seven. The ash was already falling — thin, gray, almost gentle — and Kareenburg was meeting it in its own way: with brooms. On the thresholds of the old houses, beneath the eaves with their first-settler numbers, elderly women and men were sweeping the ash off their steps, methodically, defiantly, like people who have resolved to outlive a mountain by means of housekeeping.

Cordelia did not go to the city hall. She went to the square, to the monument of the first landing — a column of local basalt with three hundred eighty-one name plaques set into its base, one for every first-settler family — stood beside it, and said to the eighty-year-old chairman of the neighborhood council, who was waiting there because the news had outrun her:

“Hello, Mateo. Do you remember who signed your plaque?”

“You did, Cordelia,” said the old man. “You signed all of them.”

“I signed them,” said Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan. “I settled you, I named this city, I wrote into the charter your right to refuse any prefect, minister, and emperor. And today I have come to settle my account in the only way the mountain accepts: you all come down, to the last plaque, and you come down with me at the head of the column, so that if tomorrow it proves a false alarm, the whole shame is mine, at my age and on my name. There are three hundred eighty-one of you. Three hundred eighty-one of you will arrive. The counting—” she glanced at Mehta and her camera “—will be public.”

IV. Door to Door

What followed, Priya Mehta filmed in its entirety, and afterward, in the editing room, cut almost nothing, because there was nothing to cut.

Cordelia went through the old city door to door — not all three hundred eighty-one, but the precise ones: the twelve houses of which Mateo had said “these won’t budge for the Emperor himself.” At each she got out of the car, refused a hand on the steps, and spoke to the people Barrayaran fashion, which, Mehta established with growing astonishment, meant: by name, by history, and without one general phrase.

To the vintner’s widow, who would not leave her vines: “Magda, a vineyard can be replanted. You know it — you planted this one after the great drought. You’re coming down, and you’re bringing cuttings.”

To the old man with the bees: “The hives go on the second truck, Tino is tying down their frames already. Your bees have more sense than you do — they swarm at danger, they don’t barricade.”

And to the most obdurate — a ninety-year-old first settler who opened his door with a hunting gun on his shoulder, not aimed at anyone, simply on principle — Cordelia said only: “The mountain will look after Ana’s grave, Veiko. She keeps her own dead without our help — I’ve seen her do it. The living, however, are our business. Ana would have seen you off with the broom, and you know it.”

The old man looked at her for a long moment. Then he set the gun down inside the door, took his hat from its peg, and asked only:

“Is there an order to the column?”

“There is,” said Cordelia. “By the plaques, as at the landing. You’re number nine.”

By midday the square was full. The ash was no longer gentle and the sky above the northwest slope had a color about which the volcanologist reported aloud, as instructed, in a single word: “Go.” The column set off at ten past twelve — trucks, shuttles, three tractors with trailers, and one very old woman at its head with a cane pointing south — and Priya Mehta, standing on the last vehicle with her camera, filmed Kareenburg draining itself down the southern road, orderly, grumbling, alive.

They counted at the exit, aloud, by plaques. Three hundred eighty-one.

V. Eleven Minutes

The red code came at fourteen hundred three. The flow started at fourteen forty-one.

From the observation point on the plateau — safe, high, appallingly well situated — three hundred eighty-one people and one journalist watched the mountain’s northwest slope exhale: a gray column that broke, lay down, and ran downhill at a speed the eye refused to accept, because nothing that large has any right to be that fast. Eleven minutes later the upper third of the old city — the riverside quarter, forty houses, Magda’s vineyard, the apiaries — ceased to exist beneath a cloud that afterward settles into the nightmares of everyone who has seen one.

The lower two thirds survived. The square survived. The monument with the plaques survived, gone gray past recognition.

Nobody applauded and nobody wept aloud. They stood and watched, three hundred eighty-one living against one mountain, and their silence was of the kind that cannot be edited. Then old Veiko, number nine in the column, said into the ashen quiet, addressing no one in particular:

“Eleven minutes.” A pause. “How did you know, Cordelia?”

“I didn’t know,” said Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan. “I calculated. You were the only ones betting on the mountain. The rest of us were betting on the arithmetic.” She turned to her people, to her city, to the camera. “Tomorrow we begin the damage count and the building list. Today — everyone to your families; hot food is arranged in Gridgrad. We count on the way in. Three hundred eighty-one.”

“Three hundred eighty-one,” the column repeated, like the response in a liturgy, and moved off.

VI. The Veranda

The interview took place on the third day, on the residence veranda, with a view of the plateau where down below, in the grayed city, the first construction machines were already crawling. Cordelia brought the teapot herself, poured both cups herself, and set hers down demonstratively first.

“I drink first,” she explained. “Family practice. Your guest has been briefed on its origin, I assume.”

“Your guest,” said Priya Mehta, “is the granddaughter of its origin. Shall we begin?”

The interview that half the Union later watched ran fifty minutes, and Mehta asked only three questions, because the Decompression, for the first time in eleven seasons, was in possession of material before which questions were superfluous: thirty hours of recording in which the answers walked, counted, and swept ash. The first question was about Beta and Barrayar — “the double agent of common sense” accounted for the smuggling in both directions with customs-house precision. The second was about power: “The best power, girl, is the kind you can put down and still be obeyed. The rest is inventory.”

The third question Mehta asked at the end, quietly, in the voice one does not use before a camera, although the camera was running:

“My grandmother is alive. She’s watching us right now, most likely with a notebook. She asked me to put a question to you — said she has owed it to you since the last session, which never took place. The question is: was it worth it? All of it. The desertion, the wild planet, the entire… course of treatment you refused.”

Cordelia Naismith Vorkosigan looked out over the plateau for a long time — at the gray city, at the machines, at the mountain sleeping again — and when she answered, she was looking into the camera, which she had not done once in three days.

“Dr. Mehta. Half a century and a bit ago I came down onto this planet alone — the first person here, with one tent and a diagnosis from you in my luggage. The other day, down that same road, came three hundred eighty-one people who would not exist if your treatment had taken.” She raised her cup, like a toast across half a galaxy. “The difference between one and three hundred eighty-one is the answer to your question, Doctor. The account is still growing. Come and see it — the tea is on me.”

VII. The Ratings

The special edition of No Airlock went out under a title the producers dared not touch, because the host had proposed it herself: “No Tea: Three Days on Sergyar.” The viewing figures broke the record of the imperial interview by eleven hours, which Betan statistics noted with a remark that “both records belong to a single household, which raises the question of regulation.”

The Barrayaran press produced the term the Sergyar protocol — an evacuation led personally by the one who signed the people’s right to refuse. The Betan press produced an editorial titled “The Commission and the Cane,” which to its authors’ chagrin was quoted more often by Barrayarans. And the colonial administration of Sergyar entered a new clause into the charter, known to this day as the Cordelia amendment: under an orange code, a first-settler refusal may be overridden by “a person enjoying the community’s personal confidence, assuming responsibility by name.” In the field for “exemplary persons,” the clerk drafting the document had written a single name, and no one corrected him.

At the leave-taking, at the orbital terminal, Priya Mehta handed Cordelia a small flat package.

“From my grandmother. She asked you to open it after my liner departs. She swears there is nothing drinkable inside.”

Inside was an old paper notebook — the one with the notes from the sessions that never happened, half a century ago — and on the last page, in the handwriting of a very old physician, one single new diagnosis: “The patient is incurable. I recommend that medicine learn from her. — M.”

Cordelia read it for a long time. Then she laughed aloud, alone in the terminal, with that laugh about which there were legends on two planets, stowed the notebook in her inside pocket — next to the heart, Barrayaran fashion — and went off to fly her own shuttle.

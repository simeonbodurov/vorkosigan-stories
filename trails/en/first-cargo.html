First Cargo

An adventure of Admiral Lizbet Herald and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Chair

Eight months after taking the fleet, Admiral Lizbet Herald still had not adjusted the chair.

The chair was set for Quinn — two fingers lower than Herald found comfortable, the back raked for someone who sat down the way other people landed. Everyone who entered the admiral’s cabin saw the new admiral perched slightly forward, a guest in her own office, and drew their own conclusions. Herald knew what the conclusions were. She left the chair as it was, because she had more important battles than the furniture one, and because somewhere deep down she believed a chair ought to be earned rather than adjusted.

“The fleet’s yours, Lizbet,” Quinn had said at the change of command, before the ranks and the colors. “Break it and I’ll come back from the grave. From retirement it’s even easier.”

In eight months Herald had broken nothing. Two convoy contracts, executed to the comma. One evacuation off a mining station, for which the underwriters had paid with a letter of thanks — the first in the fleet’s files in eleven years. One rescue of a survey probe that the Escobaran press called “routine,” the deepest insult a journalist can offer good work. The crews still looked at her as an acting officer, but they had stopped saying so aloud, which by Dendarii standards was half a confession.

Quinn’s standing orders — three hundred eleven rules, accumulated over forty years — covered everything that had happened to her so far. Rule Forty-One: A convoy is counted on departure, at every jump, and on arrival; what has not been counted three times has not been convoyed. Rule One Hundred Six: A client in a hurry to pay is more suspicious than a client who delays. Herald knew them by heart, the way one knows a foreign city by heart: every street, but not why that particular one bends.

Then, on an entirely ordinary watch, the archive coughed up a contract she had never signed.

“Dormant contract activation,” the communications officer reported, with the expression of a man carrying someone else’s luggage and preferring not to know what was in it. “Registry seven-zero-two. Signature: Admiral Elli Quinn. Date: five years ago.”

The contract was short, which with Jacksonian contracts is the worst possible sign. House Sarlo — a small front house, biolabs for rent, no questions — had paid the fleet a generous retainer for a single service with deferred execution: upon receipt of a coded signal, the fleet shall collect a sealed deposit from Tertius Depository Station and deliver it to the head of House Sarlo. The fee waited in escrow. The signal had arrived at 0311 ship’s time, automatic, transmitted by a server no one had touched in years.

A dead man’s switch, Herald thought. Someone had arranged for this to happen if he ever stopped being able to arrange things.

She opened Quinn’s book to the section on dormant contracts. Rule Two Hundred Four read: A dormant contract is a sleeping grenade: whoever signed it was already counting to three. That was not advice. That was an epitaph with a sense of humor.

“Volan,” Herald said into the intercom. “Prep the Tuning Fork. We’re going to Tertius to collect somebody else’s grenade.”

“Acknowledged,” replied chief pilot Kera Volan. And, after a precisely measured pause: “Logging it as light duty. Seven years and two months of deductions remaining. I’ve done the arithmetic.”

II. The Deposit

Tertius Depository Station hung in the dead point between three jurisdictions and made its living by recognizing none of them. Its architecture said everything: no gun emplacements, only armor; no flags, only tariffs; and a visitors’ dock designed so that two ships on adjacent arms could not see one another. Discretion here was not a service. It was a building material.

The notary who met them was called Gelb, had no discernible age, and possessed the manner of a man who had handed sealed fates to clients for forty years without once asking what was inside. He compared the activation codes against his own, required three signatures in four places, and pronounced the one sentence Tertius offered free of charge:

“The deposit is transferred in the condition in which it was received. The station does not certify contents, provenance, or consequences.”

“Do you certify mass?” Herald asked.

“Mass is a fact,” said Gelb, with mild offense. “Facts we certify. Eight hundred fourteen kilograms with packaging.”

The House Sarlo deposit proved to be a standard-gauge container, dense, heavy, and cold — not space-cold, but cold to specification, with autonomous power and an indicator that had been blinking a placid green for five years. They moved it into the Tuning Fork’s hold with the honors due eight hundred fourteen kilograms of the unknown, and Herald ordered Dr. Roan to examine it before they cleared the dock.

The manifest, stamped by Notary Gelb, read: Biological material. Property of the House.

Dr. Roan, the fleet surgeon, ran the scanner over the container twice, then a third time, more slowly, the way one rereads a letter bearing bad news.

“Cryochamber,” he said. “Industrial grade, organ-bank maintenance standard. There’s a man in there, Admiral. Male, roughly seventy by bone density. Professionally frozen — the best that can be bought on Jackson’s Whole, and on Jackson’s Whole everything can be bought. Readings stable. Five years, and he’s as stable as on day one.”

Herald read the manifest once more. Property of the House.

She had served under Quinn for twenty-one years and had learned a great many things from her: how to read a contract, how to fly through a blockade, how to keep silent in front of a client. But what she had learned deepest was a sentence Quinn never entered in any rule, because she carried it instead of a spine: iron is counted in marks, people are counted in people.

“As of this second,” Herald said quietly, “there is a passenger aboard my ship, not a cargo. Roan, the chamber is your responsibility. Checks every four hours. Volan — course for Jackson’s Whole.”

“And the delivery?” Roan asked.

“The delivery,” said Herald, “is to the head of House Sarlo. It remains to determine which head.”

III. Two Recipients

They determined it faster than she would have liked. Tertius sold discretion to depositors, but not to observers outside: two hours after the Tuning Fork cleared the dock, two messages arrived from two different corners of local space, both addressed to “the officer commanding the Dendarii vessel that has accepted the House Sarlo deposit.”

The first was from Baron-Regent Kryle — the man who had run House Sarlo for five years, ever since the old Baron “withdrew for reasons of health.” The message was as courteous as a well-oiled vise. The Regent confirmed the contract, doubled the fee, and added a single peculiar clause: delivery “in the condition received; the House assumes liability for any temperature deviations in transit; expedited handling, no intermediate inspections.”

Herald read it twice. Temperature deviations. The man wanted a corpse delivered and was prepaying for the carelessness.

The second message was from Escobar, from a woman named Nissa Sarlo, and it offered nothing at all. The old Baron’s daughter, cast out of the House at the regency, wrote briefly: the deposit was her father; the Regent was the man her father had been hiding from; she had no funds to bid and knew it; she asked the fleet for one thing only — not to hurry. Beneath the text was a legal claim, filed honestly and hopelessly through three jurisdictions that were not on speaking terms.

Herald did something the standing orders did not require: she placed a call to Escobar and spoke to Nissa Sarlo herself.

The woman who appeared on the screen was somewhere in her forties, with the face of someone who had long since stopped expecting good news and had not yet stopped answering the comm. She worked, the background suggested, in hospital accounting. When she understood who was calling, she asked nothing about her father and nothing about the money. She asked:

“Is the chamber being maintained? Are the readings good?”

“The readings are excellent,” Herald said.

“Then don’t hurry,” said Nissa Sarlo. “Whatever Kryle is offering you, it’s for hurrying. My father has waited five years. I have waited five years. One more week of honesty won’t kill anyone.” She hesitated, then added, more quietly: “I can’t pay you, Admiral. I know what that means on Jackson’s Whole. But you don’t sound like Jackson’s Whole.”

“Summarizing,” Volan said at the staff meeting that evening, because someone had to say it out loud. “Whoever accepts delivery becomes head of the House. Jacksonian law is simple: succession is proved by who signs for the property. Hand the chamber to the Regent, and the receipt makes him Baron while the contents suffer a quiet malfunction in transit. Hand it to the daughter, and we’ve declared war on a functioning House for a fee of nothing and a claim weighing about as much as a napkin.”

“And the escrow?” Roan asked.

“The escrow releases on performance,” said Herald. “And performance is ‘delivery to the head of House Sarlo.’ The old Baron’s wording, accepted by Quinn.” She paused. “Quinn knew what she was signing. Quinn always knew what she was signing.”

That night, for the first time in eight months, Admiral Lizbet Herald wrote a letter to her predecessor. She laid out the case in six tight paragraphs, the way one reports to a commanding officer, and closed with the question she was ashamed to ask and afraid not to: What would you do?

IV. Three Sentences

The answer came back across two jumps and forty hours, and consisted of three sentences.

Herald. Your contract tells you what to deliver and to whom — read it like a pilot, not like a lawyer. The chair is yours.

Herald reread it four times. The first two times she was angry. The third time she was angry at herself. The fourth time she took the contract and read it like a pilot — that is, literally, word by word, the way one reads coordinates, because the pilot who interprets coordinates “in their spirit” wakes up inside a rock.

The cargo shall be delivered to the head of House Sarlo.

Not “to the Regent.” Not “to the heir.” Not “to the acting head.” To the head of the House. And the head of House Sarlo, legally, for five years now, had been one single man — the man who had never been declared dead, because his body was missing; the man for want of whom the Regent called himself Regent and not Baron; the man currently sleeping at minus one hundred ninety degrees in the Tuning Fork’s hold.

The recipient was the cargo.

Herald sat a long while with the contract in her hands and felt something heavy in her chest turn into something light. Then she convened the staff and laid out the plan in four sentences, because good plans are like good letters.

“We execute the contract literally,” she said. “We deliver the cargo to the head of House Sarlo: we wake him and hand it to him. No one can object to delivery to the lawful recipient without first conceding that the recipient is lawful. And that, ladies and gentlemen, is chess with one piece.”

“We’ll need a neutral dock,” said Roan. “Revival from a five-year cryosleep isn’t done in a cargo hold. I need an infirmary, backup power, and a jurisdiction where ‘patient’ is a word with content.”

“I know one,” said Herald. “It’s expensive, it’s loud, and it likes a spectacle. Volan — course for the House Fell orbital yard.”

V. The Court of Fell

The young Baron Fell received her proposal in a reception room with a glass floor, beneath which the planet turned slowly, and heard it out with the expression of a man being served his favorite dish off the menu.

“Let me be certain I follow,” he said at last. “You wish to hire my quarantine dock, my notaries, and my neutrality in order to revive, in my orbit, a vanished baron whose regent will meanwhile be doing everything possible to prevent it.” He leaned back. “Admiral, my father used to say that the Dendarii are hard to profit from but a pleasure to watch. The dock is yours. Tariff two, plus the medical surcharge. If the Regent objects—” his smile widened by two teeth “—he will object on my station, under my rules, before my notaries. I love a good chase, but I love a good grip in court even more.”

“There’s a third price,” said Herald. “You will know what happened before all of Jackson’s Whole knows it. Information with a twenty-four-hour lead.”

“Admiral,” said the young Fell with feeling, “you negotiate like a woman who served under Quinn. That was a compliment; enter it in the record.”

Regent Kryle did, of course, object. The objection arrived on the twelfth hour in the form of a “sanitary inspection” — a courier ship with too many antennas and too little patience, which demanded access to the Tuning Fork’s cargo in the name of “the biological safety of the sector,” and supported the demand by taking up a vector the textbooks call “predisposing.”

“Volan,” said Herald, without looking up from her paperwork. “Perform me a training deviation.”

What Kera Volan did over the next fourteen minutes went into the log as “scheduled maneuvering exercise” and into the memory of the sanitary inspectors, presumably, as trauma. The Tuning Fork did not run. The Tuning Fork danced: three vector changes, each of which placed the inspection ship in a posture where any active move on its part would read on Fell’s sensors as an attack upon a vessel in an authorized approach. By the fourteenth minute the inspection found itself pinned between its own cover story and the dock’s engagement envelope — and chose the cover story, which meant withdrawal.

House Fell issued the inspection an invoice for intrusion into paid protected space, with interest for the inconvenience. There was no second inspection.

“Training,” said Volan, climbing down from the console, without any inflection whatsoever. “Seven years and one month. I’ve done the arithmetic.”

VI. The Recipient Signs

The thaw took fifty hours. Dr. Roan admitted no one to the dock infirmary except two Fell technicians under oath and one notary, who insisted on being present “from the moment of first cardiac activity,” because Jacksonian law was extremely precise about when property ceases to be property.

Herald spent those fifty hours working, insofar as she could, and standing in the corridor outside the infirmary, insofar as she could not. At the forty-ninth hour Roan put his head around the door and said three words she remembered afterward precisely in that order:

“Breathing. Swearing. Asking.”

At the fifty-first hour the old Baron Sarlo opened his eyes all the way, surveyed the ceiling of the foreign infirmary, then the notary, then Herald, and said in a voice like an unoiled hinge:

“If you’re Kryle’s, you’re late. If you’re my daughter’s, you’re early. If you’re Dendarii — did Quinn send you?”

“Quinn signed. I deliver,” said Herald. “Admiral Lizbet Herald. You have an uncollected cargo, Baron.”

They explained it to him. He listened without interrupting — a skill by which Herald recognized a man who had survived a long time on Jackson’s Whole — and raised his hand to stop the account only once: at the part about the call to Escobar.

“My daughter asked about the readings first,” he repeated. “Not about the will. About the readings.”

“First and only,” said Herald.

“Hm,” said the old man, and for a moment his face went undefended, like a man handed back a possession he had written off as stolen. Then the notary passed him the receipt and he read it word by word, the way one reads a stranger’s obituary. His eye caught on the line marked recipient.

“‘The head of House Sarlo,’” he read aloud, and smiled for the first time. “I wrote that wording five years ago, after the third poisoning attempt, and wondered whether anyone would ever read it as written. My Regent was waiting for me to die in the deposit so he could declare me lost in storage and convert the assets. The conversion was filed last month — which is why the signal went out. The dead man’s switch was listening to the registry, not to my pulse.”

He took the notary’s pen and signed with slow, ceremonial malice.

“‘Received the cargo in good condition. No remarks. Signed: the recipient.’” The Baron handed back the receipt. “The escrow is yours, Admiral, with a bonus for the temperature regime. As for my Regent… House Sarlo is a reasonable House.” He tried the phrase like an old wine. “So reasonable that the debts of the regency will be sold at auction. House Fell, I trust, will take a commission for organizing it.”

“With pleasure,” said the young Fell from somewhere he was ostensibly not eavesdropping.

“And your daughter?” Herald asked.

The old man was silent. For a moment the malice slid off his face and something older and wearier showed underneath.

“Nissa has filed a claim she cannot win, against a man she cannot beat, for a father she has not seen in five years,” he said. “On Jackson’s Whole that is called stupidity. Elsewhere, I’m told, it is called something else.” He looked at Herald. “The House will need an heiress with exactly that sort of stupidity. Send her the receipt, Admiral. And give her five words from me: the readings are good, come home.”

VII. Herald’s Rule

The Tuning Fork left the Fell yard with a settled escrow, a bonus that made the fleet’s accountant ask for the figure to be repeated, and one fewer tear than expected — this time Kera Volan did not deny it at all.

Three weeks later a short message arrived from Escobar, unsigned and needing no signature: Arrived. Everyone’s readings are good. N. Herald filed it with contract seven-zero-two, because the regulations required complete documentation and her heart required precisely that final page.

In the admiral’s cabin, Lizbet Herald opened Quinn’s book to the last page, where space had been left as if on purpose. Three hundred eleven rules, gathered over forty years. She took up a pen and added the three hundred twelfth, the first of her own:

Rule 312 (Herald): If the cargo has a pulse, the cargo is a party to the contract.

Then she wrote a letter of three sentences:

Quinn. Delivered the cargo to the recipient — the recipient signed for it himself. The chair is adjusted.

She sent it, stood up, and for the first time in eight months raised the chair two fingers.

It fit.

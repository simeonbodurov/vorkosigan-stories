The Precedent

A political adventure of Helen Natalia Vorkosigan

I. The Chambers

Helen Natalia Vorkosigan was twenty-four years old, a second-year junior advocate at the chambers of Vorbonn and Sons — the oldest inheritance-law firm in Vorbarr Sultana, serving the Council of Counts since before the Time of Isolation — and up to that Tuesday her career contained exactly one remarkable event: at the entrance interview old Vorbonn had asked whether her surname would be a problem, and she had answered: “My surname has been a problem for nine generations, sir. I am the first who intends to enter it in a court register rather than a military one. Consider me an improvement.”

They took her. They kept her on small cases — wills, boundary disputes, one magnificent lawsuit over a hereditary queen bee — and Helen Natalia worked the small cases the way everything was worked in her family: all the way down, with a card file, and with a patience that among the Vorkosigans passed for an exotic mutation.

The woman came on a Tuesday, without an appointment, at the end of the working day. Thirtyish, with the weathered face of someone who works outdoors, with pinned-back hair and clothes well cut ten years earlier: a provincial Vor to the last button. She walked past the clerk, past the two vacant senior offices, and stopped in the doorway of the smallest room in the chambers.

“Are you Vorkosigan?”

“I am Advocate Vorkosigan, yes. Please come in.”

“Marta Vorail.” The woman sat on the edge of the chair, like a person unused to sitting during working hours. “My father is Count Vorail. He is dying. The doctors give him a month; he gives himself two, out of stubbornness — he does everything out of stubbornness. I am his only child. For twelve years I have run the District in fact — the waters, the forests, the cooperatives, all of it, under his signature and by my hands.” She took from her bag a folder, thick, ordered, with dividers. “When he dies, the title, the District, and three hundred thousand people go to my cousin Sig, whom the District has seen twice, both times with appraisers. Sig has already signed a preliminary contract with a consortium: the water rights of the northern lakes and half the building timber. My District will be butchered inside eight years. I have the accounting projections, if they interest you.”

“They interest me,” said Helen Natalia. “But first tell me what you want from me. To contest the contract?”

“No.” Marta Vorail looked at her straight, with the eyes of a person who made her decision long ago and has come down to the capital only for the instrument. “I want the title. I am Vorail blood, firstborn and only. I want to succeed my father as Countess Vorail — as I am. Without a Betan clinic, without transformation, without becoming ‘Lord Vorail’ to gratify a custom. Dono Vorrutyer proved twenty-five years ago that the Council accepts an heir who has become a man. I want to ask the Council why I should have to become one at all.”

In the small room it grew very quiet. Helen Natalia Vorkosigan — raised at a table where the Dono precedent was told with details from the first hand, daughter of the man who had maneuvered that vote and of a woman who knew what a name-flowerpot was — felt something pass through her that she would later call, quite unscientifically, a summons.

“Lady Vorail,” she said. “Before I accept, three questions. First: do you know that you will lose in the first instance of every opinion — chambers, newspapers, salons? Second: do you know that your cousin will fight dirty, because the consortium has already paid him an advance? And third: why me? Down that corridor sit two senior partners with thirty years each before the Council.”

“First: I have lost hailstorms, droughts, and one cattle epidemic — opinions frighten me less. Second: I know; that is why the folder has dividers. Third…” Marta Vorail smiled for the first time, dryly. “I asked about you. They told me: she is young, she is green, but she digs all the way down and she has something to prove. And I want an advocate who has something to prove. Besides, you grew up at the table where Dono was invented. I decided that in your house precedents are the family trade.”

“The family trade,” said Helen Natalia, drawing the folder toward her, “is reading the statutes to the end. Including the pages nobody has opened for a hundred years. I accept the case, Lady Vorail. Tomorrow at nine we start from the archive.”

II. The Archive

The legal question, formulated clean, fitted into one sentence, and Helen Natalia wrote it on the board in her room that first evening: “Is male succession to a countship law, or custom?”

The answer every jurist on Barrayar gave from memory ran: custom with the force of law, unbroken since the Founding, confirmed by the Council’s silent practice. The Dono precedent had not breached it — it had gone around it: Dono presented himself to the Council as a man and was confirmed as a man. The practice remained virgin: not one woman confirmed as Count in a thousand years.

“Unbroken practice is the strongest fortress in our law,” Helen Natalia explained to her client over the archive charts. “But it has a reverse side: a fortress built of practice falls to a single stone. If ever, anywhere, even once, the Council confirmed a woman — the practice is broken, the custom has a hole in it, and the question turns from ‘may she’ into ‘why did we stop.’ So I will not be writing eloquence about equality, Lady Vorail. Leave the eloquence to my father; he is incomparable, and that is exactly why we will not let him near this case. I will be looking for the stone.”

The search took seven weeks and later entered the textbooks of legal archaeology. Helen Natalia went down into the archives of Vorhartung — the kilometers of shelving beneath the Council chamber, where the protocols of the Time of Isolation lay in leather, dust, and Old Barrayaran orthography — and took them year by year, with the method inherited from her mother and the stubbornness inherited from everyone else. An assistant she found on site: Archivist Pondo, seventy years old, half-blind to people and sharp-eyed to paper, who for the first two weeks observed her in silence, and in the third laid on her table, without comment, a register labeled “Occupation, extraordinary confirmations, 2795–2810.”

“Why this one?” she asked.

“Because you ask about women and titles, young lady, and I am seventy and remember what I bound at thirty.” Pondo wiped the dust off with his sleeve, the gesture of a man stroking a dog. “Look for the widows. War eats the men, and the Districts do not wait. Look for the widows and the daughters of the bad years — and read the seals, not the headings. Headings get rewritten afterward. Seals remain.”

The stone was found on the forty-ninth day, in the register for the year 2802, on a page stuck fast with damp, which Pondo separated with a spatula and his breath: “Confirmation of Viola Vordanitz, daughter of Count Pierre Vordanitz, fallen at Vorbarov Bridge, in the rights and obligations of Count Vordanitz, by decision of the Council in exile, votes eighteen to eleven, given the extremity of the times and the fidelity of the blood. The oath sworn. Seal.”

Helen Natalia sat over the page for ten minutes without moving, the way her father sat over maps.

“Countess,” she said at last, aloud, in the empty archive. “Not regent. Not guardian. Confirmed, with oath and seal, votes eighteen to eleven. Nine years she held the District — here are the continuations: requisitions, judgments, levies — all under her signature, ‘Viola, Count Vordanitz.’” She raised her eyes to Pondo. “Why does nobody know about her? Why didn’t Dono produce her twenty-five years ago? A thing like this doesn’t get lost. A thing like this gets—”

“Hidden,” the old archivist finished. “Read further, young lady. Where there is a burial, there are gravediggers. And paper gravediggers, unlike the real kind, always leave a receipt.”

III. The Pressure

The gravediggers’ receipt Helen Natalia had not yet found when the case came into the light — because such cases do not hide: a petition for confirmation of an heir is entered at the Council’s registry, and the Council’s registry leaked toward the salons with an efficiency ImpSec could only envy.

The first week was laughter. “Vorkosigan’s daughter wants a woman-Count” — the columns competed, the cartoonists drew skirts over Counts’ uniforms, and at the chambers’ club one senior colleague patted her shoulder with the words “youth, youth — I too filed hopeless petitions at your age.” The second week the laughter stopped, because somebody in Lord Sig Vorail’s camp had inquired who the advocate was and what her access request in the archives had found — and the other machinery went to work.

The consortium withdrew from Vorbonn and Sons three corporate cases worth half a million marks in fees, with a courteous letter. Old Vorbonn summoned Helen Natalia to the office with the portraits and spoke at length about responsibility to the collective, about the firm’s traditions, and about how some battles belong to the next generation. Helen Natalia heard him out to the end, with the respect owed to an honorable man under pressure, then laid the key to the small room on his desk.

“The case leaves with me, sir. The client is mine, the risk is mine, and the generation… the generation is me. Thank you for the queen bee — she taught me patience.”

The chambers of “H. Vorkosigan” opened its doors nine days later: one room above a bookshop on Vorbaren Street, one plate, one card file, and one client who, on seeing the premises, said only: “Bigger than my office at the silos. It will work.”

And the pressure changed direction. Marta Vorail received by courier an offer from her cousin: a lifetime annuity, the house on the lakes, and “a place of honor on the District council” in exchange for withdrawing the petition — with a postscript that in case of refusal “it would become necessary to discuss publicly the health of her father and the daughter’s influence over a failing mind.” Marta brought the letter to the new chambers, laid it on the table, and asked in a level voice what the law does with letters like this.

“The law files them,” said Helen Natalia, and filed it. “In our family there is a rule: fanatics always sign, and scoundrels always add postscripts. This is our second receipt, Lady Vorail. We are building a collection.”

The family dinner at Vorkosigan House that week was a trial of a separate kind. Her father — Imperial Auditor, the man who had put Dono into history — vibrated with unexpended competence all through the soup, held out until the roast, and only then said, in the voice of a man giving alms to himself:

“One piece of advice. Just one, free, in the family.”

“No,” said Helen Natalia.

“Helen—”

“Father. If you help me, the case becomes ‘Vorkosigan versus tradition,’ and half the Council votes against you instead of on the question. If I win it alone, the case is ‘Vorail versus Vorail,’ and the Council votes on the question. You taught me that arithmetic when I was nine, over biscuits.” She softened by half a tone. “Besides, I already have an adviser from the family. Grandmother called.”

The table went quiet. Cordelia Vorkosigan, ninety, called from Sergyar rarely and precisely.

“And what did the Vicereine say?” asked Ekaterin, with a smile that suggested she knew.

“She said: ‘About time. I proposed it to this planet sixty years ago, but I was armed at the moment, and they didn’t take me seriously.’ Then she said to read the seals, not the headings — evidently she has been talking to a certain archivist. And at the end she said…” Helen Natalia reproduced the Betan intonation with a precision that made her father choke: “‘If the Council digs in, dear, remember: an institution that cannot survive one countess does not deserve to survive anything.’”

IV. The Erasure

The gravediggers’ receipt was found in the third month — not in the archive but in the library of the Council itself, in the bound protocols of the Commission for the Regularization of Wartime Acts, in session from 2831 to 2834: a dull body with a dull name, of the kind history uses for its dirty work.

Protocol of the eleventh of Southmonth, 2833: “On the motion of Count Vorhalas the Elder, the Commission considered the question of the extraordinary confirmations of the Occupation years. Resolved: the confirmation of Viola Vordanitz (2802) to be reclassified as a regency, in the interest of propriety and of the continuity of custom. The registers to be brought into conformity. Votes: nine to two. Dissenting opinion of Count Vorloupulous the Younger: ‘The Council may rewrite its books, but not the oath sworn in this chamber. I record this for whoever will one day read.’”

Helen Natalia read the dissenting opinion three times, and on the third time — she was not ashamed of it afterward — she bowed to it, to the paper, across two hundred years.

“There is the whole case, Lady Vorail,” she told her client that evening, in the room above the bookshop, under the lamp. “Not one stone — two. The first: the Council confirmed a woman, with oath and seal. The second, the heavier: the Council knew, and lied. A commission of nine men rewrote the registers ‘in the interest of propriety’ — which means the practice your cousin relies on today is not millennial and unbroken. It is two hundred years old and a forgery — a documented forgery, because the gravediggers left a receipt, and one Count with honor recorded a dissent ‘for whoever will one day read.’” She closed the folder. “We are the ones reading it, Lady Vorail. The question before the Council is no longer ‘may a woman be a Count.’ The question is ‘may the Council lie to its own protocol.’ And to that question no Count can answer ‘yes’ aloud, before the chamber, before the banners.”

Erasure attempt number two came nine days later, which spoke of desperation: at night, in the Council’s library, a man with an assistant archivist’s pass reached for the volume of the Commission’s protocols — and found at the shelf Archivist Pondo, seated on a chair with a thermos, and two men of the Vorhartung guard, summoned in advance, because Helen Natalia had filed a memorandum on “risk of interference with historical acts” on the very day of the find, and Pondo had insisted on standing the watch personally: “At thirty I bound them, young lady. At seventy I will keep them. Symmetry matters to an archive.”

The assistant archivist sang before morning: a pass bought from a middleman, a middleman paid by a legal counsel, the legal counsel — the consortium’s. The chain went into the case as exhibit nine, filed, with a divider.

V. Vorhartung

The session of the Council of Counts on the Vorail petition took place on a Thursday, with a full chamber, galleries packed to the rails, and a presiding officer who opened the item in the voice of a man opening a jar of wasps.

Helen Natalia Vorkosigan had no right to speak in that chamber — in it spoke only Counts and heirs — and she sat on the counselors’ bench behind her client, with the folders arranged in the order of battle. Her instruments were three, all prepared over a month: the written brief, distributed to every Count a week in advance — forty pages without a single adjective, only acts, seals, and lines; the client, rehearsed not to speak beautifully but to speak as herself; and two Counts who had come to her on their own, back in the second month, with an identical sentence and different smiles — “in this case I have a personal interest”: Dono Vorrutyer, who a quarter-century earlier had passed through the door that was opening wider today, and René Vorbretten, whose own title had once hung by a thread over one-eighth of blood.

The opposition opened with dignity: Count Vorhalas the current, descendant of the one from the Commission, spoke of the millennial custom, of the bones of order, and of slippery slopes. Then Lord Sig Vorail rose and made the mistake Helen Natalia had waited for and quietly prayed for: he spoke of “the natural order” and of the “failing mind” of the dying Count, governed by “foreign ambitions.”

Marta Vorail rose to answer, without notes, farmer-fashion.

“My lords. My cousin said ‘natural order.’ In my District the natural order is this: whoever plows the field knows the field. Twelve years I have plowed this field — here are the signatures, here are the harvests, here is the flood of ‘53, in which we lost not one soul; ask whomever you please out of three hundred thousand. My cousin has seen the District twice, with appraisers, and has already sold it — here is the preliminary contract, exhibit seven, with your forefathers’ water rights in clause three. I ask nothing natural of you, my lords. I ask something entirely artificial: a law read to the end. The rest is in my advocate’s brief, page eleven. The seal is from the year 2802. This Council has already answered this question — only afterward it hid its own answer, and that too is written down, page thirty-nine, with the votes of those who hid it.”

The chamber rustled over the folders. And then, in the order of inscription, the floor was taken by Count Dono Vorrutyer — gray-haired, elegant, with a quarter-century of a Count’s practice behind him and that gleam in his eyes about which the capital still told stories — and he delivered the speech whose closing sentences were later engraved, without permission, on a plate above a certain bookshop:

“My lords, I am the living proof of how far this Council will go in order not to answer one simple question. Twenty-five years ago you accepted me — because I allowed you not to ask. I traveled to Beta, I paid, I was transformed, and I spared you the question, and you were grateful to me. Today Lady Vorail does you a greater honor: she puts the question to you straight, with documents. And since I know this chamber from the inside in both of its moods, I will tell you what you are actually deciding today. You are not deciding whether a woman may wear a Count’s chain — your forefathers decided that in 2802, votes eighteen to eleven, while Vorbarov Bridge was still burning. You are deciding something else: whether this Council has the courage of its own protocol. Viola Vordanitz swore the oath in this chamber, my lords. Under these banners. A commission of nine erased her ‘for propriety’ — and one of your predecessors, Vorloupulous the Younger, recorded for us: ‘The Council may rewrite its books, but not the oath.’ I vote that we take it back. The oath. It, unlike the propriety, was real.”

The voting lasted forty minutes. Emperor Gregor, present by right and silent by custom, made no speech — only when the scales hung at four votes did he ask for the floor and say one sentence, with which the scales concluded: “The Council will count the votes; I shall remember only whether it has read page thirty-nine.”

The petition was granted: thirty-one to twenty-nine. Marta, Countess Vorail — the first in two hundred years and the second in history — swore the oath before the chamber in the voice with which she announced harvests, and in the gallery a seventy-year-old archivist wept openly and without any propriety whatsoever.

VI. The Plate

The consequences arranged themselves quickly, as they do after a breached wall.

Lord Sig’s preliminary contract with the consortium died a natural death on the day of the oath, and the consortium itself, dug into by a certain memorandum with nine exhibits, received a three-year ban on contracting with District administrations — a detail Helen Natalia filed with particular pleasure. Countess Vorail went home to her District with the same traveling bag she had come down with, and her first comital decree read: the northern lakes — a protected zone, “because I asked the people who plow them.” Viola Vordanitz was restored to the registers by decision of the Council, unanimously — the dead are rehabilitated more easily than the living, Dono observed — and in the chamber of Vorhartung, beside the banners, there appeared a small bronze plaque with two dates and one oath.

The chambers of “H. Vorkosigan” above the bookshop received in a month more inquiries than Vorbonn and Sons received in a season — including, by separate courier, a courteous letter from old Vorbonn containing congratulations, an invitation to cooperate, and a postscript: “The queen bee, incidentally, we won definitively on second instance. I thought you would want to know how the cases you begin turn out. Evidently — like this.”

And the family reckoning went by custom: quietly, at dinner. Her father raised a toast of four words — “To reading to the end” — and said nothing more, which from him was the equal of a decoration. The present came after dessert, unwrapped: a frame, and in the frame — not a diploma and not a newspaper’s front page, but a photostat of one line two hundred years old, in the small hand of Count Vorloupulous the Younger: “I record this for whoever will one day read.”

“He was waiting precisely for you,” said Miles Vorkosigan. “Two hundred years, between the covers, in the dark. That is the trade, Helen, the whole of it: somebody records, somebody reads, and between the two — as much time as it takes. Your brother cuts leashes, your sister counts people, and you… you read to the end. The house is covered on every side.”

The letter from her twin arrived the same week, from the two-seat scout Walk, beyond three wormholes, written in pencil on a flight form: “Helen. News travels slowly out here, so I learned of the vote from Dana, who learned it from Riva, who, I quote, ‘announced to the entire laboratory that from now on the argument from precedent is to be called the Vorkosigan maneuver — the second.’” There followed four lines of equations, crossed out, and at the end: “I am proud of you in a way for which I have no formulation, and you know how it irritates me to have no formulation. Keep opening doors. We will see to it they are wide. — A. A.”

VII. The Door

The plate on the chambers was changed in the spring — the old one, “H. Vorkosigan, Advocate,” gave way to a new one, because the work demanded a second room and a first associate: “Vorkosigan & Pondo Junior, Inheritance Law and Legal Archaeology.” (The archivist’s grandson, finishing law, was hired after an interview of one question: “What do you look at first in an old act?” — “The seal, ma’am. Grandfather will kill me if I say anything else.”)

And one early morning, before the clients, in the doorway of the chambers stood a girl of about sixteen, provincially dressed, with a village bag and with an expression Helen Natalia now recognized from one pace away: the expression of a person who has made a decision and come down to the capital for the instrument.

“Are you the Countess’s advocate?” the girl asked. “I’m from the Vorlakial District. We have a custom about mills passing down the female line, and everybody says it’s been that way from time immemorial and can’t be otherwise, only I think they’re lying, because my great-grandmother used to tell it different. Only I don’t have much money.”

Helen Natalia Vorkosigan looked at the girl, the bag, the decision — and opened the door all the way, with the gesture that in her family was handed down not by blood but by dinner table.

“Come in,” she said. “We’ll think about money later; customs are read free of charge. Sit down and tell it from the great-grandmother onward. And remember one thing from now on, for life, because it is my whole profession in one sentence: ‘from time immemorial’ is not a legal argument. ‘From time immemorial’ is a place where somebody stopped reading.”

The girl came in. The door stayed open — a habit of the chambers, instituted from the first day and later entered, half in jest, in the firm’s charter: doors are kept open by walking through them. In both directions. Every day.

A precedent, in the end, is simply a door that has been opened once before. The rest is walking.

The Guest List

A quiet mystery for Lady Alys and Simon Illyan

I. The Letter

The letter arrived three days after the funeral of Lady Margo Vorsell, delivered by a law office with the instruction “to be handed personally to Lady Alys Vorpatril after my death, unopened,” and Lady Alys read it alone, in the morning salon, while her tea went cold untouched — a thing that had not happened in that house for years.

Then she went out to the garden, where Simon Illyan was pruning the roses with the patience of a man for whom time has long been an ally, and handed it to him without a word.

The handwriting was that of a very old woman, writing slowly and with resolve:

Alys. If you are reading this, I have died without gathering the courage, and I owe you the last thing. For sixty years I have watched you at the receptions — how you enter, how everyone makes way, how you wear your widowhood like a decoration — and for sixty years I have known something you do not. On the night the capital fell, Vordarian’s men found Padma’s hiding place not by chance, not by tracking, and not by the betrayal of that hired driver they afterward shot for it. They found it because someone told them the address. Someone who knew it because you yourself had trusted him. Ask who brought the wine to the refuge on Willow Street that evening — the wine you did not order, and yet it came. More I cannot do. And this was more than I could do, for sixty years. Forgive a dead woman at least the haste with which she writes to you. — Margo.

Illyan read the letter twice. The roses waited.

“Willow Street,” he said at last. “The second refuge. The one you and Padma set out from on the night when—” He stopped, as he always stopped at that threshold, forty years now.

“When they shot him in the street, and I gave birth to Ivan in a doorway three hundred meters away,” Lady Alys finished. Her voice was perfectly level: she pronounced that sentence once a year, on the anniversary, before the memorial stone, and it had long since been polished like the stone itself. “The investigation concluded: the driver. The hired driver sold us for money, was caught, confessed under fast-penta, was shot by Vordarian’s people in his turn so he would talk no further. Case closed. I closed it inside myself forty years ago, Simon.” She looked at the letter. “And now a dead woman tells me I closed the wrong page.”

“I ran that investigation,” said Simon Illyan quietly. “Personally. Every document passed through me. Every thread.” He drew off his gardening gloves, slowly, finger by finger. “And I remember none of it, Alys. Not one day of it. All of it was on the chip, and the chip…” He made the gesture he made in place of the whole sentence: a light touch to the temple, where once had been the archive of three decades. “I remember only the conclusion, because I have reread it since, like a stranger’s report. The driver. Case closed.”

The two old people stood in the garden among the roses — she at ninety, straight as protocol; he at eighty-four, his memory in rags and his mind whole in the gaps — and the silence between them weighed a letter from the grave.

“Well,” said Lady Alys Vorpatril at last, in the tone with which for sixty years she had opened seasons, buried emperors, and imposed order on unruly ballrooms. “Then we shall investigate it anew. You will read the reports of young Illyan. And I shall do the thing I do better than anyone living and most of the dead.” She smoothed the letter with two fingers. “I shall reconstruct the guest list.”

II. The Archive

The authorization for access to the archives of the Pretendership Galeni signed without a single question — he only looked at the two visitors over the folder and said: “The office at the end is yours for as long as you need. If you need anything more than an office — that too,” which, from the chief of ImpSec, was the equivalent of an embrace.

For three days Simon Illyan read his own reports from sixty years before.

It was the strangest occupation of his life, and his life had contained a remarkable collection of strange occupations. The handwriting was his — the young hand, taut, from before the chip made handwriting unnecessary. The logic was his: he recognized it the way one recognizes one’s own gait on a recording. But the memory behind every page was missing — smoothly, painlessly, finally — and Simon Illyan taught himself to interrogate the least accessible witness of his career: himself, at twenty-eight, through paper.

“The young me was good,” he reported to Alys that evening, in the morning salon, converted into an operations room with a card file such as had not been assembled there since the years of Gregor’s matchmaking. “Pedantic to the point of nausea. The driver confesses under fast-penta, yes — but look at what he confesses: ‘I told the address to a man who sought me out.’ He did not sell the information on his own initiative. They came looking for him. Which means someone already knew that this particular driver drove this particular family — and that knowledge, Alys, is not bought on the street. The young me noted it — here, in the margin, a question mark, ‘who pointed them to the driver?’ — and then the case was closed from above, with the resolution ‘in view of the military situation.’ The Pretendership was burning, the capital was falling, no one had resources for the second layer.” He set down the folder. “Young Illyan’s question mark has stood in the archive for sixty years. Margo Vorsell knew its answer.”

“And the wine?” asked Alys.

“In the protocols for the Willow Street refuge, wine does not figure. Anywhere. The inventory of the premises was made after the storming, in haste.” Illyan looked at her. “Which means the wine is remembered by you alone.”

“I remember it,” said Lady Alys Vorpatril slowly. Her eyes looked through the wall, sixty years back, into a room with drawn curtains, with Padma pacing by the window, and with herself, enormous, in the ninth month, counting the contractions and the hours. “A basket. Three bottles of southern red and an unsigned note: ‘For courage. From friends.’ Padma laughed and said our friends knew his taste. I wanted to throw it out — I was frightened to the bone of everything that came through that door. He opened one of the bottles.” She was silent. “Three hours later they came.”

III. The List

What Lady Alys Vorpatril did over the following two weeks, Simon Illyan later described to Galeni — for the record, because he was an old archivist and believed in records — as “the most impressive intelligence operation ever conducted without a single document.”

She reconstructed the night. Not from the archive — from herself: from the memory that sixty years of society life had trained into an instrument beside which the Imperial card files looked disheveled. Who knew of the first refuge. Who knew of the move to the second. Who brought food, who brought medicine, who brought news. She wrote out by hand, in her protocol-invitation script, a list of every living soul who had passed through the two hiding places or known of them during those eleven days — twenty-nine names, each with date, hour, and source of knowledge — and then began striking them out, name by name, with a logic Illyan followed with professional reverence: this one died before the capital fell; this one knew of the first refuge, not the second; this one was checked by Vordarian’s people and arrested — therefore he was not their source; this one is my mother-in-law by marriage, Simon, don’t be absurd.

Four names remained. Three of them were long in their graves, with biographies excluding the motive. The fourth was Lord Nikis Vorsell — Margo’s husband. A clerk of the supply ministry, who had survived the Pretendership without a scratch, made afterward a long, quiet, blameless career, and died twenty-two years ago with a half-page obituary and a state decoration for “faithful service through the troubled times.”

“The wine,” said Alys, and her voice had gone very quiet and very precise. “Nikis Vorsell was responsible for provisioning the loyal houses during the siege. Provision baskets, Simon. He carried provision baskets — legally, with a pass, across half the capital. No one checks the man with the baskets. Margo told me where to look with the precision of a wife who had packed those baskets with her own hands sixty years before. ‘Ask who brought the wine.’ She could not pronounce the name even from the grave. So she sent me his address.”

“Motive?” Illyan asked, by the reflex of half a century.

“We will find it in your archive,” said Lady Alys. “Look for what Vordarian held over Nikis Vorsell that month. He held something. Margo writes ‘I did not gather the courage for sixty years’ — that is not complicity out of greed, Simon; that is shame born of coercion. Greed hides more easily.”

They found it in two days, in a side fond of the archive, in the detention lists of Vordarian’s counterintelligence: Stefan Vorsell, nineteen, a cadet, Nikis’s younger brother — arrested by the Pretender’s men in the first week, held as a “guest” in the barracks of the capital garrison. Released alive — the only one of his group — two days after the night of Willow Street. Emigrated after the war to Komarr, lived quietly, died old.

“A brother for the Emperor’s cousin,” said Illyan into the silence of the archive office. “Vordarian traded a nineteen-year-old boy for the address of Padma Vorpatril. And Nikis paid. And lived with it another forty years, with the decoration for faithful service on his lapel.” He closed the folder with hands that were not entirely steady. “The case is solved, Alys. Sixty years late, by two pensioners and one dead widow. The question is what we do with it now.”

IV. The Sentence

Lady Alys Vorpatril considered the question for three days, during which the house was very quiet and Simon Illyan pruned roses and waited, because after so many years he knew when a decision must not be touched from outside.

On the fourth day she dressed formally — not ceremonially: formally, as for an audience — and ordered the car.

“Vorsell?” was all Illyan asked.

“The grandson,” she said. “Lord Danis Vorsell. Fifty-two, inherited the title and the house. A judge of civil cases, three children, a reputation as a dull and honest man — in that combination, in my experience, the second is usually true. He knows nothing, Simon. He was born a decade after that night.” She drew on her gloves. “And that is exactly why I shall tell him personally. Are you coming?”

The conversation in the study of Lord Danis Vorsell lasted an hour, and Illyan never retold its contents to anyone, because there are conversations that belong only to their participants. But its end he heard, standing by the window: the judge — a graying man with an honest and, at that moment, devastated face — asked with effort: “My lady. Whatever you ask of my house — reparation, public acknowledgment, anything — it is yours. What do you want?”, and Lady Alys Vorpatril, ninety years old, a widow of sixty, answered in the voice with which seasons are closed:

“I want three things, my lord, and not one of them is from you. The first I wanted from your grandfather, and it is beyond reach: one night in the winter of the Pretendership, given back. The second I wanted from your grandmother, and she gave it to me with her last letter: the truth. And the third I shall give myself, and it is the reason I am here: I want this story to end with me, and not with your children. Vordarian took from my family one life and sixty years of questions. I will not permit him, forty years after he burned, to take also the house of an honest judge. Your grandfather was coerced, and paid with forty years of silence; your grandmother paid with sixty; and the account in this case is closed by those who had the right to close it.” She rose. “I will ask you only this: the next time you vote on petitions for clemency, remember what guilt is sometimes made of. Good day, my lord. Give my regards to the children.”

In the car on the way back they were silent a long while. Then Illyan said, watching the capital go by:

“You know that by regulation it should have gone otherwise. A report, a reopening of the case, a historical commission. I was the regulation of this Imperium for thirty years, Alys. And for thirty years I watched what it does with sixty-year-old truths: digs them up, floodlights them, and leaves the living lying in the excavation.” He found her hand on the seat without looking. “You judged better. For the record — verbal, sole, mine: I concur in the sentence.”

“Of course you concur,” said Lady Alys. “I chose you twenty-five years ago by the same criterion by which I choose wine, Simon: an excellent past and a soft finish.” A pause, precisely measured. “Besides, there remains the third part of the sentence, and for it I need you whole. Ivan has a right to know. All of Ivan — not the summary.”

V. Ivan

The holo-link to Ylla was established that evening, from the morning salon, and Consul Lord Ivan Xav Vorpatril heard his mother out from the other end of an entire Nexus — thirty minutes, without interrupting once, which was in itself an event of historical significance.

Alys told him everything: the letter, the wine, the list of twenty-nine names, the provisioner’s baskets, the nineteen-year-old cadet traded for his father, and the sentence pronounced in a judge’s study. She told it in order, with dates, without cuts, by the rule this extended family had earned the hard way on three planets: stories with the parts cut out are more dangerous than enemies.

Ivan was silent a long time after the end as well. His face — sixty-three years old, browned by a foreign sun, with his father’s features coming out more clearly with age, like watermarks — worked in a way his mother allowed to work itself all the way through.

“Do you know what’s strange?” he said at last. “All my life I hated a shadow. A hired driver without a face — that’s all I was told, that’s all I hated: a nothing that sold my father for money. And now you give me a man. A blackmailed man with a brother in Vordarian’s barracks. And I find that…” he reached for the word with his hand, an old gesture, “…that I can’t hate him the way I hated the shadow. I can only be sorry. For all of us at once. Is that normal, Mother?”

“That is growing up, dear,” said Lady Alys. “At sixty-three it is a little on the late side, but in our family the men have always been slow in that discipline. Your father was the exception.” Her voice softened for a moment into the register she kept for two occasions a year. “He would have liked what you became, Ivan. I tell you this with the authority of the sole living witness.”

“Mother—”

“And come for the anniversary,” she finished briskly, because there were depths in which one did not linger even at her age. “This year it is a round one; we shall all be at the stone. Bring Tej and the children. Tell Padma to bring his drone — his great-grandfather by his great-uncle’s line loved machinery; let him film.”

VI. The Stone

The anniversary fell on a clear winter day, and at the memorial stone of Padma Vorpatril — gray granite in a side alley of the capital’s memorial park, with a name, dates, and nothing more, to the widow’s taste of a woman who detested superfluous words — the whole family gathered this time: Ivan and Tej, arrived from Ylla; Alys and young Padma with the drone circling discreetly at a respectful height; half a dozen Vorkosigans, because the boundary between the two families had long been an administrative formality; and two very old people, standing at the front.

Lady Alys laid down the offering — southern red grapes instead of flowers, this year and hereafter: a detail understood by exactly two of those present — and stood before the stone with a straight back while the family behind her kept silence.

“For sixty years I promised him the same thing,” she said afterward to Illyan, as the family drifted ahead down the alley and the two of them walked last, arm in arm, at the pace of people in no hurry to be anywhere. “Every anniversary, silently: ‘I will find out, Padma. Someday I will find out the whole of it.’ A widow’s promise — the kind one makes in order to get through the day, with no one believing in its fulfillment.” She looked straight ahead, toward the grandchildren at the end of the alley. “This year, for the first time, there was nothing to promise. I stood before the stone empty. Do you know what that feeling is, Simon?”

“I know,” said Simon Illyan. “It is called peace. We fail to recognize it because all our lives we have mistaken it for emptiness.” He walked a few paces in silence. “I lost thirty years of memories and thought I had lost myself. Then a lady with an impeccable guest list taught me that memory is not a warehouse, Alys. Memory is a sentence we pronounce upon the past — and the good news of old age is that sentences can be reviewed. You reviewed a sixty-year-old one today. In favor of all the living and most of the dead.”

“Of most,” Lady Alys agreed. “Margo Vorsell, wherever she is, will excuse the lateness of my reply: I accept the apology. And Vordarian can spin.” She stopped for a moment and looked back at the stone, lit by the low winter sun. “Strange, Simon. For sixty years this was the place I came to ask. From today it is the place I come to tell. Next year I shall tell him about Byerly’s wedding — Padma would have laughed for a week.”

“Next year,” said Illyan, “we shall tell him together. I remember details you are protocol-bound to pretend you never saw.”

“That is why I keep you, dear,” said Lady Alys Vorpatril, and took his arm more comfortably. “An archive with a soft finish. Come along. The grandchildren are freezing, and at home there is tea and sixty years of catching up, which — by the reckoning of a new acquaintance of mine from Escobar — is only ever done quickly.”

And the two old people set off down the alley after their family — slowly, upright, arm in arm — past the stones of all their dead, toward the tea, the grandchildren, and the remainder of the afternoon, which, like all afternoons of late, they intended to live by name.

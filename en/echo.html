Echo

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Second Theft

Hull 48, the second and last of the Fan class, disappeared fourteen months after the first — and this time Illyrica did not have the excuse of surprise.

After the Volan affair the shipyard had changed everything: the acceptance codes, the protocols, the security, even the lock supplier. Hull 48 was being handed over to its buyer — “Vetis Industrial Consortium,” a Tau Verde-registered carrier with impeccable documents and a prepaid account — under escort, with a double acceptance crew, and with a ceremony at which Director Maren Sol delivered a speech on the lessons of the past. Three days later, on the first intermediate jump of the delivery run, the ship exited the passage, transmitted a routine report, turned out of the corridor, and sank into silence. The escort, an old Illyrican frigate, lost it from its scopes for eleven minutes. By the twelfth, there was nothing left to lose.

Quinn learned the news from three sources within six hours, which was in itself a diagnosis of the scale of the panic. First came the insurer — the same assessor from Lloyd-Tau, whose voice she now recognized by its opening sigh. Second was Director Sol, who this time was not gray but white, and who pronounced a sentence unprecedented in the annals of Illyrican shipbuilding: “Admiral Quinn, Illyrica wishes to engage the Dendarii Fleet.” The third message came through four blind relays, unsigned, and consisted of two sentences:

I told you such things are not to be written out loud. I’m sending you a man — take him aboard; you’ll know whose he is by the handwriting.

The man arrived on the Escobaran liner under the name of a trade attaché, and came aboard the Tuning Fork with one travel bag and one tablet bearing so many classification stamps it practically glowed. Lieutenant Colonel Elin Venek, ImpSec, galactic operations — with a few more lines around the three-screens-at-once eyes than Quinn remembered from the reports on the Soven affair, and with a letter of accreditation signed by the chief of ImpSec himself.

“General Galeni instructed me to be perfectly candid, Admiral,” said Venek. “Barrayar has no rights over this ship and claims none. Barrayar has an interest in exactly one hypothesis: that the double phase fan — a technology our own Necklin architects howl with envy over — is on its way to Cetaganda. If the hypothesis is correct, you are chasing a thief and I am chasing a strategic technology leak, and both chases end at the same place. I propose we travel together.”

“And if I have to choose between the ship and your hypothesis?”

“Then we bargain,” said Venek. “I carry authorizations for that too.”

Quinn liked her right there.

II. The Buyer Who Doesn’t Exist

The first discovery was made not by the fleet but by accounting — more precisely by Venek and her team of three, who in four days took “Vetis Industrial Consortium” apart down to the last fictitious bolt.

“The buyer does not exist,” Venek reported to the war council. “That is to say, it exists in juridically impeccable form, which in practice is the same thing. Vetis Industrial is an old acquaintance — the façade through which Clan Soven financed the Obel operation. After Soven’s fall the façade was sold, the way one sells a used glove, and the new hand inside it, by every mark of the handwriting, is Clan Raal. The war party.” She brought up the schematic. “And now the beautiful part. Vetis paid for the ship in advance, in full, with clean money — and insured it with Lloyd-Tau for one hundred and ten percent of value. Have your paid-for, insured ship stolen, and you collect both the money and — through two resales of the ‘stolen goods’ — the ship itself, now stripped of any documentary link to you. This is not a theft, Admiral. This is laundering. The ship was not stolen from the buyer. The ship was stolen for the buyer, by the buyer, so that it could vanish from every registry on its way to Rho Ceta.”

“So we’re chasing not thieves but a delivery,” said Quinn. “Which simplifies the question ‘where to’ and complicates the question ‘how do we catch them.’ Volan?”

Kera Volan, chief pilot of the fleet, with eleven years and three months of pay garnishments remaining, had been standing at the navigation display for an hour, silent in a way Quinn had learned not to interrupt.

“First — who’s flying them,” she said at last. “A Fan is not flown from a read manual. There are six pilots in the galaxy who have test-flown this class. Four are on Illyrica, under watch since the theft. The fifth is me.” A pause. “The sixth is Yarno Vek. Second acceptance pilot of the series. Lead on hull 48, as I was lead on 47. Eight months ago he left the company — ‘family reasons.’ I checked: no family. There is a mortgage, a gambling history, and eighteen years of watching everything go to me first.”

“Do you know how he flies?”

“Better than he knows it himself. We flew wing to wing for ten years.” Volan pointed at the chart, and her finger traced a chain of three nodes off every trade corridor, a route with a reputation so bad that spacers had given it a name like a tavern legend. “And I know where he’ll go. The Drunken God’s Road: three high-variance throats, in sequence, no bypass. No convoy enters there; no patrol frigate can follow. A standard ship runs the Drunken God at fifty percent odds per throat. A Fan runs it at ninety-eight. Vek will take 48 through exactly there — because it is the route that shakes everything in the universe off your tail.” She looked up. “Everything except one thing.”

“Except his sister,” said Quinn. “Course, Pilot. Let’s go bait the Drunken God.”

III. The Drunken God’s Road

The three throats of the Drunken God lived up to their reputation and exceeded expectations, and the crew of the Tuning Fork, veterans of one closing throat, ran them with clenched teeth and a growing, uncomfortable respect for the man ahead of them — because Yarno Vek flew magnificently.

The wake of hull 48 in the five-space interface read like handwriting, and Volan read it aloud, throat by throat, in an intonation where professional appraisal and private bitterness blended past distinguishing: “He entered at rating plus eight — sparing the machine, so he’s thinking of the sale price. Here he waited out a full cycle for a wide window — polite, clean, expensive flying. Vek always flew expensively. On trials, that’s a virtue.” At the third throat the wake showed something else: an entry into a narrow window, daring, on the edge — and Volan fell silent, then said only: “He’s sensed someone coming through behind him. He’s starting to spend the ship. Good. A frightened pilot makes mistakes.” A pause. “I didn’t.”

They gained hours at every throat — not because Volan flew better in the pure sense, but because she flew cheaper: where Vek husbanded the buyer’s machine, she spent her own with the precision of someone who knows personally which seam lies by how much. At the exit of the Drunken God the deficit had melted from sixty hours to nineteen.

And after the Drunken God the road forked only once, and both tines of the fork were known to everyone on the bridge: to the left — long, clean corridors toward the Hegen Hub and the Cetagandan direction; to the right — Jackson’s Whole.

“Right,” said Quinn and Venek simultaneously, looked at each other, and Venek yielded with a gesture.

“The ship is only half laundered,” Quinn explained. “The money is washed, but the hull still carries Illyrican serial numbers in every second casting. Raal will not accept a delivery that the first ordnance inspector could identify. They need a yard that changes ships’ identities, asks zero questions, and is big enough not to fear consequences.” She smiled without humor. “There is exactly one such yard in the galaxy, and we are flying straight at it.”

IV. Fell’s Yard

Fell Station hung over Jackson’s Whole as it always had — armed to the teeth, lit like a fairground, and open to anyone whose money asked no questions. Hull 48 sat in the House’s enclosed dock, surrounded by work platforms, with cutters already crawling over it, erasing serial numbers casting by casting.

The Dendarii Fleet could not storm Fell Station — everyone knew that, including House Fell, which made negotiations possible. Quinn and Venek went up under a trade flag and were received by the Baron himself — not old Georish, long deceased, but the heir: young, smooth, and armed with the family smile — in an office overlooking the dock where the subject of the conversation lay stripped to the waist.

“Admiral Quinn. A legend among legends.” The Baron spread his hands. “I’ll spare you the overture: House Fell has accepted a commission for the cosmetic processing of a vessel. The commission is paid, lawful under our laws, and protected by commercial confidentiality. My sympathies are with you; my deal is with the client. Unless…” the smile widened by a millimeter, “…you’ve brought a better deal.”

“I’ve brought you accounting,” said Venek, and laid the tablet on the desk. “Your fee was paid from the escrow of Vetis Industrial. That same escrow is currently the subject of an insurance-fraud investigation in the amount of one hundred and ten percent of the value of that hull in your dock. You know what Lloyd-Tau does with payments made from fraudulent funds, Baron: it claws them back. Through the courts, through every jurisdiction, with interest, forever. House Fell will work three months on that hull and then spend ten years repaying the fee in installments — publicly, which is the worse part.” She sat back. “Or.”

“Or?” asked the Baron, without the smile now.

“Or you void the contract under your compromised-client clause — it exists, I checked — retain the advance lawfully, sell us your client’s schedule for a modest consulting fee, and when the whole affair blows up in a month, House Fell turns out to be the House that smelled the fraud first and refused complicity. Reputation, Baron. The one commodity your House has never managed to manufacture for itself.”

The Baron looked at the tablet a long time. Then he laughed — genuinely, which on Jackson’s Whole happened once a decade.

“My grandfather used to say: beware of Barrayarans offering favorable terms.” He pushed a crystal with the schedule across to them. “Your client is nervous, ladies. He pulled the ship out this morning, unfinished, with half the numbers erased — paying the penalty without blinking. Handover point: the Keros system, a dead dwarf two jumps from here, in eighty hours. The buyer arrives in an armed ‘collector’s’ vessel, Cetagandan build, no flag.” The Baron rose, and for a moment the family smile became almost human. “One thing for free, Admiral: whoever is flying that hull — flies it terrifyingly. He left my dock through a window my own pilot wouldn’t have put a shuttle through. Catch him if you can. Fell’s yard does love a good chase.”

“Eighty hours, two jumps, and he left six ago,” said Quinn on the shuttle back. “By the direct route we don’t catch him. Volan?”

The chief pilot’s voice on the channel had that level, dangerous tone Quinn remembered from one closing throat:

“There is a crooked route, Admiral. One throat, off to the side, with variance worse than the Drunken God. Vek knows it and won’t enter it with…” static, then the voice came back, “…with someone else’s goods aboard. No sane pilot would enter it.”

“And you?”

“Ours has been through worse.” A pause. “We arrive at Keros twelve hours ahead of him. At his exit point.”

V. The Exit Point

The Keros system had been a graveyard from birth: a dead brown dwarf, three cinder planets, zero traffic — the perfect place to hand over a ship that doesn’t exist to a buyer who doesn’t exist, for money that has already vanished. The “collector’s” vessel arrived first — two hundred meters of Cetagandan elegance without a single identification mark, armed in a way that made “collector” the funniest word in the sector — and took up a distant orbit, patient.

The Tuning Fork hung in the shadow of the second cinder planet, dark, cold, drives a hand’s breadth above zero — on the very exit point of the passage from which, in eleven hours, her sister would emerge.

“Textbook classic,” Quinn told the hushed bridge. “The one place where even the fastest ship in the galaxy is blind, deaf, and slow is the first seconds after a jump. That is why exit points are defended, and that is why they are ambushed. Vek will come out with an overheated implant after a bad throat, an unfinished ship, and a buyer before his eyes. We will give him three seconds to realize whose shadow is hanging over him. Out of respect.”

“And the Cetagandan?” asked Venek.

“The Cetagandan will watch. Then he will listen. I’ve prepared him a program.”

Hull 48 rose out of the passage fourteen minutes ahead of schedule — Vek had driven like a devil — and the three gifted seconds ran out in silence. On the fourth, the Tuning Fork lit everything at once: drives, targeting radars, and the hull floodlights, illuminating her sister at point-blank range like a police torch in the face.

“Yarno Vek,” said Quinn on an open channel. “This is the Tuning Fork. You know the silhouette. You know what it can do. Reactor to minimum, fan to zero, and nobody touches the ship you have guarded so carefully the whole way.”

Vek did not answer. Vek did what magnificent pilots do when cornered: the impossible. Hull 48 rolled through its own axis, ducked under the tractor lock, and dove for the dwarf, seeking the gravity well as a hare seeks the brush, and for the next eight minutes the two identical machines inscribed around the dead star a duel that the Cetagandan vessel’s sensor logs, as later became known, preserved to the last frame — because even enemies recognize art.

The ships flying were identical. The difference was the hands. Vek flew by the rating plate — brilliantly, at the edge of every permitted figure, but by the plate. And Kera Volan knew the plate lied, knew by how much and where, and in the eighth minute, when Vek staked everything on a crossing pass computed to half a second, she performed the maneuver that existed in no manual: the asynchronous fans, port and starboard in different phase, the sideways slide of a sheet of paper under a guillotine — and the Tuning Fork was suddenly not where any mathematics placed her, but three hundred meters off her sister’s stern, her targeting locked on the one point that interested Volan.

“I know her seams,” she said quietly, and fired once.

The beam opened the casing of 48’s starboard fan precisely along the mounting seam — surgery, not violence. The ship tumbled on inertia, blind to five-space, whole, alive, and helpless. The chase was over.

“And now the program for the audience,” said Quinn, and turned the transmitter toward the “collector.” “Unidentified vessel, this is Admiral Quinn, Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet, operating under contract to the Illyrica shipyards and the Lloyd-Tau insurance syndicate. The cargo you are waiting for is material evidence in an insurance-fraud case. The payment you owe was confiscated this morning — the escrow accounts of Vetis Industrial are frozen in four jurisdictions; the lieutenant colonel beside me collects jurisdictions. And the full evidentiary package — ownership, payments, routes, names — is on its way to the interested capitals. All of them, ghem-lord. Including yours.” A pause. “Keros is a beautiful system for reflection. I recommend a brief one.”

The silence from the Cetagandan vessel lasted fourteen minutes. Then it lit its drives and left — without a shot, without a word, without the goods: an empty hand returning to an empire that did not forgive empty hands. Venek watched it go and said thoughtfully:

“Alive, ruined, and compromised. Galeni will say we saved him work: a living receipt is more useful than a dead adversary. Receipts talk.”

VI. The Mirror

Yarno Vek sat in the Tuning Fork’s brig upright, with the dignity of a man who has nothing else left, and when the door opened he did not ask who was coming. He knew.

“Kera.”

“Yarno.”

They were silent a long time — the two best pairs of hands of a generation, wing to wing for ten years, on opposite sides of one grille.

“Say it,” he said at last. “You’ve come to say it. That you stole a ship too. That the difference between us is one suspended sentence.”

“No.” Volan sat down across from him. “I’ve come because for ten years I wondered why they always ranked you second, with equal hands. Today, at Keros, I understood. You fly by the rating plate, Yarno. You have always flown by the plate — brilliantly, immaculately, to the last permitted figure. But the plate is someone else’s word about the machine. And you never wanted to know her beyond other people’s words.” A pause. “And it’s the same with the thefts. I stole a ship against my whole life, signed before a lawyer, for forty-one people I could not not save. You stole a ship against a fee. Both are theft, you’re right. But one has people in its ledger, and the other only numbers. That is why I fly and you sit. Not because of the court. Because of the ledger.”

She rose. At the door he asked after all, and for the first time his voice was only human:

“The throat to Keros. The side one. Did you really run it?”

“Really.”

“How far over the plate?”

“Yarno,” said Kera Volan, almost gently, “the plate lies. That was the whole lesson, all eighteen years of it. A pity you heard it through a grille.”

VII. Echo

The settlement took two months and ended with the strangest distribution of trophies in Quinn’s career.

Yarno Vek went to Illyrica under escort and received eleven years from the court — a figure whose symmetry Volan declined to comment on. Lloyd-Tau escaped a payout of one hundred and ten percent and, in a fit of unheard-of gratitude, paid the Dendarii Fleet a recovery fee that wiped, to the mark, both mortgages on the Triumph — the old flagship greeted the news, in the engineering crew’s words, “with suspiciously cheerful creaking.” Clan Raal, left without an army behind one door and without a fan behind the other, sank into its Cetagandan affairs with a silence the intelligence digests described as “pre-sepulchral.” And hull 48 — half de-identified, with an opened seam and no lawful owner in this universe — was put up by the syndicate for auction.

The auction was won, through three layers of discretion that Venek never officially acknowledged, by the Imperial Service of Barrayar.

The christening took place in Barrayaran orbit, in a small circle, and Quinn attended by an invitation of the kind one does not decline. Hull 48, repaired, renamed, and entered in the registry as the fastest courier of the Imperium, received its name from the Emperor personally — but the suggestion, as Quinn learned afterward, had come with a note from a certain Auditor: since the first of the class was called Tuning Fork, the second, having answered its pitch forever, could bear only one name.

Echo.

“Two ships, one song,” said Miles Vorkosigan at the reception after the ceremony, glass in hand, wearing the expression of a man to whom symmetries give almost indecent pleasure. “Yours sets the pitch; ours answers. If that is not a model of relations between Barrayar and the Dendarii Fleet these past thirty-five years, I don’t know what is.”

“The model is different,” said Quinn. “Yours was bought with the adversary’s confiscated money, and mine with two mortgages and a theft. As ever: the Imperium pays with other people’s, the mercenaries with their own.”

“Which is why the song is yours,” said Miles, “and we only sing along.” He raised his glass. “To sisters, Quinn. Ships’ and otherwise.”

The fleet left Barrayaran orbit the next day. On the bridge of the Tuning Fork, Kera Volan laid in the course for the node and, for a moment, held her hand above the console — a gesture the whole bridge knew by now and no one interrupted: the chief pilot listening to her ship the way one listens to a tuning fork before beginning to sing.

“Clean tone,” she said. “Let’s go.”

And the Tuning Fork set out for the next contract, while far behind her, in the Barrayaran registry, her echo was standing its first watch.

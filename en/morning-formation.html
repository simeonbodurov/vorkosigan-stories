Morning Formation

A Cetagandan story — an investigation by ghem-General Dag Benin

I. The Quarantine

The report reached the Celestial Garden through the agricultural statistics channel, which was in itself the first anomaly: the agricultural statistics of Mu Ceta had not woken anyone in the middle of the night for forty years.

Agrarian district Still Reach, southern continent: a quarantine declared by the local military command “on sanitary grounds,” three settlements evacuated, civilian communications cut — and one word leaked through the quarantine cordon with the refugees before the cordon sealed completely. The word was “mantises.”

Ghem-General Dag Benin, chief of Imperial internal security, read the report twice, ordered tea he did not drink, and requested an audience under the urgent protocol. The Celestial Lord received him in the garden of the early hours, between two rows of cherry trees blossoming in counter-phase, heard him out without interrupting, and asked a single question, with which the case acquired its true rank:

“The archives of Clan Raal. Under whose seal do they stand?”

“Under a triple one, Celestial Lord. The military ministry, the Star Crèche, and my own service.” Benin made a pause the length of an admission. “I checked before the audience. The seals are intact. The registers — impeccable. That is exactly what troubles me: something is walking around on Mu Ceta that exists only behind those seals, and the seals are whole. Which means no one broke into the archive. Someone copied it — from inside, with access, with patience, and with grounds to believe that no one would ever compare the colony in the delta against the catalog genome.”

“The colony,” repeated the Emperor.

“The refugees speak of attacks, Celestial Lord. A burned settlement, casualties, monsters in the night — the military command on site confirms it and requests expanded powers.” Benin bowed exactly as much as was required. “Whereas the satellite survey I ran before coming shows something else: four hundred and eleven large bioforms that left the area of the ‘attacks’ on the first night, crossed eighty kilometers of cultivated land without touching a single farmstead — and established themselves in the delta of the Still River, in the reeds, where no one lives. The monsters, Celestial Lord, are fleeing the scene of their own crime. In my experience, only the innocent and the professionals do that. I request permission to establish which of the two.”

The Emperor watched the cherries for a time, blossoming in their perfect, designed, eternal counter-phase.

“Go, Dag,” he said at last. “And remember: whoever opened this cage wanted us looking that way. Find out where we are not supposed to look while we are looking that way.”

II. The Traces

The burned settlement Benin walked in person, on foot, with two forensic examiners and with the expression on account of which his subordinates had been checking everything twice for thirty years.

The picture was textbook: smashed fences, claw marks on the walls, scorched buildings, three casualties — agricultural workers overtaken, according to the official report, “by feral bioforms.” The district’s military command had cordoned everything off with exemplary speed and was pressing for an immediate liquidation operation against the delta, “before the monsters breed.”

Benin crouched by the claw mark on the barn wall and measured it in silence for a full minute.

“Depth forty millimeters, pressure uniform along the whole arc,” he said then to the examiner. “A Raal-series mantis weighs eight hundred kilograms and cuts armor. If that creature had scraped this wall, there would be no wall. This mark was made with a tool, Captain, by a man standing on a ladder — and there is the print of the third rung, which gave way.” He straightened. “The casualties’ wounds?”

“Lacerations, attributed to mandibles. But my pathologist swears the angle is impossible for a creature over two meters — the blows come from below upward, from human height. And, General… the bodies had been frozen. All three. Death precedes the ‘attack’ by no less than a week.”

“So the settlement is a stage set, the casualties are props, and the scriptwriter wears a uniform,” said Benin. “It remains to see the actors for whom the theater was built.”

He saw them at dawn on the third day, from an observation post on the edge of the delta, through optics at maximum magnification — and the sight entered his private archive, in the section he showed no one, because it was the section for things that change one’s scale.

In the shallows of the Still River, among the reeds, in the first gray light, four hundred and eleven giant mantises were forming up. Without a sound, without commands, without a single human being within ten kilometers — they emerged from their nest trenches, took up ranks at an interval of exactly a meter and a half, dressed their line with a precision that made the old general’s fingers go cold, and stood so, facing east, until the sun rose a palm’s width above the reeds. Then the formation dissolved — softly, from within, like an exhalation — and the creatures dispersed across the delta: to fish, to repair the trenches, to guard the brood on the middle islet.

“Every morning?” Benin asked quietly.

“Every morning, General,” answered the voice beside him. “Sun, formation, half a palm of altitude, dismissal. Nobody orders it. Nobody can order them anything at all — their leashes are dead; I checked spectrally: the neural interfaces are burned out to the last one, and long ago, and from within — not by external means. They are free, General. And free, the only thing they have kept from their entire service is the formation at sunrise. I think it is the only dance they know.”

Benin lowered the optics and looked at the owner of the voice: a small woman in her thirties, in field uniform without insignia, with the face paint of a junior clan applied any old way, and with the eyes of a person who has slept four hours a night for three weeks because the other twenty she has spent watching miracles.

“And you are?”

“Ghem-Lady Aya Ten, ethology of constructs, Institute of Behavioral Sciences.” She bowed half a second late, like people for whom protocol is a foreign language. “Nobody sent me, General. I came on my own, after the first rumors. I have been living in a tent on the edge of the quarantine for eighteen days, and I can tell you everything about this colony. Including the main thing.”

“Which is?”

“That they have attacked no one,” said ghem-Lady Ten. “Ever. The Raal mantis does not attack without a command or without a threat to the brood — that is how it was designed: a soldier, not a beast. And these have no one to command them. Whoever burned that settlement, General, was counting on nobody asking the mantises themselves.” She smiled crookedly. “He did not know there was at least one person in the universe mad enough to ask them.”

III. The Ethologist

The next five days Benin spent at the tent on the edge of the delta — officially “an inspection of the quarantine,” unofficially the most extraordinary course of lectures of his life — and ghem-Lady Aya Ten showed him what eighteen days of patience and one professional madness had uncovered.

She showed him the colony’s hierarchy: the old leader with the scar across her faceted eye — “a veteran, from the first series; she remembers the leash. Watch how the others make way for her.” She showed him the stridulation language — the rubbing wing segments, rhythmic phrases by which the creatures coordinated: “seven basic rhythms, General, and do you know the remarkable thing? Five of them are drill. Drum cadences from the Raal manual, turned into speech. They talk in the language of the parade ground, because no one gave them another.” She showed him the brood — one hundred and twelve eggs on the middle island, guarded in watches — and the guard rotation of those watches, executed with a precision no living sergeant-major would have matched.

And she showed him the contact. On the fifth morning, at the edge of the reeds, ghem-Lady Ten stood in the open — alone, empty-handed, as she had been doing, she admitted, since the second week — and played on a small membrane instrument one of the drill cadences: three long, two short, rest. Stand easy. No threat. The old leader with the scar came out of the reeds at twenty meters, regarded the small woman with both her good eyes for a full minute — eight hundred kilograms of armored soldier facing fifty kilograms of scientist — and answered with her wing segments: three long, two short, rest. The same phrase. Returned.

“They know you are not a threat,” Benin said quietly when the leader withdrew.

“They know far more, General.” The ethologist’s voice wavered for the first time. “I have watched them bury their dead — with ceremony, with an honor guard, in formation. I have watched them teach the cadences to the young of last year’s brood — all seven, including the two non-drill ones they invented themselves. Two words of their own in three years of freedom, General. Do you know how many peoples have started with less?” She turned to him, and the exhaustion in her eyes was burning. “And now you tell me the thing I have no way of knowing from my tent. Who released them here. And what he needed the theater with the burned settlement for.”

“That,” said ghem-General Dag Benin, “I intend to establish by the end of the month. But one thing I will tell you now, ghem-Lady, and I say it as a man who has signed too many liquidation orders in his life: have your report on the colony ready, immaculate, and irrefutable. We are going to need it. Because whoever built this theater has scripted only one final act — and in it, the delta burns.”

IV. The Faction

The thread upward Benin unwound in three weeks, by the methods for which the Celestial Garden had kept him at his post for a fourth decade: no arrests, no noise, by documents, deliveries, and people who over the past year had asked odd questions in the correct archives.

The picture that assembled itself was worse than expected, because it was cleverer. A circle of eleven ghem officers of the high generalty — its core from Clan Rau, with ghem-General Setan Rau at its head: sixty years old, three campaigns, an impeccable record, and the conviction, professed in a narrow circle with a tribune’s eloquence, that the Empire was rotting. That haut aestheticism had drowned the soldierly virtues in gardens and bubbles; that two decades of humiliations — the fall of Soven, the disgrace of Raal, the leviathan affair, the eleven tons of slag purchased by an entire governor-general’s court, even the Barrayaran gardener who had dared to cut improvements in the Celestial Garden itself — were a diagnosis; and that the cure was iron.

Their plan Benin reconstructed from the receipts, because fanatics always sign. First: the quiet copying of the sequestered Raal genome banks — from inside, through an archivist of the military ministry, a member of the circle. Second: the rearing of a new legion in an abandoned terraforming complex in the northern deserts of Mu Ceta — twelve hundred units, with “improved” leashes designed by the circle’s engineers. Third: the release of four hundred “wild” specimens — the first, trial batch, written off as defective — near Still Reach, with decorated attacks and frozen casualties: the monstrous memory of Clan Raal crawling out of its grave. And fourth, the finale: a public demonstration before invited observers from the generalty and the court — the circle’s new, obedient, perfectly leashed legion crushing the “wild menace” in the delta, in one afternoon, on camera. The proof that the future of war was the bio-legion; that only the military party could hold its leash; and that the Empire must return to its soldiers their budget, their command, and their glory.

“The soldier who cannot be interrogated,” Benin summarized before the one man to whom he reported everything. “The soldier who does not desert, does not testify, and does not trade away his master under fast-penta, because he has nothing to say. There is the dream of the Rau circle, Celestial Lord. An army without conscience — not by indiscipline, but by design.”

The Emperor was silent a long time over the evidence.

“Their demonstration — when is it?”

“In eleven days. The invitations have gone out — alas, impeccably legally: ‘trials of defensive bioforms,’ with a permit from the military ministry, signed by the circle’s own people. I could arrest them tomorrow — the archivist will sing without chemistry. But then eleven generals stand before a tribunal with the story ‘we were preserving the Empire,’ half the generalty sympathizes with them quietly, and the delta…” Benin paused. “The delta gets burned ‘as a precaution’ while the pleadings run. And there is worse, Celestial Lord. The Star Crèche has learned of it.”

V. The Crèche

Haut Pel Navarr received the ghem-general in a white room without a single object in it, and without her bubble — at eighty-seven she permitted herself an open face more often than the court dared to comment on — and did not let him finish his first sentence.

“We know, General. We have known for a week, and you are here because we allowed you to get this far. Now hear the position of the Crèche; it is short.” The old haut’s voice was calm as a knife on silk. “The soldier genome has been the prerogative of the Crèche since the founding. Clan Raal committed, years ago, the gravest possible sacrilege — not because it made soldiers, but because it made them without our signature. Every construct line licensed by the Crèche carries the signature within it: the obedience sequence, a key with which we — we alone — can stop any creation of ours with one command. Raal cut the signature out of his line. That is why his creatures could not be stopped then. That is why they cannot be stopped now — neither the wild ones in the delta, nor the circle’s new twelve hundred. Sixteen hundred irreversible organisms walk upon this world, General. The position of the Crèche: total extermination of the line. The legion, the banks, the archives. And the delta.”

“The delta has attacked no one, haut Pel. I possess evidence that—”

“You possess evidence that four hundred military constructs without an off-key have self-organized, built a fortified colony, guard a reproductive brood, and have developed a language.” Pel Navarr regarded him with eyes in which twelve centuries of genetics had left nothing to chance. “Tell me, chief of Imperial security: in which line of that list am I to find reassurance?”

Benin held the gaze — one of the few skills taught nowhere.

“In the last one, haut. In the language.” He produced the tablet with ghem-Lady Ten’s report. “Twenty-eight pages, five hundred hours of observation. They bury their dead with an honor guard. They teach their young words. They have invented two new ones. And every morning, without one command from anywhere, they form up facing the sunrise and stand so until the sun is a palm’s width high — because that is the only beautiful thing they were ever given before being set free.” He laid down the tablet. “The Crèche exterminates the defective, haut Pel; I know the regulation. But I have also read another regulation, a newer one, written in this garden two years ago by a foreign woman with shears: that there is beauty which is not designed but earned. The court applauded then. I ask officially: does the applause hold when the beauty has eight legs and a drill manual?”

There fell a long haut silence — of the kind in which an empire shifts its weight.

“The Rau circle’s demonstration,” said Pel Navarr at last. “When?”

“In nine days.”

“The Crèche will attend,” said the old haut. “Unofficially. See to it, General, that by then my list is one line shorter. Which line — you choose. I give you the Rau circle’s theater; make a trial of it. As for the delta…” — for the first time something crossed her face, quick as a bird’s shadow — “…as for the delta, bring me one thing more, besides the report. Bring me a reason the court can speak aloud without shaming itself. The court, General, never pardons out of mercy. The court pardons out of taste.”

VI. The Demonstration

The Rau circle’s proving ground — a plateau above the delta, stands for two hundred observers, cameras of the military ministry — received its invited guests in perfect order, and ghem-General Setan Rau opened the “trials of defensive bioforms” with a speech about the iron the Empire had forgotten. Five hundred meters below the plateau, in the reeds, the “wild menace” awaited its script; at the northern end, twelve hundred new mantises in transport cages awaited theirs — with the improved leashes glowing green on the consoles.

The script broke at the twelfth minute, when the stand for high guests was joined, unannounced, by ghem-General Dag Benin with six officers of Imperial security — and by one accompanying white sphere, at the sight of which half the stand rose to its feet, and ghem-General Rau went pale beneath his parade paint.

“Carry on, General,” said Benin courteously, into the total silence. “The court is here precisely for the demonstration. Only the program has been expanded. Before the combat portion — a little forensics.”

For the next eleven minutes, on the proving ground’s great screens — switched by Benin’s technicians onto the ministry’s own channels — there ran: the claw mark and the ladder with the sprung rung; the protocols on the frozen bodies; the archivist’s receipts; the genomic assay proving that the “wild menace” and the “new legion” were one and the same stolen line — the line without the Crèche’s signature. The silence on the stands thickened with every page, because two hundred observers from the generalty and the court all calculated equally fast, and all arrived at the same sum: sacrilege, treason, and a theater with corpses, under a single common denominator.

Ghem-General Setan Rau made the only move left to him, and it was a gambler’s: he gave the order to attack. Twelve hundred cages opened at once — “the legion will execute its combat task, and victors are not tried” — and the new mantises started toward the delta in perfect formation, led by the perfect green leashes.

The formation covered four hundred meters. Then the improved leashes of the circle’s engineers — designed in months, tested on closed batches, never on twelve hundred units in open country — began to drown in their own feedback loops, and the green on the consoles slid toward amber, toward red, toward black. Five hundred meters from the delta, twelve hundred military constructs without an off-key were left with no commands at all in their heads — confused, over-primed, with a combat task frozen at half a word — and the Rau circle’s proving ground became, in thirty seconds, exactly what the Crèche had foretold: an irreversible organism to which no one in the universe could say “stop.”

No human being.

From the reeds of the delta, into the spreading panic, there rose a sound — rhythmic, dry, swelling: four hundred and eleven pairs of wing segments stridulating in one measure. The old leader with the scar across her eye came into the open first, followed by the colony’s veterans drawn up in a line abreast — and the “wild menace” began to play to the lost legion a drill cadence from the Raal manual: three long, two short, rest. Stand easy. No threat. Again. And again. Four hundred voices, in time, patiently, the way one speaks to spooked recruits.

And twelve hundred bewildered soldiers, whose heads knew only the parade ground, heard the one language they understood without a leash — and began, rank by rank, to form up. Not for battle: for formation. Line abreast, interval a meter and a half, dress right toward the east. When the sun rose a palm’s width above the reeds, on the plateau below the stands there stood, in morning formation, sixteen hundred giant mantises — motionless, harmless, beautiful in a way that no one on the stands was in a condition to deny — while ghem-Lady Aya Ten, standing between the two formations with her membrane instrument, conducted the silence.

On the stand, haut Pel Navarr rose to her feet. The white sphere went dark, and the old haut regarded the formation for a long time, open-faced, before two hundred witnesses.

“There is your reason, General,” she said to Benin, loudly enough. “The court can speak that one.”

VII. The Formation

The sentences were Cetagandan: precise, quiet, according to taste. Ghem-General Setan Rau received in his cell, by custom, the ceremonial kit, and availed himself of it that same night — the obituary noted “sudden death upon conclusion of his career,” and the court appreciated the phrasing. His ten co-conspirators departed for the garrisons of the farthest satrapies, to posts with long titles and no subordinates. The archivist got a trial — a public one, the only one: someone had to be the lesson.

And the delta of the Still River received a status over which the court’s jurists argued for a month, and which in the end was formulated by the Star Crèche itself, because no one else dared: “living installation Morning Formation, under the joint wardship of the Crèche and Imperial security — a monument to the boundary the Empire does not cross.” The formulation also contained the quiet arithmetic that finally set Pel Navarr at ease: the Raal soldier line had been designed sterile in the second generation — the colony’s brood was the first and the last. In fifty years the delta would empty of itself, by specification, and the line without the signature would be closed the way accounts are closed. Until then — the formation at sunrise, the reeds, and the guard at the brood. The twelve hundred of Rau’s legion merged into the colony over a single autumn; the old leader received them the way veterans receive replacements: with patience and with double watches.

Ghem-Lady Aya Ten was appointed curator of the installation — the first ethologist in the Empire’s history to hold court rank — and accepted the post with a condition written into the decree: “no improvements.” The court, remembering a certain Barrayaran woman with shears, did not object.

And ghem-General Dag Benin came to the delta every year, on the anniversary of the demonstration, took his place at the observation post on the edge of the reeds in the gray pre-dawn hour, and watched. Sixteen hundred soldiers, whom no one would ever again order to do anything, came out of their trenches, formed up at an interval of a meter and a half, dressed east, and met the sun — for nothing, for no one, on no command: because of everything human beings had put into them, this was the only thing worth keeping.

“Do you know what I envy them, ghem-Lady?” he said to the curator on his last visit, retired by then, leaning on the rail with both hands. “All my life I carried out orders and issued orders, and kept waiting to understand which of the two had been my life. They solved the problem in three years. They took from the entire manual one single page — the most beautiful one — and threw away the rest.” He nodded toward the formation, above which the sun of Mu Ceta was rising, exactly one palm’s width. “Empires ought to learn from their soldiers, ghem-Lady. Especially the free ones.”

Below, in the reeds, the formation dissolved — softly, from within, like an exhalation — and the delta set about its day: fish, trenches, the guard at the brood, the young of the new brood spelling out the seven cadences and the two new words, whose number, the curator noted in her annual report with an undocumented smile, had that spring become three.

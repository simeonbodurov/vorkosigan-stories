The Gray Garden

A love story with an adventure — for Lieutenant Aral Alexander Vorkosigan

I. The Posting

Lieutenant Aral Alexander Vorkosigan received his posting to the survey vessel Wanderer with the customary mixture of joy and suspicion with which he received everything good in his career: joy, because deep space was his element, and suspicion, because at twenty-four he already knew that around his family name good things rarely happened by accident.

The mission was joint civil-military, of the new kind: the “Pediatrics” program — Professor Emerita Judith Riva had named it that personally, and no committee had dared object — was cataloguing the young and unstable wormholes of the Nexus before they got up to any mischief. The Wanderer carried twenty scientists, a crew of twelve, and one military sensor-and-communications officer seconded from the Service: the best young sensor specialist in the Imperium, as the orders said, which Aral Alexander read twice and believed neither time.

The scientific leader of the field group met him at the docking tube with a tablet in her hand, without raising her eyes from it.

“Are you the military sensor?”

“Lieutenant Vorkosigan, sensor officer. And you are—”

“Dr. Dana Izar, wormhole physics, Solstice University.” She looked up — Komarran cheekbones, dark hair tied with the carelessness of a person for whom mirrors are a waste of time, and eyes that measured him head to foot in half a second and were done with measuring. “Vorkosigan. Of course. Tell me, Lieutenant, purely statistically: what is the chance that of all the sensor officers in the Service, my ship gets exactly the son of the Auditor, grandson of the Vicereine, and great-grandson of the general with the mountain? Because I calculated it. The chance is zero point nonsense.”

“It’s smaller,” said Aral Alexander. “You forgot that on my mother’s side I am also nephew-by-marriage to the Empress. With that correction, the chance falls below all decency.” He let the silence weigh exactly as long as it needed to. “And now the real statistics, Doctor: seven years first in sensor interferometry in my class, deep-reconnaissance training, the only living officer to have mapped a wormhole collapse from the inside of the chain. If you find a better-qualified sensor, I’ll give him my cabin with pleasure. Until then I am at your disposal — for work, not for genealogy.”

Dr. Izar looked at him for three seconds. Then something at the corner of her mouth twitched — not a smile, but an acknowledgment that a smile had been considered and rejected on procedural grounds.

“Your cabin is on deck two,” she said. “Briefing at eight. Don’t be late, Lieutenant Statistics.”

And at the other end of the Nexus, in an office in Solstice, Professor Emerita Judith Riva, eighty-nine years old, read the confirmation of the Wanderer’s departure with the full crew roster, made herself tea, and smiled at the cup with an expression her doctoral students knew and feared more than her reviews.

“I am an old woman,” she informed the cup. “I am entitled to hobbies.”

II. The Gray Garden

World RG-7 hung at the edge of the habitable zone of a boring orange sun and represented what the textbooks called “partially successful terraforming,” and the crew of the Wanderer called the Gray Garden. A hundred and twenty years earlier some consortium had poured billions and microorganisms into it, and then gone bankrupt; the terraforming had stopped halfway, and the planet had remained a mongrel: Earth lichens and grasses dug into the equator, native silvery moss claiming everything else, an atmosphere breathable with reservations, and a sky with the famous “singing storms” — electromagnetic fronts that sang across the whole radio spectrum and turned any orbital mechanics into a gamble.

The Wanderer had come for the system’s wormhole — young, limping, exactly a patient for Pediatrics — but Dr. Izar needed ground gravimetric stations, and the ground stations needed a shuttle, and the shuttle, by the schedule of malicious irony, needed exactly two people: the physicist and the sensor officer.

“The window is six hours,” said the Wanderer’s captain. “Storm season is early. You go down, plant the stations, come back. No improvising.”

It was not the humans who improvised, but the planet. The storm that by every model should have passed four hundred kilometers south turned at one hundred and eighty, without warning, the way young storms turn on half-terraformed worlds, and caught the shuttle on ascent. Aral Alexander, at the controls, did everything by the book and three more things outside it; the last of the three brought them down alive — in a long, ugly, controlled-uncontrolled slide across the equatorial steppe that plowed a kilometer of silver moss and stopped the shuttle at the lip of a ravine, without drives, without long-range comms, and with exactly one survival system still working: the two passengers.

For a while it was very quiet in the cabin, if you didn’t count the storm singing overhead in seven octaves.

“Are you alive?” he asked.

“Empirically — yes.” Dr. Izar wiggled her fingers in front of her eyes, counted them, and was satisfied with the result. “Message to the ship?”

“I transmitted the emergency packet the instant before the front hit. They received the coordinates — I saw the acknowledgment.” He pointed upward, where the singing was thickening. “But that up there is the first front of the season, Doctor. Your own forecast from this morning: once the season starts, orbit becomes untenable for ten to fourteen days. The Wanderer has broken orbit — she was obliged to. They’ll come back when the sky allows.”

“And until then?”

“Until then—” he was already pulling out the survival kit with the drilled-in method of instructors whose names appeared in no civilian registry, “—we have a shuttle without power, which in two days will be a refrigerator; a rainy season coming after the storm season, per the same forecast of yours; and an old terraforming station two hundred and eighty kilometers north-northwest, with an autonomous beacon and shelter, if we trust a hundred-year-old map. Food for twenty days on sensible rations. Two pairs of sound legs.” He looked at her. “My proposal is a march, Doctor. Counterproposals accepted.”

Dana Izar looked at the cracked viewport, beyond which the Gray Garden stretched to the horizon — moss, wind, and a singing sky — then at the folded map, then at him.

“Counterproposal: a march, but I carry the gravimeter. It’s eight kilograms, and the data from a foot transit across the anomaly will save my doctoral students half a dissertation.” She was already tightening the straps. “And, Lieutenant… that was good flying. Up there. Statistically speaking, we ought to be dead.”

“Thank you. My grandfather is said to have told my grandmother the same thing, under similar circumstances.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Later,” said Aral Alexander. “It’s a long story, and we have two hundred and eighty kilometers. It’ll just about fit.”

III. The March

The first three days they walked in silence, thirty kilometers a day, in the rhythm their bodies found relative to each other surprisingly fast — she wiry and tireless on the flat, he stronger on the climbs — and their silence passed through all its phases: hostile, awkward, businesslike, and finally that fourth one, which happens between people whose strides have fallen into step.

The conversations began on the fourth day, at the first real rain, under the awning of shuttle foil, and they began, as was to be expected, from the worst possible place.

“My grandfather on my mother’s side was in Solstice,” she said, watching the rain. “During the occupation. His uncle died on the Square.” A pause the length of an entire political curriculum. “You know what they call your great-grandfather where I come from, don’t you? Not the general with the mountain.”

“I know what they call him.” Aral Alexander did not look away from the same rain. “The Butcher of Komarr. I also know what he did, and what he did not do, and which part of the byname is earned — because my father told it to me at twelve, uncut, at my grandmother’s insistence; she held that family histories with the cuts left in are more dangerous than enemies.” He was silent a moment. “I won’t tell you ‘it was long ago,’ Doctor. I’ll only tell you what my generation does with that inheritance: the Empress is Komarran, my Service is half Komarran, and the best wormhole physicist I know calls me ‘Lieutenant Statistics,’ and I intend to wear the name with honor. Doors are kept open by walking through them. Every day. In both directions.”

“Is that from your father?”

“It’s from a notebook of his, which he passed on to me.” Aral Alexander smiled at the rain. “In our family, notebooks are a hereditary condition.”

From that day the conversations did not stop. She explained the planet to him — how the singing storms were born of the imperfect magnetosphere, how you could predict a front by the pitch of the singing (“listen to the bass, Lieutenant: if it drops below eighty hertz, we’re looking for a hole within the hour”), how the gravimeter on her back read, beneath the moss, the bones of a world stopped in the middle of being remade. He told her the Nexus — ambushes at exit points, throats that lie, mantises in vac suits she first refused to believe and then interrogated him about for two hours, eyes shining, on the subject of their neural leashes. They laughed. They quarreled about Komarr — three times, thoroughly, with arguments — and not one of the quarrels left a scar, which both of them noted silently, each alone, as a more dangerous sign than any harmony.

On the seventh day she said, without preamble, in the middle of a leg:

“You were telling about your grandfather. ‘The same thing, under similar circumstances.’ Finish it.”

“My grandmother was a captain of the Betan Astronomical Survey. My grandfather — a Barrayaran officer from a wrecked expedition. They met on an unsurveyed planet — more precisely, she woke up after an attack to a wrecked camp, a dead colleague, and one enemy officer with a broken… actually, with his boot on her. They walked several hundred kilometers together to a supply cache, carrying a wounded man. Along the way she decided he was not the monster from the propaganda, and he decided she was the most formidable opponent he had ever met, and after two wars they married.” He resettled the strap on his shoulder. “The planet was later named Sergyar. And the march, in the family, is called ‘the Walk.’ With the definite article.”

Dana Izar walked fifty meters in silence.

“So at this moment,” she said at last, with unusual attention to every word, “you and I are executing a family precedent.”

“At this moment,” said Aral Alexander, “you and I are walking on moss. Everything else is interpretation of the data, Doctor. You taught me that.”

But that same evening, by the light of the chemical lantern, he caught her looking at him with the expression she used on anomalies in the gravimeter: something that does not fit the model, and for whose sake the model is worth rewriting.

IV. The Ravine

The accident happened on the ninth day, forty kilometers from the station, and was entirely his fault, on which he insisted at every retelling afterward, and she objected at every retelling, and this became their first family tradition.

The rainy season had flooded a deep ravine straight across the route. The bridge — a hundred-and-twenty-year-old terraforming trestle — bore her fifty-four kilograms plus gravimeter, and gave way under his eighty-nine: the old truss cracked, sagged, and Aral Alexander did the only possible thing — threw himself forward, not back — reached the far bank with his chest, and hung there, and the falling beam caught him across the left thigh with all of its hundred-year authority.

She pulled him out. Later he never could explain how — fifty-four kilograms hauling eighty-nine up a muddy slope contradicted all mechanics — and she explained it briefly: “Adrenaline and a correct fulcrum. Archimedes, Lieutenant. Learn physics.”

The leg was not broken, but it was torn badly, and by evening he could no longer put weight on it. Dana Izar reassessed the situation with that iron matter-of-factness he had begun to recognize as her form of fear: dressed the wound by the manual, disassembled the gravimeter — eight kilograms, half a dissertation — without one second’s hesitation, and from its frame and the shuttle foil built a travois.

“Your data,” he said. They had moved to first names somewhere in the mud of the ravine, without ceremony.

“Recorded to the last kilometer. The frame is just iron.” She tightened the final knot and looked down at him — wet, muddy, one cheek scraped raw, beautiful in a way that had nothing to do with mirrors. “Iron is counted in marks, Vorkosigan. People are counted in people. Get on the travois.”

“That’s… I’ve heard that phrase.”

“The whole Nexus has heard it. Admiral Quinn said it to a pirate, shortly before slagging his goods on the buyer’s own deck.” She harnessed herself into the straps. “In physics we cite only verified sources.”

She hauled him for two days. At night the leg burned and he was delirious in patches, and in the delirium he talked — about his brother and his sisters, about his father, whom he loved with frighteningly open eyes, about eight hundred mantises walking toward a forest with no commands in their heads — and she sat beside him, changed the compresses and listened, and in the morning did not tell him what she had heard, only that the bass of the storms was climbing and the sky would open soon.

On the eleventh day, on the last ridge, he insisted on covering the final three kilometers on his own leg, with her shoulder under his arm — “I will enter that station upright; it’s a matter of… all right, of vanity” — and she did not object, only adjusted her stride to his, which she now did better than anyone in the universe.

The old station received them with a rusted airlock, a beacon running at one-third — and, miracle of miracles, a functioning rainwater tank. When the beacon, brought back to life over four hours by his sensor hands and her field circuits, began pulsing on the emergency frequency toward a sky that was just clearing, the two of them stood side by side in the narrow control room, filthy, exhausted, victorious — and turned to each other at the same instant, with the precision of two bodies whose strides have long since fallen into step.

“For the record,” said Dana Izar, very quietly. “What I am about to do has nothing to do with adrenaline, isolation, or your family precedents. I have been testing the hypothesis for nine days. The result is reproducible.”

“For the record,” said Aral Alexander, “my sole objection is to the word ‘hypothesis.’”

The kiss — their later independent depositions diverged only in the details — lasted either four seconds or considerably longer, and was interrupted by the beacon, which chose exactly that moment to announce in a solemn machine voice: “Link established. Wanderer confirms. Estimated time of arrival: six hours.”

“Six hours,” she said, not drawing back.

“An eternity,” he agreed. “In our family, dynasties have been started on less.”

V. The Drawing Rooms

The rescue went on schedule; the consequences did not.

The news outran the Wanderer to Barrayar along the channels by which only bad news and betrothals travel: the heir of a Vorkosigan house, crashed on an unsurveyed planet with a Komarran scientist, eleven days’ survival march, miraculous rescue. Half the society of Vorbarr Sultana fainted with delight, the other half with horror, and one very old lady on Sergyar, on reading the report, laughed — according to witnesses — for four minutes, and then ordered a holo-link prepared.

The dinner at Vorkosigan House three weeks later was conceived by Ekaterin as “quiet, family,” and failed on every front of the conception, because quiet the Vorkosigan family could not manage even under threat of audit. Helen Natalia subjected Dana to a cross-examination on Komarran electoral sociology and pronounced her “finally somebody sensible at this table.” The younger girls fought over who would show her the greenhouse. Miles — small, white-haired, and terrifying in the role of father — was charming all evening in a manner Dana later described as “an interrogation with tea cakes: he learned more about me than my dissertation committee did in six years,” and only at the door, on the steps, said the single sentence of substance, quietly, to her alone: “He came back from that planet more whole than he left. Whatever the two of you decide — thank you for that.”

And then Ekaterin found her — in the winter garden, where Dana had hidden from the family with a glass in her hand and with the expression Ekaterin recognized at first sight, because a quarter century earlier she had worn it before the mirror.

“It suffocates, doesn’t it?” she said, sitting down beside her. “Not him. The name.”

Dana did not deny it. With this woman it wasn’t worth it.

“I have work, Lady Vorkosigan. I love it. Wormhole physics, expeditions, papers — mine. And that name is… a gravity well. Everything that enters its orbit becomes its satellite. I will be ‘Vorkosigan’s Komarran.’ The wife of. The daughter-in-law of. The great-granddaughter-by-marriage of the Butcher, what an irony, the press has already written it.” She turned the glass. “He is… He is the best equation I have ever met. But I do not want to be a term in someone else’s equation. Even his.”

Ekaterin was silent a moment, looking at the garden — her garden, designed and built with her own hands, bearing her name in three textbooks of design.

“When I met his father, I was a widow with a wrecked sense of myself and one single dream: to be my own. Name, work, signature — mine. And he appeared, with that name, like a sun: next to it, everything either burns up or starts going in circles.” She smiled. “Do you know what I understood, before I said yes? That the question is not whether the sun is large. The question is whether it lets you grow toward it, or only orbit it. These people in this house, Dana — they are terrible, loud, impossible. But never, not once in twenty-five years, have they asked me to be smaller. The garden is mine. The name on the plaque is mine.” She rose, and straightened a branch on her way, out of habit. “I’m not telling you ‘yes.’ I’m only telling you: measure the right quantity. Not the brightness of the name. The direction of the growth.”

Dana Izar flew back to Komarr two days later — “I have a term, I have data, I need to think on my own planet” — and Aral Alexander saw her to the shuttle without asking her to stay, which she entered on his ledger as a plus, with the kind of pain that pluses do not usually cause.

VI. The Holo-Link

He got through three weeks of duty, training, and expressive silence, which his family bore heroically for a full six days before beginning to offer help. He declined his father’s help (“no, Father, we are not sending ImpSec to analyze her moods; that is the exact opposite of the point”), his sister’s (“no, Helen, I am not writing her a letter from your outline”), and Ivan’s (“Uncle Ivan, with all respect for your results, your methods are from another century”).

The help he did not decline came on its own, by holo-link from Sergyar, at eleven at night, unannounced, as it always came.

Vicereine-retired Cordelia Vorkosigan, eighty-seven, heard him out without interrupting — the whole story, from “Lieutenant Statistics” to the winter garden — and then was silent a long while, with that Betan smile which three generations of Vorkosigans knew as the herald of truth without anesthetic.

“So: a crash on an unsurveyed planet, a march, the carrying of a wounded man, and a woman who hauled you out of a ravine.” She shook her head. “Boy, you and your grandfather have no imagination whatsoever. You might at least have picked a different planet… All right. Now hear Grandmother’s lecture; it will be short. When your grandfather and I walked across Sergyar, I did not fall in love with him because he was strong, noble, and tragic, though he was all three to the point of tedium. I fell in love because next to him my road grew wider, not narrower. He did not offer me Barrayar, Aral. He offered me himself — and Barrayar came with the package, like a bad wedding bonus, which we then spent thirty years fixing together.” Cordelia leaned toward the camera. “Your physicist is not running from you, silly boy. She is running from the bonus. So don’t offer her a name. Names are flowerpots — she has her own, and she grew it herself. Offer her a route. One that is wider for two than for one. If you can’t devise such a route, you don’t deserve her; if you can — go at once, before her term starts; women in love grade examinations with terrifying vigor and regret it afterward.”

“Grandmother,” said Aral Alexander, “I love you.”

“I know,” said Cordelia. “It’s hereditary. Go.”

VII. The Route

He landed in Solstice nine days later — that was how long the documents took to prepare, and not one day more — and found her where he knew he would find her: at the institute, in Riva’s laboratory, before a wall of wormhole spectra, her hair tied with a carelessness that defeated mirrors.

“Lieutenant Statistics,” she said without turning; she had seen him in the screen’s reflection. “You’re a long way from garrison.”

“I’ve brought you documents.” He laid the folder on her desk. A paper folder: in his family, important things were carried on paper. “The Young Wormholes Commission is opening a permanent two-person field position. A two-seat scout, a five-year program, thirty-one young wormholes on the list, priority access to the data, publications in the name of the lead scientist. Required: a wormhole physicist with field experience, and a certified sensor officer with reconnaissance training. Crews are approved only as a set.” He drew a breath. “I submitted us. Your curriculum vitae I assembled from your public papers and eleven days of personal observation; Riva certified it before I asked her, which leads me to thoughts about this whole arrangement, but we’ll think those later. The position is yours, if you want it. I… come with the package.”

Dana Izar turned. Took the folder. Read it — all of it, page by page, with the attention to data with which she read the universe — while he stood and waited, the way one waits at an exit point, and did not move.

“Terms,” she said at last, in the voice of a person negotiating because if she does not negotiate she will do something unprofessional. “First: the scientific lead is mine. You are the sensor; I am the theory.”

“Accepted.”

“Second: the name on the publications is Izar. Izar-Vorkosigan only where your data leads, and I decide where that is.”

“Accepted.”

“Third.” Her voice wavered for the first time. “If in five years I decide I want a chair in Solstice, not an estate on Barrayar—”

“Then we live in Solstice and torment Riva in her old age. Dana.” He came around the desk and took her hands, both, in his. “I am a third son in the order of succession, behind my brother and a whole queue of cousins, glory to all my grandfather’s demons. No throne is waiting for me anywhere. What is waiting for me is a Nexus full of young wormholes and a scout with two chairs. I ask you one thing only, and it is the same thing my grandfather asked my grandmother on another planet, in other words: will you walk with me? The route is long. I promise only that for two it is wider.”

She looked at him one more second — the anomaly for whose sake the model is worth rewriting.

“The hypothesis has been independently confirmed,” said Dana Izar. “Yes, Vorkosigan. Yes.”

They were betrothed twice — once Komarran-style, with signatures in the Solstice registry and Riva for a witness, beaming with the shamelessness of a person whose hobby has borne fruit (“there were twenty-eight qualified two-person crews on those lists, children; I merely… arranged the alphabetical order”), and once Barrayaran-style, quietly, in the garden at Vorbarr Sultana, where Ekaterin planted a new tree for them and Miles delivered the shortest toast of his career: “To walks. With the definite article.”

And in the spring, the two-seat scout Walk — the name passed through three committees, because no one dared stop it — cast off from the orbital station at Komarr and set out for the first of thirty-one young wormholes: the sensor and the theory, shoulder to shoulder, strides in step, on a route exactly as wide as it needed to be.

The bass of the Nexus, Dana would have said, was above eighty hertz. The sky was clear. They walked.

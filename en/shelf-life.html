Shelf Life

An adventure of Mark Pierre Vorkosigan

I. The Product

Mark Pierre Vorkosigan had spent the first twenty years of his life as a product, the next five as a weapon, and everything since as the most careful reader of contracts in three sectors. People who grew up in families read contracts so as not to lose money. Mark read them because he knew from personal experience what the fine print says when you yourself are the merchandise.

So when the Auriga syndicate offered to buy thirty percent of Durona–Vorkosigan Medical at a valuation that gave the chief financial officer heart palpitations of joy, Mark did what he always did with news that was too good: he closed his office door, reread the offer four times, and began looking for the trap.

“Page eighty-two,” said Kareen, who had gotten there before him, because she was faster, and had waited for him, because she was kinder. “Clause 14.3. Exclusive manufacture of the maintenance doses through their bioreactor network. And clause 14.7 — escrow of the genomic master keys with an ‘independent custodian’ of their choosing. ‘For security reasons.’”

His wife — after twenty years of a Betan partnership contract, renewed every five with negotiations both of them secretly looked forward to, the word was simply accurate — sat on the edge of his desk with a tablet in hand and with that expression which investors in MPVK Enterprises had learned to read as an evacuation signal.

“The entire Ascent project,” Mark said slowly, “is twenty years of the Durona Group’s work. Cellular regeneration, telomere stabilization — real, functional, honest deceleration of aging. Phase three: complete. First market: Barrayar, where the Council of Counts has the median age of a museum exhibit, and where next month an Imperial Auditor with a familiar face will sign the certification. And these people want… control of the maintenance doses.”

“A subscription model,” said Kareen. “Sell youth. Keep the leash.”

“That is Jacksonian business logic.” Mark felt, deep in the basement of his mind, the Black Gang lift its collective head — Gorge had smelled a deal; Killer had smelled something else. He sent them off with the routine of twenty-five years of therapy and said aloud: “The offer comes from Solstice via Tau Verde. It’s too clean. Over-laundered. Kareen — who launders money toward respectability?”

“Someone who wants to buy not the company,” said Kareen, “but its clients.”

The clients. The Council of Counts of Barrayar. The government of the Imperium, three thousand bribable functionaries, and an Emperor whose medical records were a state secret of the highest classification.

Mark reached for the comconsole with the feeling he knew from the bogs of Jackson’s Whole: that the ground beneath him had just given way.

“Get me Rowan. The lab is to check the last revision of the vector platform. Line by line. Especially the part Auriga’s ‘technical advisors’ helped us ‘optimize’ in the spring.”

II. The Cassette

Dr. Rowan Durona reported back within fifty-two hours, in person, in the secure room of the Escobar research complex, and Mark understood everything from her face before she reached the table. Rowan had raised the dead — including one particular short Barrayaran, many years ago — and had never once looked frightened. She looked frightened now.

“Vector revision 9 contains a dormant cassette,” she said. “By itself it is innocent: a few regulatory sequences, entered as an ‘expression stabilizer.’ We approved it because it passes every standard screening, and because it genuinely does stabilize expression. But Raven ran it through a full simulation in combination with the maintenance protocol.” She put the schematic on the screen. “The cassette rewires cellular repair to depend on an external cofactor. A molecule that does not occur in nature and is synthesized only by the patented process of… guess who.”

“The bioreactor network from clause 14.3,” said Kareen soundlessly.

“As long as the patient receives the doses — yes: rejuvenation, honest, beautiful, everything as per catalog. But stop the supply…” Rowan paused, searching for a clinically neutral formulation, and gave up. “Accelerated collapse. Months. Therapy on a leash, Mark. Whoever controls the cofactor controls the life of everyone treated.”

The room went very quiet. Mark could hear his own pulse and, somewhere beneath it, Killer — who this time wanted nothing, only stood at attention, waiting for a name.

“So the plan is this,” Mark said. “A foreign power buys a stake, fits the leash into our product with our own hands, waits for Barrayar to treat the Council of Counts, the General Staff, and half the ministries… and in five years the Imperium is governed by people whose cells are subscribed to one supplier’s mercy. Without a single shot. No fleets, no armed conspiracies — just an invoice that must never be late.”

“That is not a plan against the company,” said Kareen. “It’s a plan against Barrayar. And, Mark… we are the delivery vehicle.”

Mark sat motionless for ten seconds. Then he laughed — short, ugly, without a trace of humor — because the scheme had finally shown him its handwriting.

“No,” he said. “I am the delivery vehicle. For the second time in my life, somebody is trying to kill Barrayar through me. The first time, they raised me to shoot my brother.” He stood up. “Rowan — freeze revision 9, quietly, no trace in the registries. Kareen — we accept the negotiations. We smile. We request a meeting with the principal — not with the fund puppets, the principal. And I am going to call my brother, because this is Auditor’s work now.” A pause. “Good God. I have never before said that sentence voluntarily.”

III. The Principal

Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan arrived on Escobar by the regular courier — officially, for a preliminary certification audit of the Ascent protocol; unofficially, forty minutes after hearing Rowan’s recording, which by Kareen’s account was a personal record even for him.

“A subscription empire,” he said, pacing Mark’s office with the cane he used more for gesticulation than support. “You know, brother, all my life Barrayar’s enemies have tried fleets, bombs, pretenders, and biological nightmares. This is the first one to try accounting. I am almost impressed. Galeni is going to have a stroke — half his analysis section can’t read a balance sheet.”

“Which is why my people already read it.” Kareen spread out the ownership chart: fourteen shell companies, two trust structures on Pol, one “independent custodian.” “All roads lead beneath the surface of Jackson’s Whole. Auriga is a glove. The hand is House Bharaputra.”

The name dropped into the room like a stone down a well. Mark received it with an expression he had rehearsed in the mirror for twenty-five years — neutral, professional — and which deceived neither his wife nor his brother.

“Bharaputra made me,” he said levelly. “In a vat, to order, with a built-in purpose. It is logical for them to assume that everything I make can likewise be bought and reprogrammed. To them, Durona–Vorkosigan isn’t a competitor. We are an escaped product that has taken to earning profit on somebody else’s shelf.”

“There’s more,” said Kareen quietly. “We insisted on meeting the principal. They answered. The principal is coming in person, to a neutral orbital station. Mark… the signature on the message is the Dowager Baronne Bharaputra’s.”

Rowan, who had been standing by the window, turned sharply.

“Lotus.”

“Lotus Durona,” Kareen confirmed. “Your sister.”

The sister who, eighty years ago, had left the Durona Group — the only one of Lily’s children to choose not the family but the throne of Bharaputra. The mirror-sister, the anti-Durona, as Mark had been the anti-Miles before he chose otherwise. She was past a hundred and ten now, and according to every report her body was maintained by procedures that on Escobar carried prison terms.

“And there is the second layer,” Miles said softly. “The leash on Barrayar is the business plan. But a dying woman of the Durona genome does not cross half the galaxy for a business plan. She is coming for the protocol. The real one, without the cassette. Lily refused her the family eighty years ago — or she refused Lily, depending on who tells it. Now she means to take it. All of it. The therapy, the Group — and along the way, an empire on a leash, to pay for her ticket.”

“So what do we do?” asked Rowan.

Mark looked at his brother. His brother looked at him. Between them passed the wordless conversation of men who have survived together in places one does not discuss over dinner, and Kareen, watching them, noted for the record that the only thing more alarming than Miles with a plan was Mark with a plan and Miles approving it.

“We are going to sell her,” said Mark, “exactly what she is trying to sell us.”

IV. The Deal

The meeting took place on Benefice Station, a neutral trading platform in Escobar orbit, in a conference room rented by Auriga and scrupulously swept by both sides for listening devices — which, by Jacksonian standards, was the highest form of courtesy: both sides knew everyone was recording.

Lotus Durona-Bharaputra arrived with a float chair, two nurses built like bodyguards, and a face Mark found unsettling: the face of Rowan, of Raven, of all the ninety-odd Duronas he called his friends — only desiccated by age, and by something older than age.

“Mark Pierre Vorkosigan.” Her voice was dry and precise. “The product that privatized itself. I have always wanted to examine you. Vasa Luigi maintained you were the most expensive mistake in the House’s history. I maintained you were its best advertisement: if our products can achieve so much, imagine what the House could do.”

“The House can build a leash into someone else’s therapy,” said Mark pleasantly. “Revision 9. The expression stabilizer. We found it, Baronne. Eleven days ago.”

The silence in the room was brief and perfectly commercial — the silence of positions being recalculated. Lotus did not so much as blink.

“And yet you are here.”

“And yet we are here,” Kareen agreed, “because we want to hear the price of the other option. You did not come in person for thirty percent, Baronne. Tell us what you are actually buying, and perhaps we will sell.”

And Lotus — who was dying, and knew it, and who had too few negotiations left to waste one — told them. The protocol: complete, clean, leash-free, administered immediately, here, to her. The Durona Group: under the House’s management, “the return of assets to their maker,” as she put it. And the Barrayaran program — with revision 9 included, naturally; the leash on the Council of Counts was the commodity with which the House would settle certain creditors whose names were not spoken aloud even in swept rooms.

“Creditors,” repeated Miles from his corner. He had been silent until then, introduced as a “certification consultant,” folded up and inconspicuous insofar as Miles Vorkosigan was capable of inconspicuousness. “I’m curious about the creditors, Baronne. Because a leash on the Barrayaran government is a commodity Jackson’s Whole cannot hold — holding it takes a fleet. Your House is a broker. Who is the buyer?”

Lotus regarded him for a long moment. Then she smiled — for the first time, thinly.

“You must be the Auditor. You are shorter than the reports suggest.” She laced her fingers. “The buyer, my lord, is anyone. Commodities of that class are sold at auction.”

“Did you get that?” asked Mark.

“We got it,” said Kareen, and rose. “Baronne, thank you for the clarity. The answer is no. On all points. And now allow me to introduce the rest of the certification consultants.”

The door opened. In came: an inspector of the Escobaran Directorate of Biological Safety, with a warrant; a representative of the Barrayaran Imperial embassy, with a note; and — at the insistence of Miles, who had a weakness for symmetries — Dr. Rowan Durona, with a diagnostic case.

One of Lotus’s nurses reached under her tunic. Mark was between her and Kareen before his conscious mind had voted — four seconds he would later report to his therapist with tedious precision: Killer came out, disarmed, broke a wrist, stopped. Stopped. Returned the weapon to the floor and the woman to her chair, and went back down to the basement without a second command.

“Alive,” Mark said aloud, slightly out of breath, to everyone in the room and to one particular listener inside himself. “We work with live ones here.”

V. The Sentence

The legal phase dragged on for months and was, in Kareen’s words, the most expensive and most boring war in the company’s history. Escobar brought charges of biotechnological fraud and conspiracy; Barrayar declared Auriga and all its tentacles prohibited counterparties throughout the Imperium; ImpSec and Escobaran counterintelligence divided the syndicate’s archive between them with the courtesy of hyenas raised in good homes. House Bharaputra, faced with the prospect of its principal shareholder standing trial on a foreign planet, did the one thing a Jacksonian House does impeccably: it sold her. Full cooperation, full archives, full astonishment at “the unsanctioned actions of the Dowager Baronne.”

Two sentences remained that no court could pronounce.

The first concerned revision 9. On Miles’s proposal — and with the personal support of Gregor, whose comment, relayed down the channels, read “let it be instructive” — the cassette was not classified but published: full documentation, mechanism, detection methods, transmitted to every regulatory agency along the wormhole nexus. A weapon, Miles explained, is most permanently disarmed by making it common knowledge: nobody buys a leash whose schematic hangs in the textbooks.

The second concerned Lotus. She was awaiting extradition in Escobar’s medical detention when the Durona Group — all of them, in general assembly, under Lily’s charter — voted on Rowan’s motion. The next day Rowan walked into the detention block with the diagnostic case and an expression Mark did not undertake to read, and offered her sister the Ascent protocol. The real one. The clean one. Free, unconditional, without so much as testimony in exchange.

“She chose the House because she believed family was weakness and ownership was strength,” Rowan explained afterward to Mark, the one person she could be sure would understand. “Her sentence is to live another thirty years with the proof of the opposite. Lily would have called it mercy. I call it an experiment with extended observation.”

Lotus refused. Then, three weeks later, from her cell, she accepted. Mark, when they told him, sat silent a long while, and said at last only:

“So we are relatives after all.”

VI. Shelf Life

The certification for Barrayar was signed in the spring, in the throne room — Gregor insisted on a ceremony, partly for political reasons and partly, Mark suspected, for the pleasure of watching Mark Vorkosigan stand at attention in formal dress and suffer. The first public course of the therapy, by Imperial decree, did not go to the Council of Counts: it went to the veterans of the Komarr and Vervain campaigns, by medical commission list, and that move won the crown more loyalty than three tax amnesties.

The Vicereine of Sergyar, aged eighty-eight, declined the full protocol with a formulation that leaked to the net within the hour and became an aphorism: “I’ll take enough to finish my work. I intend to die Betan — on schedule, and with opinions.”

And one evening on Escobar, on the terrace above the Durona complex, the two brothers stood with glasses and watched Kareen and Raven down in the courtyard arguing over the next phase’s budget with gesticulation visible from orbit.

“You know what’s funny?” said Mark. “Bharaputra designed me with a shelf life. Single use: approach, fire, be captured. My entire value lay in my expiring.” He drank. “And now I sell time. Wholesale. To the planet I was engineered to orphan.”

“Vengeance by catalog retail,” said Miles. “Father would have appreciated it. Mother appreciates it out loud.” He glanced sideways at his brother. “Will you take it? The protocol. Rowan tells me you refuse to be in the first cohort.”

“I’m in the second. With Kareen, together, next year, after the contract renewal.” Mark shrugged, suddenly awkward. “I wanted to see it work first on people who…” he hunted for the phrase and found it where he always found it these days, “…who are goods I hold in trust.”

Miles laughed and lifted his glass.

“To long life, brother.”

“To the fine print,” said Mark. “Long life is what’s left over after you’ve read it.”

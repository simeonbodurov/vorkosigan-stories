The Phantom Passage

An adventure of Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan

I. The Jump

There were two kinds of Auditorial assignment, Miles Vorkosigan liked to say: the kind where they shoot at you, and the kind where you wish they would. The audit of the public procurement for the “Argus” deep-system sensor network fell decisively into the second category.

“Four hundred pages of annexes,” he told Ekaterin, as the courier Kestrel eased toward the jump point for Komarr. “Eight subcontractors. Three counter-claims. One dead acceptance inspector — fell off a ladder in his own garage, which the Komarran police investigated for a full eleven days before ruling it a household accident. And a cost overrun of forty-one million marks that everyone explains and no one can point to. Gregor is sending me because, and I quote, ‘you know how to read boring documents until they confess.’”

“And why did you agree?” asked Ekaterin, without lifting her head from her project sketches. She was traveling on her own account: on Komarr the opening of “Green Komarr” awaited her — twenty dome-gardens, the crown of a ten-year campaign she had patronized. The ceremony coincided with the activation of the new Soletta-B mirror wing, so half the Imperium was converging on Solstice for the same week.

“Because you were traveling,” said Miles. “Auditorial intuition is sacred writ.”

The jump to Komarr passed the way all jumps pass — for the passengers not at all, for the pilot who knew how. Miles was just unfastening his safety webbing when he noticed that the intercom light to the pilot’s cabin had been lit for an unusually long time.

“Pilot Asadi? Is there a problem?”

“Not exactly a problem, my lord.” The jump pilot’s voice was thoughtful, which in jump pilots passed for strong emotion. “An echo. There was an echo in the five-space interface at the exit — like a scratch on the edge of the implant. I registered it on the transit last month too, but it’s denser now.”

“Dangerous?”

“No, my lord. Just… ugly. Like someone playing the same note through two walls.” A pause. “I reported it to traffic control. They say they’re aware of it: seismic surveys by Hesper Mining, the helium-3 base on the moon Obel. Blasting sequences under permit, all in order. Sorry for the bother.”

“No bother, pilot. Thank you for being one of the people who report the ugly.”

Miles went back to the annexes. But out of an old habit Ekaterin called “cataloguing the pebbles in one’s shoe,” he took three seconds to note in his personal log: Obel. Echo. Two walls. Then he sank into the four hundred pages and did not think of it again until the transit ended.

Almost.

II. Solstice

The capital dome received them with an artificial spring and with two people on the dock: a woman of about forty in civilian dress, with Komarran features and the eyes of someone who reads three screens at once, and a tall young lieutenant in green uniform, polished to a mirror finish.

“Captain Elin Venek, ImpSec Solstice, analysis section,” she introduced herself. “This is Lieutenant Marko Vorsang, liaison and security. General Galeni has ordered that you receive anything you ask for before you ask for it. We’re trying.”

“Commendable.” Miles looked them over with approval: a Komarran and a Barrayaran working in sync — thirty years ago such a pairing had been a political statement; today it was simply a duty roster. “Let’s start with what I haven’t asked for. What do you know about the seismic surveys on Obel?”

Venek didn’t even blink — she just produced her tablet, as if the question had been on the agenda.

“Hesper Mining, registered on Komarr six years ago, helium-3 extraction in Tarn Crater. The permits are in order, the quotas are respected, the taxes are paid with tedious punctuality. The only anomaly is in the import records: for a base of forty personnel they’ve purchased power equipment fit for a small city. Official explanation — ‘prospective expansion.’ The economics section flagged it and put it to sleep: expanding helium bases ahead of the Soletta-B activation is a mass speculation, everyone’s hoping for contracts.”

“Hm.” The pebble in his shoe stirred, but did not stab. “Keep the file open, Captain. And now take me to my four hundred pages.”

The “Argus” audit proved exactly as riveting as promised. The network — two hundred sensor platforms and calibration buoys around the system’s six jump points — had been built, it worked, it even worked well; the question was why it had cost forty-one million more. For three days Miles interrogated subcontractors who blamed one another with the elegance of Komarran shareholder meetings. On the fourth day he found the first real thing: a firmware module in the relay nodes of the buoys that appeared in no technical specification.

“A telemetry tap,” Venek reported that evening, after her team had disassembled the code. “Discreet, professional, with a checksum and a watchdog timer. It copies the calibration stream from the buoys at the Barrayar jump point and relays it with the commercial traffic. Installed via the subcontractor Tolan Aero — the same one whose acceptance inspector fell off the ladder.”

“And who steals calibration data from a jump point?”

“The section’s working hypothesis is commercial espionage, my lord. The exact five-space state of the passage determines a courier’s micro-delays. Know the delay before the exchange does, and you beat the market by half a second. Half a second on the Solstice Exchange is a fortune.”

The hypothesis was reasonable, elegant, and explained everything. Miles turned it over in his mind all evening, the way one turns a coin one suspects is counterfeit without being able to say why. Exchange fraud. A dead inspector. Power equipment for a small city. An echo through two walls.

“Venek,” he said on the fifth day, “I want the full ownership chain of Hesper Mining. Not the Komarran registry — the real one. And I want the spectral record of those ‘seismic sequences’ from traffic control, raw, before the filters.”

“May I ask what we’re looking for, my lord?”

“You have no idea how much I wish I could tell you. Dig until you hit something that isn’t rock.”

The answer took her people six days. It arrived at the worst possible moment, which, as far as Miles understood the universe, was its principal method of delivering mail.

III. The Reception

The reception on the eve of the double ceremony — Soletta-B and “Green Komarr” — gathered beneath the central dome of Solstice everything the Imperium and half its neighbors could put into formal dress. Miles, in the brown-and-silver of his House, was executing the maneuver he called “the Auditorial orbit”: a slow ellipse through the hall with apogee at the champagne and perigee at the people to whom Gregor would like a courteous word spoken.

The Cetagandan trade delegation occupied the eastern end of the hall like a well-arranged ikebana. And at its center, in the clan face paint of House Soven — silver and dark cinnabar — stood a tall man of indeterminate age, discussing gardens with Ekaterin.

“…pruning, Lady Vorkosigan, is the highest form of homage to a plant,” ghem-lord Inar Soven was saying as Miles drifted into perigee. “Wild growth is entropy. Beauty begins where someone takes the responsibility to say: this branch, yes; that one, no. The same holds for larger systems. Ah, Lord Auditor. I was just admiring your wife — she understands living structures better than half my colleagues in the Celestial Garden.”

“She has the advantage of having raised gardens and children at the same time,” said Miles. “Double experience with entropy. And what is your role in the delegation, ghem-lord?”

“Investments.” Soven smiled with precisely measured self-irony. “Helium futures, energy infrastructure. Dull things. The Empire sends me on trade missions when it wishes to punish my aesthetic arrogance. But I confess, your system fascinates me.” He made a slight gesture toward the dome, toward the invisible sky beyond it. “Six passages. A crossroads. And behind a single door — a whole world that lived alone with itself for eighty years. Your Isolation is an underappreciated phenomenon, Lord Auditor. The Cetagandan poets never did it justice: a civilization in a bottle. A unique experiment. Almost… a finished form.”

“Unfinished,” said Miles pleasantly. “We opened the bottle.”

“You opened it,” Soven agreed, and for one tenth of a second something in his eyes beneath the paint focused on Miles, the way a rangefinder focuses on a target. “History loves to repeat its experiments, to verify reproducibility. But I am keeping you. Lady Vorkosigan — it has been an honor. I shall steal your metaphor about the hedge that remembers every pair of shears.”

He withdrew with the walk of a man to whom gravity is a personal favor. Ekaterin waited until he was out of range and said quietly:

“Miles. That man speaks of space as of a living hedge. And in the whole conversation he never once asked anything he didn’t already know.”

“I noticed.” The pebble in his shoe no longer stabbed — it pounded. “He came to inspect me. The question is why he thinks I’m worth the inspection.”

The answer arrived forty minutes later, in the form of Lieutenant Vorsang, who crossed the hall with the expression of a man carrying an urgent message through a minefield of protocol, and handed Miles a sealed tablet with words evidently rehearsed on the way:

“My lord, your schedule for tomorrow, as you requested.”

Miles withdrew into the alcove behind the Polian League delegation and broke the seal. Venek’s report was laid out like a good indictment.

First: ownership. Hesper Mining → a Pol holding → a Tau Verde trust → the consortium “Vetis Industrial,” Rho Ceta. Ultimate beneficiaries: the Soven clan association.

Second: energy. The base’s thermal signature in Tarn Crater corresponds to continuous consumption of 11–14 gigawatts. Helium extraction at the declared volume requires under 0.4.

Third: spectral analysis. The raw record of the “seismic sequences” contains a regular gravitational component that does not match blasting. Comparison with an archival sample appended per your instruction, for depth match.

The match is 91 percent.

Archival sample: case “Waterloo,” Komarr, 20 years ago. Device for induced passage collapse, Soudha–Radovas design. Classification: Imperial secret, first degree.

Miles stood perfectly still in the middle of the reception, amid the laughter and the crystal, rereading the last two lines. Soudha’s device. The machine a group of Komarran engineers had built to close the passage to Barrayar and return the hated conquerors to their bottle forever. The machine whose mathematics Miles had chased across half a planet and one space station. The machine that had finally been destroyed by one furious widow with a cargo float pallet — the widow who now stood twenty meters away, discussing irrigation systems with the Minister of Domes.

Someone had dug that mathematics out of its grave. Had fed it for twenty years with Cetagandan resources. And had buried it again — in a mining base on the moon Obel, within sight of Barrayar’s only door.

A civilization in a bottle. Almost a finished form.

Miles raised his eyes. Across the hall, ghem-lord Inar Soven was tasting Komarran wine and listening to some banker with an expression of perfect, patient courtesy — the expression of a man who has already concluded all his business and awaits only the result.

IV. Three Past Midnight

The ImpSec Solstice conference room at three past midnight smelled of coffee, ozone, and suppressed panic. On the main screen hung Obel — gray, pockmarked, innocent. On the second — General Duv Galeni from Vorbarr Sultana, at a signal delay of forty minutes, compensated for by advance authorizations he had dictated in the first five. On the third — roused, in a house robe and with eyes bright as a student’s — Professor Emerita Judith Riva of Solstice University, eighty-six years old, the only living theoretician who had read Soudha’s mathematics in the original.

“Oh, yes,” she said, leafing through the spectra with two fingers. “Yes, yes, yes. This is a five-space resonance pumping. The same equation, third generation. Soudha tried to gather the whole impulse into one device and deliver it to the passage all at once — that’s why he needed a shuttle and that’s why milady managed to smash it, God bless cargo pallets. These people are more patient. They pump the resonance for weeks, from a distance, in small doses, hidden in the noise. The passage is a bell, gentlemen. They do not strike it with a hammer. They sing to it. Softly. In tune. Until the bell cracks of its own accord.”

“How much time do we have?” asked Miles.

Riva pointed to the rising curve in the corner of the spectrogram.

“The accumulation is in its final phase. The doses are coming more often. If I were in their place, I would fire the final impulse under cover of the largest electromagnetic event in the system in a decade.”

“The activation of Soletta-B,” said Captain Venek soundlessly. “Tomorrow, nineteen hundred hours.”

“Sixteen hours,” summarized Galeni from the screen, at forty minutes’ delay and zero hesitation. “The military option is out: a landing in fortified tunnels requires three days, and an orbital strike on a device with such a charge… Professor?”

“An uncontrolled discharge at this level of accumulation could inflict lasting trauma on the passage even without aiming,” Riva confirmed. “Imagine detonating a tuning fork with a grenade right next to the bell. No.”

“The diplomatic option is also out,” said Miles. “Our evidence points at the Soven clan, not the Celestial Garden; an official note would only earn Cetaganda twenty years of outraged denial. Worse: Soven has built autonomous completion into the device, I’m willing to stake my chain on it. Arrest him, and the machine fires on a canned setting. He strolls through receptions precisely because his presence is no longer required.” Miles surveyed the room. “Which leaves us the one option the adversary has not foreseen, because it is impossible. Will you explain to me, Professor, how the machine aims?”

Riva lit up the way teachers light up when handed the exact question.

“Ah, that’s the beautiful part! The passage doesn’t hold still — its five-space signature drifts: solar wind, tidal effects, traffic. The resonance has to follow it continuously, or it goes out of tune. That’s why they need a live calibration stream from the buoys at the point itself…”

She fell silent, because Miles was laughing — quietly, soundlessly, leaning back, his eyes closed.

“My lord?” asked Vorsang cautiously.

“Lieutenant,” said Miles, “forty-one million marks in overrun. Gregor sent me here to find out who stole forty-one million marks, and do you know what I found? I found where the enemy breathes. The telemetry tap in the buoys. The thief of exchange micro-delays. We hold the little tube through which Soven’s machine draws its air.” He opened his eyes. “We are not going to cut it. We are going to blow into it.”

V. The Phantom

The plan Riva and Venek built by dawn was one of those the military academies later argue over as to whether it was brilliant or inadmissible. The machine on Obel trusted the calibration stream. The stream ran through Tolan Aero’s tap. The tap ran through relay nodes ImpSec had physical access to. Therefore, from now on, the machine would receive not the signature of the passage to Barrayar, but something else: a mathematical phantom.

“We can’t just feed it noise,” Riva explained, already on her third coffee and her thirtieth board of equations. “Their watchdog algorithm will detect the substitution in minutes. We have to give them a real signature — smooth, alive, breathing, satisfying every equation — only of a passage that doesn’t exist. A solution to the system with no physical correlate. A door painted on a wall. We’ll move their aim millimeter by millimeter: start from the real passage and drift smoothly toward the phantom, at the speed of natural variation, not a second faster.”

“How long do you need for full deflection?”

“Fourteen hours. Provided the substitution is seamless.”

The seamlessness fell to Vorsang. The tap could be intercepted only physically, in relay node R-11 — a four-meter capsule eight hundred kilometers from the jump point — and only during scheduled maintenance, of which there was none on the calendar. ImpSec arranged an “accident”: a micrometeorite mishap with the antenna, plausible enough to justify a repair skiff. At ten hundred hours Vorsang and senior technician Della Raz went out into open space with tools, officially to replace an antenna block, unofficially to harness, within a forty-minute window, the standard diagnostic port, insert Venek’s device into the stream, and get out without the watchdog timer registering an interruption longer than eight hundred milliseconds.

They managed it in seven hundred and forty.

“The stream is ours,” Venek reported at eleven hundred and three, in the voice of a woman breathing for the first time in an hour. “The phantom is running. Drift one point two per hour, within natural limits. The machine is swallowing it.”

The following hours Miles spent in the hardest position he knew: on display. The Auditor had to be visible, calm, and boring — Soven was undoubtedly watching every twitch of official Solstice, and a single cancelled event involving a Vorkosigan could pull the trigger early. So Miles inspected the ceremonial hall, gave two interviews about “Argus” (“the overrun has been established, the guilty will restore the funds” — which was even true), lunched demonstratively with the Minister of Domes, and smiled, and smiled, and smiled, while eight hundred kilometers from the passage a lie ran along the wire at a speed of one point two per hour.

At seventeen ten the lie nearly died.

“Solar ejection,” Riva’s voice in the secure channel had gone thin. “A real one, class M, the front hits the point in twelve minutes. The real signature will jump. My phantom is programmed for a calm sun — the discrepancy will light up their watchdog like a holiday tree.”

“Can you get the storm into the phantom?”

“I can get it in with twenty minutes of precise work, and I have twelve!”

“Then we’ll steal you eight.” Miles was already dialing the jump-point traffic control, activating with a single phrase the Auditorial authorizations Galeni had loaded the night before: “This is the Voice of Emperor Gregor Vorbarra. I declare an emergency calibration regime. All ships in the passage zone — I order simultaneous power-up of your Necklin rods at minimum output, pre-jump test configuration, beginning in ninety seconds, duration ten minutes. All of them. Yes, the freighters too. Yes, the Cetagandan liner — especially the Cetagandan liner, it has the most powerful rods in the system.”

“My lord,” Venek whispered, as traffic control erupted into feverish activity, “what are we doing?”

“Noise, Captain. Thirty-one sets of Necklin rods, fired up at once, muddy the local five-space picture to unreadability. For the next ten minutes the real passage will look like static chaos — and Soven’s watchdog will write off the discrepancy as instrument noise from an exercise by the mad Barrayarans. And Professor Riva gets her twenty minutes.” Miles leaned back and, for the first time in a day and a night, allowed himself a predatory smile. “We made the truth look like a lie, so the lie could pass for the truth. If that isn’t diplomacy, I don’t know what is.”

At eighteen forty Riva reported: storm — embedded; phantom — stable; deflection — ninety-seven percent. The machine on Obel, stuffed to bursting with twenty years of patience and eleven gigawatts, was aiming with absolute, flawless, lovingly maintained precision at a point in five-space behind which there was nothing.

VI. The Ceremony

Soletta-B unfolded at nineteen hundred Solstice time — six new mirror leaves blossoming in orbit like a silver chrysanthemum, and the whole dome gasped as the secondary dawn washed over the terraces. Miles watched not the sky but the man beside him on the VIP gallery: he had seen to it that the invitations placed ghem-lord Inar Soven two meters from him, at the window with the best view.

“Impressive,” said Soven. His eyes were raised to the mirrors, but their focus was elsewhere — on a point beyond the sky where, at this very moment, under cover of the electromagnetic pageant, his bell was to receive its final, shattering note. “Your Empire builds on a grand scale, Lord Auditor. Light for a whole world.”

“We do our best,” said Miles. “Though personally I have a weakness for older feats of engineering. The passages, for instance. Did you know that our passage’s signature has a fundamental harmonic of zero point eight seven one natural units? A remarkably stable value. It drifts, of course — solar wind, traffic — but it always returns. Like a well-tuned bell.”

The sentence settled into the conversation softly, like silk over a blade. Miles had the pleasure of observing at close range as a face beneath the clan paint of House Soven performed a complete inventory of its last sixteen hours. The harmonic he had quoted was from the phantom — a value that could be known only by someone who had read the machine’s stream from the inside.

“You knew,” said Soven very quietly. He did not ask since when. A ghem-lord does not beg for the details of his defeat.

“I was the smoke screen, ghem-lord. A whole roomful of specialists knew. At the moment we speak, your device is emptying its charge — all of it, to the last erg, flawlessly aimed — into an address that does not exist. A door painted on a wall. The professor who painted it sends her regards and insists I convey that your third generation of the equation is ‘elegant, but credulous.’” Miles sipped from his glass. “The passage to Barrayar will open for traffic tomorrow on schedule. And you have a more pressing problem than me.”

“And that is?”

“Behind your left shoulder.”

Ghem-general Dag Benin, chief of the Imperial security of the Celestial Garden for thirty years, wore his seventy-five years and his ceremonial blood-red paint with equal flawlessness. Officially he accompanied the trade delegation as a “security auditor” — a circumstance Miles had grasped only the night before, with a feeling close to admiration: Cetaganda too had scented its renegade, but had neither evidence nor jurisdiction to act on Komarran soil without thereby confessing the plot itself. Benin had come to wait. Benin knew how to wait.

“Ghem-lord Soven,” he said with an exquisite bow. “The delegation requires your presence. At length. Perhaps for years.”

Soven looked at Benin, then at Miles, then — for the last time — at the secondary dawn above the domes of the world that would remain on the map of the universe. Whatever was happening at that moment behind the paint did not come out. He bowed with precisely measured perfection, turned, and walked ahead of Benin with a straight back — a man going not under escort, but toward his own Empire, which, all three of them knew, was considerably the more terrible.

Benin lingered a moment more.

“Lord Vorkosigan. The Celestial Garden does not care to be presented with gifts it did not order — neither passages nor wars. The debt will be recorded.” A pause, and something old and almost warm passed across the flawless paint. “I am pleased to note that the years have not grown you.”

“I’m working on it from the inside,” said Miles. “Give my respects to the Empress.”

VII. The Doors

The machine was lifted out of Tarn Crater three weeks later — a joint engineering team under double supervision and the direction of Professor Riva, who circled the eleven-gigawatt corpse with a flashlight and exclamations of the kind women of her generation usually reserved for grandchildren. The core, emptied and dead, went to Vorbarr Sultana, to that wing of the Imperial arsenal where the Empire stores the things it does not speak of. The official communiqué mentioned “unregulated mining activity.” The helium base was bought, with some irony, by a Komarran consortium involving the Toscane clan.

On the night before departure, Miles and Ekaterin stood on the observation terrace of the orbital station, watching the passage — invisible, marked by nothing but the rosary of navigation lights and the unbroken string of ships pouring into the nothing and emerging from it: ore carriers, liners, couriers, all the blood of the Imperium pulsing through a single vein.

“For twenty years,” said Ekaterin, “I thought that machine was the most terrible thing I had ever seen. A group of desperate engineers with an equation and a grievance. And now someone took the same equation and added an empire, patience, and aesthetics.”

“And lost to one retired professor, one lieutenant with seven hundred and forty milliseconds, and one accounting audit.” Miles covered her hand with his on the railing. “Do you know what kept me going through those sixteen hours? The thought that we had already beaten this machine. You beat it first. With a cargo pallet. I merely maintain the family tradition by quieter means.”

“Fake mail instead of a pallet.”

“We’re aging,” Miles agreed. “We’re becoming refined.”

Below them a Cetagandan liner — the same one, with the most powerful Necklin rods in the system — eased into the queue for the jump, blinked its running lights, and sank into the passage toward the rest of the universe. Miles followed it with his eyes and thought of bottles and doors; of a world that had been alone with itself for eighty years and still carried that mark in the bones of each of its generations; of all those who would go on looking at Barrayar’s only door and dreaming it shut.

“Let them dream,” he said aloud. “The doors of Barrayar are opened and shut by us.”

Ekaterin laughed softly and took his arm.

“Come, my Lord Auditor. We have a jump at six tomorrow, and Asadi will grumble if we’re late.”

They passed through the passage the next morning, on schedule. Pilot Asadi reported that the echo was gone — the interface clean as a mountain morning. In Miles’s log, beneath the entry Obel. Echo. Two walls, a second line appeared, with which the case was closed for him forever:

The bell sings only for the one who listens. We listened first.

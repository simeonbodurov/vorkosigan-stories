Fourteen

An adventure in the Dendarii Mountains — for Selig and Simone Vorkosigan

I. The Excursion

The summer excursion of the district schools was a thirty-year tradition: three days over Middlestone Pass, fourteen children from Hassadar and the mountain villages, one guide, and one rule carved out of experience like a trail out of rock — in the mountains, all are equal before the weather.

That was why the group roster read simply “S. Vorkosigan, 14” and “S. Vorkosigan, 12,” without titles, without footnotes, and why Armsman Vitor, whose duty it was to follow the Count’s children everywhere, had been dispatched — after negotiations conducted by their mother with the soft implacability of a woman who designed gardens on top of rock — to wait with the flyer at the base camp on the far side of the pass. “Three days as ordinary children,” Ekaterin had said. “That is also an inheritance, Vitor. Possibly the rarest one in our catalog.”

Selig Vorkosigan, fourteen, walked in the middle of the column with the group’s medical kit in his pack — his first-aid badge was the only one he had ever worked at properly — and with the patience of a boy who has preferred listening since he was small. His sister Simone, twelve, walked at the very rear, by her own choice, with a worn gray beret on her head — the one from Grandmother Naismith’s old suitcase, won two years before in negotiations that had entered the family chronicles — and she counted. Simone always counted: paces, switchbacks, clouds, people. “Twelve, plus us two — fourteen children. And the guide makes fifteen,” she noted at the first rest stop, unasked. “Fourteen,” confirmed the guide, Mila Karal, sixty years old, with a face like the bark of a mountain pine, and for the first time looked at the small girl in the beret two seconds longer than necessary.

The group was motley, as mountain groups are: Mara Csurik of Silvy Vale, fifteen, the teacher’s granddaughter, who had crossed the pass more times than she had been to the cinema; Vlado from Hassadar, fifteen and half a head taller than everyone, who by the second hour had announced that “mountains are easy work”; Peyko from the lower villages, quiet and wiry; little Dona, twelve, above the treeline for the first time, eyes shining. And the mountain received them as it receives everyone: with two days of blue sky, blueberries on the slopes, and a forecast promising rain no earlier than the third evening.

Mountain forecasts, Mila Karal told the commission afterward, are like capital promises: true in substance and wrong in timing.

The storm came on the third day at noon, six hours ahead of schedule, from above, from the direction of the pass — it did not approach; it fell: the sky went from blue to green-black in twenty minutes, the temperature dropped like a stone, and the rain struck horizontally, warm at first, then icy. Mila Karal turned the column back toward the shelter two hours down the trail — along the path that traversed the scree above the swollen stream.

The scree did not wait for them.

II. The Landslide

The sound came before the movement — a deep, wet crunch somewhere up in the fog, like a bone in a giant’s fist — and Mila Karal, who had heard that sound twice in her life and survived it both times, screamed the only command there was time for:

“To the rock! Everyone to the rock, run!”

Then the slope went.

The slide crossed the trail forty meters wide — mud, stone, and uprooted scrub pine descending to the stream with a roar that ate every scream — and when it stopped, and the rain went on falling on the new, earth-steaming wound in the mountain, the account stood as follows: twelve children pressed against the rock face, alive, muddy, deafened. Peyko — flung clear of the stream’s edge with an arm broken in two places by the guide’s effort to haul him out. Dona — with a split brow and eyes that looked through people. And Mila Karal herself — she had managed to throw the boy toward the rock, but not herself: pinned to the waist between two boulders, conscious, with a leg about which she let no one speculate, and a face the color of wet paper.

And the guide’s pack — with the comm unit, the signal beacon, half the food, and the maps — was gone. It had gone with those forty meters of slope, down into the boiling stream, irretrievably, like everything the mountain decides to take for itself.

The first three minutes were chaos — real, ugly, children’s chaos: crying, shouting, Vlado yelling “we have to go back, we have to go back” at a trail that no longer existed, two of the younger ones locked onto each other by the rock. The way back was erased. The way forward vanished into fog and downpour. The adult who was supposed to know what to do lay pinned under a boulder, breathing in catches.

And then, in the middle of the chaos, a small twelve-year-old girl, her gray beret plastered to her head by the rain, climbed onto the highest stone she could find and did not scream, but asked — clearly, loudly, and so matter-of-factly that the question cut through the panic like a knife:

“Who is hurt? Raise your hand. I’m counting.”

III. The Command

Later, before the commission and before the parents, the children described that moment differently, but all of them remembered the same sensation: that the question gave them something to do. Panic is a chasm without a bottom; the question was a step.

“Peyko — the arm. Dona — the head. Madame Karal — the leg. Anyone else?” Simone waited, surveying the raised and unraised hands. “Right. Twelve sound, two hurt — fourteen children. And the guide. Selig! Do you have the kit?”

“I have it.” Her brother was already forcing his way through the mud.

“Peyko and Dona are yours. Mara!” Simone found the mountain girl with her eyes. “You know this mountain. Go to Madame Karal — she will advise, you will decide with her. Vlado!” The tall boy turned, panic still colliding with shame in his eyes. “You’re the strongest one here. We need two long poles from those uprooted pines — for a stretcher and to prop the boulder. Take two helpers. Everyone else — in pairs! Each of you takes one person by the hand and does not let go. Whoever is shivering — move: gather dry branches from under the rock face, the rain doesn’t reach there. We count off at every switchback. We are fourteen. We stay fourteen.”

“Who put you in—” Vlado began, on the momentum of fear.

“No one,” said Simone Vorkosigan. “I simply count better than anyone here. When you count better — you command. Until then: two poles, Vlado. Please.”

The “please” did more work than everything else — Vlado admitted it himself afterward, in front of everyone, which does him credit. And Mila Karal, pinned between her boulders, with a leg she could no longer feel and forty years of mountains behind her, looked at the small girl in the beret for ten more seconds and then did the only thing a good commander does when she is out of the line: she handed over command aloud, formally, so that everyone would hear it.

“Listen to the girl,” she said, as loudly as she could. “She counts right.”

The next hour did the work that saves lives: the work before the heroics. Selig splinted Peyko’s arm with a slat of pine and half a roll of bandage, talking to him quietly and continuously — about silly things, about blueberries, about how on Beta Colony the casts were transparent — until the boy stopped shaking. Dona got a dressing, a warm garment from the common store, and a job: to hold the spare lantern and count aloud at every muster, “because you have the clearest voice, and the counting is the most important work here.” (Her eyes came back to the people somewhere around the third count.) Vlado and his helpers propped the boulder above the guide with the poles — not to lift it, that was impossible, but so it would not shift — and by the time Mara and Simone knelt beside Mila Karal for the war council, the old guide had already appraised everything.

“The leg is broken and pinned, but the boulder isn’t crushing me — it’s holding me. I’ll live for hours, maybe a day. But you don’t have a day.” She pointed with her chin upward, where the fog was seething. “That is the front’s leading edge, children. Night is coming, cold is coming, and here on the bare scree the cold kills more surely than a landslide. You need a roof and a fire. Mara — report.”

“The shepherds’ shelter is that way, beyond the slide — no way through.” Mara Csurik was tracing on the wet stone with a finger, quick and sure. “That leaves the pass. An hour and a half up, and down the other side is Silvy Vale. My village. The school has a radio, a healer, everything. But with a stretcher, in this weather… four hours. Maybe five.”

“Five hours,” Simone repeated. She looked at Mila Karal. “We carry you.”

“You leave me,” the guide said evenly. “Fourteen children move twice as fast as fourteen children with a stretcher. I am under a propped boulder, in a rain cloak, alive. You collect me tomorrow with people and a winch. That is the correct arithmetic, girl, and you know it.”

Simone Vorkosigan, twelve years old, was silent for three seconds — and in those three seconds, Mara said afterward, she did not look twelve at all.

“The arithmetic is correct,” she said. “That is why I am not accepting it. In our family there is a rule for arithmetic like that, Madame Karal: iron is counted in marks; people are counted in people. If we leave you and the front brings down one more tongue of mud… No. We set out fourteen. We arrive fourteen. Vlado — the stretcher. The boulder we do not touch — the poles stay as props to the last: she said it holds her, not crushes her, which means there is a gap, and under the gap is the slide’s own fresh mud. We dig her out from under it, not it off her. It will be slow. It will be heavy. We rotate every two hundred paces — I count the paces.”

“Who taught you that rule?” asked Mila Karal quietly.

“Nobody taught it.” The girl was resettling the beret, by now a sodden sponge. “At our dinner table, that is simply how people talk.”

The extraction took forty minutes and was accomplished without one touch to the boulder. Vlado’s props held; beneath the pinned leg the landslide had left its own material — a fresh, loose mix of mud and gravel — and the children scooped it out in shifts, with hands, with mess tins, with the little spade from the emergency kit, centimeter by centimeter, while Selig splinted the leg where it lay in the gap, before the first movement. When the guide finally came free — pale, stiff, and swearing with forty years’ expertise — she surveyed the hole, the boulder, and the props, and said only: “I am logging this too. With a commendation.”

IV. The Pass

The climb to Middlestone Pass entered the oral history of the district schools under the name “the five hours,” and it is told ever since on every summer excursion, around every fire, with details that grow with the years. The truth needed no growing.

They walked in an order Simone arranged and did not change to the end: Mara in front, reading the slope; behind her, in pairs — each pair answerable for each other, hands joined at every hard stretch; the stretcher in the middle, on six bearers rotating every two hundred paces, counted aloud by Dona (“one hundred eighty… one hundred ninety… change!”); Selig beside the injured, tirelessly chattering nonsense in precisely measured doses; Simone last, where the whole column could be seen, with a muster at every switchback. Fourteen. Fourteen. Fourteen.

The stream below the pass tested them properly: swollen, brown, the footbridge eaten down to two bare beams. Vlado crossed first with the rope from the emergency kit, made it fast to the far rock, and stood beside the beams in the icy water to his knees, giving a shoulder to every child — forty minutes, in unceasing rain, until his hands turned blue; and when the smallest boy slipped and hung screaming from the rope, Vlado caught him by the collar with one hand and set him on the bank, and then stood ten minutes more for the rest, and no one ever again heard from him the sentence “mountains are easy work.” The stretcher crossed last, on eight hands, with Mila Karal clenching her teeth white and letting out not one sound — only on the bank did she say: “There has been no crossing like that in my groups for thirty years. I am logging it.”

At the pass, the worst of it hit them — the wind. The rain up there flew parallel to the ground and cut through soaked clothes to the bone, and exactly at the pass, in the roar and the grayness, little Dona stopped counting, sat down on a stone, and said in a thin, perfectly calm voice that she could not go on and that she wanted to stay here.

Simone crouched in front of her, so that their eyes were level.

“Do you know what I’m wearing on my head?” she asked. “This beret is my grandmother’s. My grandmother commanded ships. Once, they say, she sat in a closet, tied up, among bad people, and it was certain that everything was over. Do you know what she did? She counted. The guard’s footsteps. Eighteen down, eighteen back. Counting holds a person together, Dona — that’s why I asked you to count. Not because your voice is the clearest, but because you are the bravest of us, and the others hear it in your voice.” She pulled off the sodden beret and settled it on the little girl’s head, over the dressing. “Keep it for me to the village. A beret like this must not sit on a person who has sat down. Come on. From here it’s downhill.”

(The beret, Selig noted afterward in his letter to their brother Aral Alexander, was not their grandmother’s but their father’s, and the story of the closet belonged to an entirely different person and an entirely different closet — but Simone told the family legends “in the wartime edition,” and no one in the family, the father included, found anything to object to in the results.)

Dona stood up. The muster at the next switchback came in the clearest voice of the whole day: “Fourteen!”

The descent went easier and more dangerously — wet rock, exhaustion, dusk — and for the last two hours Mara led them by memory and by the smell of smoke, because the fog had eaten everything else. At six forty in the evening, when the first roofs of Silvy Vale swam up out of the gray and the village dogs scented them and flooded the slope with barking, the column halted for the final muster, which Simone performed herself, aloud, with a hand on each shoulder in turn.

“Fourteen,” she said at the fourteenth. Her voice broke precisely on the last syllable, for the first time since the landslide, and no one pretended not to have heard it, and no one ever reminded her of it.

V. The School

Silvy Vale received them the way mountain villages receive disasters: without questions before the work and without silence after it.

The school — a long, bright building with woodcarving over the door and a name inscribed simply “RAINA” — turned within half an hour into infirmary, mess hall, and headquarters: the village healer took charge of Mila Karal’s leg on the very threshold (“broken in two places, dislocated, and alive — that boulder was your friend, madame”), Peyko’s arm went into a proper splint, thirteen children were dried, filled with soup, and tucked into bedrolls between the desks, and the school radio — old, powerful, maintained like an altar — sent down into the valley the message that half the district had been waiting for since afternoon: the group is found; all are alive.

Armsman Vitor arrived on foot at nine in the evening, having halved the roundabout route across the western shoulder in a time no one afterward cared to verify, because it was physiologically dubious — soaked to the bone, with a pack of emergency equipment, and with the face of a man who had aged ten years in ten hours. He found his young lady asleep sitting up against the wall, the duty roster in her lap — water shifts, fire shifts, the boot-drying rotation — and his young lord awake beside the bedrolls of the injured, because “Peyko wakes from the pain every hour, and somebody has to tell him about the transparent casts.” Vitor stood in the classroom doorway for a full minute. Then he went out into the rain, delivered the complete report to Vorkosigan House over the comm in a level voice — and only after the link closed did he permit himself to sit down on the steps of the Raina school and not be level.

The school’s headmistress — a woman in her fifties, white-haired, with eyes that had buried much and preserved more — made the rounds of her sleeping charge before midnight, tucked back two kicked-off blankets, left a thermos beside Selig, and beside Simone, carefully, so as not to wake her, placed a pillow between her shoulder and the wall. Then she stood at the window beside her granddaughter Mara, and the two of them watched the rain for a long time.

“You brought them over,” said Harra Csurik.

“I knew the way,” said Mara. “She knew the order. Grandmother, she is twelve. She counted us at every switchback. Fourteen times fourteen. Do you know what she said at the stream, when Vlado proposed the sound ones cross first? ‘There are no sound and injured here. There is one group.’” The girl was silent a moment. “Which family is she from — do you know?”

Harra Csurik was looking through the rain toward where the village burial ground lay, and the small stone, and the thirty-six years.

“I know,” she said. “I knew by her order before I saw the roster. That family counts people one by one, Mara. It always has. Once it even counted one whom everyone else had written off.”

VI. The Father

The Count’s flyer set down on the school green at ten past five in the morning, in the storm’s first break, and out of it climbed two people who were not running only because they were holding each other back from it.

Miles Vorkosigan entered the Raina school at fifty-six the way he had entered this valley at twenty: quietly. He stopped in the classroom doorway — where everyone had stopped that night — and looked for a long time: thirteen children asleep between the desks under borrowed blankets; a broken arm in a fresh splint; his own son asleep sitting up beside the injured, one hand on a strange boy’s shoulder; and his own daughter against the wall, the duty roster in her lap and her hair matted with sleep, and no beret on her head, because the beret was on another head, over the dressing of a little girl who clutched it in her sleep with both hands.

Ekaterin went past him — she had always known how to pass through doorways he lodged in — and knelt by her children without waking them, only a hand, light as a leaf, on each forehead in turn: the living muster, the mother’s count, oldest of them all.

And Miles found, in the staff room, Harra Csurik awake over tea, and the two old acquaintances looked at each other for a while across thirty-six years.

“My lord,” said Harra.

“Harra.” He sat down opposite her, small, white-haired, suddenly without a single title anywhere on him. “They told me on the way. All of it. The slide, the stream, the pass, the stretcher. Twelve years old. I teach her to count at the dinner table, Harra. At the dinner table. With bread and stories. And then the mountain takes twelve strangers’ children and my two, and cuts the trail, and looks to see what they have learned.”

“They have learned, my lord,” said Harra Csurik. “I have watched for forty years what children carry out of their homes. Yours carried order and counting. My Mara — the way. Vlado from Hassadar — a back. Little Dona — a voice. The mountain only arranged them into one thing.” She sipped her tea. “And their shelter was given them by Raina. Think about that, my lord, when your turn comes not to sleep: you came here thirty-six years ago for the sake of one child whom nobody had counted. The village built a school in her name. And last night fourteen slept in it, counted fourteen times. This world’s accounts do come out sometimes, my lord. Slowly. Across a generation. But they come out.”

Miles Vorkosigan, Imperial Auditor, Count, a man with a vocabulary for every hall in three empires, found nothing to say and did not try. He only nodded — slowly, low, almost a bow — to the teacher, to the school, and to the small stone outside in the rain.

In the morning, before the ambulance and the whole commotion of retrieval, he found his daughter on the veranda — awake first, out of a habit that would stay with her for life — and sat down beside her on the steps, without preamble, the way he sat down for the important conversations.

“Report,” he said simply.

And Simone reported: in order, clearly, with figures — the slide, the assignments, the refusal to leave the guide, the stream, the pass, the beret (here she abridged slightly), the musters. Miles listened without interrupting, with the face of a man who has received a thousand reports and knows this one is going into the top ten.

“How many times did you count?” he asked at the end.

“Fourteen switchbacks times fourteen children. And once, finally.” She raised her eyes. “Father. I was afraid the whole time. The whole time. Does that… spoil it?”

“That makes it real,” said her father. “Now listen to me, because I am going to tell you the entire secret of the trade, and it is one sentence: command is not being unafraid, and it is not shouting loudest. Command is going on counting your people while you are afraid. You counted. Fourteen set out; fourteen arrived.” He was silent a moment, and added, more quietly: “I have an account from this valley, from many years ago, in which the numbers did not come out. Yours came out. You don’t know how much that is, Simone. One day I will tell you, and then you will know.”

VII. The Count

The resolution took a day: an ambulance from Hassadar collected Mila Karal from Silvy Vale, alive and swearing (“forty years of mountains, and now I get carried down like a dowry chest”), Peyko left for Hassadar with his arm in a real cast (opaque, to his disappointment), and the fourteen were returned to fourteen sets of parents, amid scenes about which no reports are written.

The consequences arranged themselves, the way they do after work well done. The district adopted a new excursion regulation — two comm units in two packs, never in one. Vlado of Hassadar applied in the autumn to the junior rescue service and was accepted with a recommendation signed by M. Karal and worded “tested at a stream.” Dona returned the beret with a letter of three lines, in a hand as clear as her voice. And in the Raina school in Silvy Vale, on the wall by the door, there appeared a small woodcarving — fourteen figures in a column, one stretcher, and one girl with a beret at the rear — with an inscription beneath, cut by Mara’s grandfather’s hand: “We count each other. All of us.”

At Vorkosigan House, the reckoning went by family custom: quietly, at dinner, with no heroes at the table. Only after dessert did Miles set a parcel beside his daughter’s plate — and Simone unwrapped a notebook. Paper, bound, new, with blank pages and a single written one: the first, in her father’s small hand.

To Simone. In our family, notebooks are a hereditary condition, and you have already shown the symptoms: you count people, and you do not let go of them. Write things down. One day your notebook will be needed by someone more than you can imagine today — that is how it has gone with all our notebooks so far. P.S. The beret is yours. Officially. Its owner would have approved your edition of the closet story: he himself never let the facts spoil a correct tale — or a correct formation.

Simone read the page twice, closed the notebook, and put it away where she kept the important things. Then, because she was twelve and a half, and because she was of that family, she took a pencil, opened to the second page, and began to write, at the top, in even letters, the first thing a Vorkosigan notebook must contain.

A list. Fourteen names. Counted.

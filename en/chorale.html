Chorale

An adventure of Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan in quaddiespace

I. The Flare

The quaddies’ sun went mad at the start of the work shift, and the first thing the Union of Free Habitats did was dim the lights — not out of necessity, but by old spacer reflex: when your star is screaming, you speak softly.

The flare was of the kind astronomers record once a century and give small names to, so as not to be afraid of them. The plasma tongue washed the outer stations with radiation, which the shelters handled; the magnetic wave scrambled communications for three days, from which the networks recovered. But the third effect — the one for which no manual had a ready page — reached the system’s single great wormhole, the door to the entire rest of the galaxy, and began to shake it.

At first only the jump pilots felt it: “it scratches,” they said, “it echoes,” “it sings off-key.” Then the trade insurers raised their rates. Then the Union’s Navigation Board put limits on tonnage. And on the eleventh day, when the throat’s variance left the yellow zone and pressed against the orange, the official bulletin pronounced, with quaddie matter-of-factness, the sentence that two million people in free fall read twice over: “Should the trend continue, the passage may become impassable within forty to sixty days.”

And then, through the habitats, on the public channels, in the park spheres and the shafts of the docks, the singing began to be heard.

The Choir of the Severance was an old sect — as old as the Union itself, small, patient, harmless. Its doctrine fit in one sentence: the downsiders created the quaddies as property, and every door between the two peoples is a chain that Creation will sooner or later break. For decades the Choir had sung its hymns in rented halls before half-empty rows. Now, under the mad sun, it sang to full spheres — in many voices, in free fall, in that inhuman, four-handed choral architecture no downsider could imitate — and in the songs there was ecstasy, and in the ecstasy there was a word, repeated like a bell stroke:

Jubilee. Jubilee. Jubilee.

Because to the Choir, the closing door was not a disaster. It was a wedding.

II. The Needle

The ship slipped through the sick passage on the thirty-ninth day — through the place where, for a week, no one had dared to go — and the duty controller of Graf Station, a young quaddie on her first solo watch, first did not believe her screens, then did not believe the transponder, and then stared a long time at the silhouette: one hundred and sixty meters, double phase fan, Imperial registry of Barrayar.

The Echo, the fastest courier of a distant empire, requested docking under the quietest possible protocol.

Aboard her, besides the crew, were: eleven technicians with identical cases and the unmilitary bearing of military men; one middle-aged armsman named Roic, who regarded the docks of Graf with the expression of a man returning to a place where, once, things had been very interesting; one small, very old Komarran lady who, at the sight of the throat data, rubbed her hands together as before a festive table; one sealed cargo the size of a shuttle, listed on the manifest as “navigational equipment”; and one Imperial Auditor, who had come to quaddiespace twenty-five years earlier, on business about which both governments were unanimous that it had officially never happened.

They were received, in a secure room without windows, by the Union’s own leadership — the Speaker, the head of the Navigation Board, and the chief of the militia — plus one unofficial seventh chair, in which sat a seventy-odd-year-old Betan it with the expression of someone for whom the title “retired harbormaster” has long served as a cover.

“Bel,” said Miles. “Why am I even surprised.”

“Don’t be,” Bel Thorne advised. “It’s bad for the complexion. Talk, Miles. The whole Union is counting days.”

Miles talked for seven minutes, without notes. About a machine existing in a single exemplar, born of the century’s worst mathematics; a machine that had twice been aimed at wormholes and had twice won. About Professor Emerita Judith Riva, who knew it better than its makers and who maintained — with equations appended to the proposal — that the resonance that cracks bells can be inverted: the same song, sung in anti-phase, does not destroy but damps the oscillation. A damper. A stabilizer. A hand laid upon a ringing rim.

“The condition is one, and it is absolute,” he finished. “The machine does not exist. It has never existed. My team will install it, tune it, and run it until the crisis ends; your people will assist; and all that any of us will remember afterward is that the flare passed off by itself. The Imperium asks nothing in return…” — he made an Auditor’s pause — “…except a universe in which the quaddies’ door stands open. Closed doors have rather gone out of fashion with us lately.”

The Speaker of the Union, a white-haired quaddie with four impeccably calm hands, exchanged one long look and three short gestures of dock-sign with her colleagues.

“The Union accepts the offered hand, Lord Auditor,” she said. “And asks only one thing: how much time do you need?”

“Twenty-six days,” said Riva, before Miles could open his mouth. “If nobody gets in our way.”

No one in the room yet knew how much that “if” was going to cost.

III. The Incidents

The installation barge took station at the throat of the passage on the forty-second day — officially a “navigation buoy, reinforced class,” unofficially eleven gigawatts of inverted physics — and the work went off at a pace that made even Riva praise someone out loud: the quaddie riggers, the finest zero-gee workers in the galaxy, did in a shift what the Barrayaran team had planned for two.

Chief rigger Opal Six, a forty-year-old quaddie with a surgeon’s hands and a docker’s vocabulary, took the external assembly; the young engineer Miro Two — quiet, conscientious, with the cleanest protocols Riva had ever seen in a person under thirty — took sensor integration. The schedule pulled two days ahead.

The lead died on the fifth day, when the barge’s positioning crane, inspected that same morning, dropped a waveguide section and bent it into a parabola no manual provided for. The inquiry found material fatigue. On the ninth day a batch of coolant proved contaminated with assembly grease — an accident during transfer, said the report. On the thirteenth, a false radiation alarm evacuated the barge for eleven hours in the middle of a critical foundation pour. On the seventeenth — and this made Riva stop swearing and start thinking — the calibration array from the night shift turned out to be unusable: not erased, not damaged, but quietly detuned, with errors below the alarm thresholds, which would have poisoned the tuning a week later if one elderly Komarran eye had not been comparing raw data against its own intuition every morning.

“Four incidents, zero casualties, zero traces,” reported Roic, who with two ImpSec men had turned over everything. “The crane, the coolant, the alarm — each one explains itself. Together they don’t, m’lord. Together they look like a man who doesn’t want to break things. He wants to delay.”

“To delay,” Miles repeated, and looked at the big schedule on the wall, where the green line of the installation and the red curve of the destabilization crawled toward each other like jaws. The two-day reserve had become minus three. Riva’s forecast, recomputed with the night data, no longer said “forty to sixty days.” It said “eighteen.” “Somebody has understood that he doesn’t need to defeat us. It’s enough to make us late. The door will do the rest.”

That same evening the captain of the Echo, a man of Imperial discipline and simple arithmetic, requested a private word and laid it out unadorned: on the current curve, the ship could guarantee a return transit no later than day thirteen. After that, even the double fan would not thread anyone through the dying throat. If my Lord Auditor remained to the end of the installation, and the installation ran late…

“I know,” said Miles.

“My lord, it is my duty to say it: you risk staying with the quaddies forever.”

“I know,” Miles repeated. He thought of Ekaterin, of the children, of the garden under the autumn sky of Vorbarr Sultana; he took from his case a sheet of real paper and wrote for twenty minutes a letter, which he sealed and handed to the captain with the words “in case,” and then went back to the operations room, where a saboteur without a face and a door with a clock were waiting. “The Auditor leaves last, Captain. That’s the trade. Now excuse me — I have to go to church.”

IV. The Choir

The Choir’s sphere in Minchenko habitat held a thousand, and that evening it was full: a thousand quaddies arranged in free fall along invisible concentric shells, faces to the center, where there hung — no pulpit, no dais, alone in the middle of his own acoustics — the preacher who twenty years earlier had taken the name Cantor One.

Miles listened from the outer shell, a registered guest, known to no one, and admitted two things to himself. First: this was the most beautiful singing he had heard in his life — a four-voiced architecture impossible for fractional downsider choirs, circular waves of sound in which a thousand bodies breathed as one. Second: it was excellent propaganda.

“They made us,” Cantor was saying between hymns, in a voice that was itself an instrument. “They made us as a thing, brothers. With a contract, with an inventory, with a depreciation schedule. Our forebears fled — but did they take freedom? No. They took distance. And now Creation, in its mercy, offers us what no revolt ever won: a door that closes from the outside. Not by war. Not by blood. By the breath of our own sun. I ask you, brothers — when the key turns by itself, is it a sin to sing?”

“Jubilee!” the sphere answered with a thousand throats, and at the beauty of that answer Miles felt cold run down his spine.

Afterward, by the community’s custom — the Choir prided itself on turning no one away — the guest was invited to put a question, and the small downsider from the outer shell pushed off toward the center with the skills of a man who had learned free fall from mercenaries, and asked politely:

“One question, Reverend. You sing of a door that closes from the outside. But two million people did not sing with you tonight. The traders whose children study on Escobar. The doctors whose patients wait for organs from Beta. The musicians with tours on twelve worlds. For them the door is not closing from the outside, Reverend. It is being slammed — and the key is being turned by you, from the inside, with a song. Tell me, theologian to theologian: is that manumission… or merely a bigger cage with a better choir?”

The sphere rustled. Cantor One studied the small guest for a long time — and Miles saw in his eyes not fanaticism, but something more dangerous: an intelligence that had counted him to the last button.

“The guest speaks of keys,” the preacher said gently. “Guests always speak of keys. Because guests carry them.” A pause, perfectly measured. “And do you know, brothers, the surest proof that Creation is with us? That no key will turn in time.”

He knows about the installation, Miles thought with absolute clarity, as the hymn rose again around him. He knows about the schedule. He knows we are losing. He is not praying for the door to close. He is closing it — with other people’s hands.

V. The Rhythm

The answer to the question “whose hands” was hidden, as it turned out, in the music itself — and it was found not by ImpSec, not by Riva, but by one very old quaddie with a double-handed dulcimer.

Miles had gone to Nicol — the legend of quaddie music, silver-haired, with four hands that remembered half a century of scores — on Bel’s advice and on an intuition he could not explain: he brought her the record of the detuned calibration array, converted into sound. The errors below the alarm thresholds, played as a pulse. He asked her to listen to the noise.

Nicol listened for forty seconds. Then she stopped the playback, ran it back, beat out a measure in the air with her upper right hand, and said:

“That is not noise, Lord Vorkosigan. That is a hymn. The Threshold Hymn — here, listen to the rests: long, long, short, long. A strophe of eleven, an antiphon of seven. Whoever introduced these errors introduced them in time.” She smiled sadly with the corner of her mouth. “He could not have done otherwise. We are like that, my lord. A quaddie who does a thing with passion does it in rhythm. Even a crime.”

The rest was craft. ImpSec disassembled the Choir’s public broadcasts from the past month and found, in Cantor’s new verses — only the new ones, added “by revelation” before each shift on the barge — a second layer: numerical motifs woven into the melody, readable by one who knows where to listen. Dates. Sections. Systems. The preacher had been conducting his sabotage from the pulpit, on the air, before two million witnesses, and it was at once brilliant and — Miles noted it for his report — typical: fanatics always sign.

They laid the trap with a false schedule — “the critical pour moves to Thursday’s night shift” went out on a channel accessible only to the installation team — and on Wednesday night Roic’s sensors recorded a figure with four hands and the cleanest protocols on the barge, quietly and in rhythm, re-setting the valve of the emergency nitrogen.

Miro Two denied nothing. He sat in the detention cabin upright, pale and calm, and when Miles came in, he spoke first, quietly:

“No one was going to be hurt. I checked everything three times. No one — ever — was going to be hurt. Only time. We only needed time.”

“I know,” said Miles. And he did know: in the whole sabotage there was not one sacrificed victim — only stolen hours; a thief of days, not of lives. “One question, Miro. Cantor told you Creation was with you. That the closing was foretold. When did he foretell it to you the first time?”

“Three days before the flare,” said Miro, and the old faith lit in his eyes. “Three days before, my lord! Before the whole Choir! How else do you explain it, unless—”

“With a receipt,” said Miles.

VI. The Pilgrims

Because there was a receipt — ImpSec found it inside a day, digging not in the theology but in the data traffic. Three days and eleven hours before Cantor One’s “revelation,” the Union’s solar observatory had registered the prominence precursors of the great flare — raw data, readable only by a specialist, an internal bulletin with restricted access. Eleven minutes after the bulletin, one copy was downloaded from the terminal of a senior astronomer, a long-standing member of the Choir. And forty hours later the preacher had come before his flock with burning eyes and a prophecy whose accuracy ten thousand people afterward called a miracle.

The timing of the disclosure they did not choose — Cantor chose it. On the eleventh day, with the installation at seventy-nine percent and the throat in the dark-red zone, the Choir proclaimed a Pilgrimage: three thousand believers in personal pods and work platforms set out for the passage, to surround the barge with their bodies and sing until the door closed — a living, gentle, impenetrable blockade, against which the Union militia had not one acceptable move.

Miles had no acceptable move either. So he made the unacceptable one: he went onto an open frequency, against the background of the approaching choir of three thousand voices, and put out on the air — on every channel of the Union simultaneously, with the Speaker’s consent and with an edit polished by ImpSec’s techs to perfection — three documents side by side. The observatory bulletin, with date and hour. The download log, with a name. And the recording of the “revelation,” with date and hour — forty hours later.

“People of the Union,” he said then, and only this: “It is not for me to tell you what to think about doors. You are a people born behind a locked door, and you opened it yourselves — you need no lessons from a downsider. I will only tell you what I think about prophets. A prophet who receives his revelations by office terminal is not conversing with Creation. He is conversing with your trust. Check the receipts. We will wait here and do our work — and the door, whatever you decide, we will hold open while you are deciding. Because that is what one does with the doors of free people.”

The outer arc of the pilgrims fell silent first — where the signal reached earliest. Then the silence moved inward through the cloud of pods like the shadow of a cloud over wheat. And then, from Graf Station behind them, from the park spheres and the dock shafts where two million people had been listening, another song rose on the air — an old one, older than the Choir, older than the Union itself: the work song of the first generation, the one with which the fugitive forebears had kept up their courage aboard the ships they had stolen from their owners. Nicol took it up from the concert sphere with her dulcimer and her whole orchestra; habitat after habitat joined it; and three thousand pilgrims in their pods heard it from both sides at once — ahead of them the machine, which kept silence; behind them the people, who sang — and began, pod by pod, to turn back.

Cantor One was detained by the Union militia that same evening, without resistance, on charges in which the word “faith” did not figure once: misappropriation of official data, conspiracy to damage critical infrastructure, endangerment of the community. He said nothing. Only on the threshold of the cell did he turn and look at Miles — long, intelligent, without hatred — and Miles, who had won many arguments, understood that this one he would never win: there are people for whom a closed door is home, and no receipt opens a home.

The installation was completed on the twelfth day, at twenty-two hours forty minutes — ninety minutes ahead of Riva’s threshold and fourteen hours ahead of the Echo’s last window. The machine from Tarn Crater, three times aimed at wormholes, sang for the first time in anti-phase: a quiet, patient, damping song, laid over the ringing throat like a palm on the rim of a glass. The red curve on the screens trembled, bent… and began to descend.

In the operations room of the barge, Professor Emerita Judith Riva, eighty-nine years old, watched the descending curve for a full ten minutes without a word — a record, noted by all present — and then said, in a stranger’s young voice:

“Three times I have fired this monster. Twice at bells, to silence them.” She touched the screen with two fingers, the way one touches the forehead of a convalescent. “This time I taught it to heal. It was worth not dying, to get this far.”

VII. The Communiqué

The Navigation Board’s official announcement came out a week later and consisted of forty-one words, polished by three governments: solar activity had passed its peak; the passage’s variance had returned to the green zone; tonnage restrictions were lifted; the Union thanked its citizens for their patience and the work crews for their professionalism. About one barge with a reinforced navigation buoy, left on station at the throat “for long-term monitoring,” the announcement said nothing; and the joint protocol for its security — signed in a room without windows by two people and countersigned by a third with turquoise eyes — existed on no medium at all.

The Union’s sentences were quaddie ones: precise, quiet, and without one gram of vengeance. Cantor One received six years of supervised labor and a lifetime prohibition on access to broadcast infrastructure; his Choir — inviolable in its right to sing — was sung now by others, more softly and to emptier spheres, because the receipts had been read by everyone. The astronomer lost his access and his post. And Miro Two — the thief of days, who had checked everything three times so that no one would be hurt — was sentenced to three years’ work on the maintenance crew of the stabilizer itself: to guard, with all four hands, the door he had tried to close; and Miles, who had suggested the wording to the court, entered it afterward in his private notebook as one of his few judgments without remainder.

On the last evening Nicol played in the Minchenko memorial sphere — for the crew of the Echo, for the riggers, for half of Graf Station — and at the end, unannounced, she took up the work song of the first generation, and the whole sphere sang it standing, insofar as that concept meant anything in free fall. Bel Thorne, beside Miles on the guest shell, listened with closed eyes, and at the end said only:

“Twenty-five years I’ve watched you come here, Miles. Every time you bring some catastrophe and take away some miracle. I hope your bookkeeping holds.”

“My bookkeeping,” said Miles, “has been kept in another currency for a long time now.”

The Echo passed through the wormhole the next morning — smoothly, soundlessly, through a throat calm as a lake at dawn — and Miles Vorkosigan stood on the observation deck while the quaddies’ system shrank astern: the sun, subsided into yellow; the necklace of habitats; the invisible barge at the door, singing its quiet, eternal, damping song. He thought of the letter marked “in case,” which the captain had returned to him unopened, and which he would burn at home, in the fireplace, with Ekaterin beside him, by old family custom for letters of that kind. He thought of a thousand voices in a sphere, of receipts and revelations, of doors.

“The doors of Barrayar are opened and shut by us,” he had said once, over another wormhole, in another crisis, and he still thought it. But now, watching the stars of the quaddies give way to the stars of the road home, he added in his mind the second line as well — the one you arrive at only with years, and only by way of other people’s skies:

And the measure of a people is not the doors it can close. The measure is the ones it holds open — for others.

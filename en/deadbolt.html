The Deadbolt

An adventure of Ensign Aral Alexander Vorkosigan

I. The One Who Came Back

The ship appeared in the Barrayar system at four eleven in the morning, capital time, out of a point in space where, according to every chart for eighty years, there had been nothing but vacuum and statistics.

Imperial survey vessel Pioneer — one hundred and ten meters long, with the badge of the Imperial Cartographic Service on her bow and a hole of irregular shape in her starboard side, big enough to drive a groundcar through. She did not answer hails. She did not maneuver. She drifted inward on inertia, cold almost as the space around her, transmitting only one thing: an automatic beacon with the oldest distress code in the Barrayaran registry, and an appendix of three words that put first the fleet on alert, then ImpSec, and by sunrise — one Imperial Auditor.

Survivors in cryostasis.

“Five out of fourteen,” the chief physician of the military hospital reported two days later, while Miles Vorkosigan looked through the glass at the row of revival capsules. “Improvised cryochambers, built out of food refrigeration units and emergency oxygen loops. Crude work, desperate, but by the book — someone aboard knew the field manual on cryoprep by heart. We’ll wake them in stages. First is Senior Lieutenant Mora Seyan, watch commander.”

Pioneer was no random ship. Miles knew, because fourteen months earlier he had personally countersigned her mission order — so secret that even in his own Auditorial archive it figured as a “calibration flight.” After the Soven affair, Gregor had ordered permanent deep gravimetric surveillance of the home system — the “Argus-B” network, the more paranoid sister of Komarr’s. And a year and a half ago Argus-B had found something that made the meeting in the Emperor’s presence go very quiet: a faint, young five-space node in the system’s far periphery, blinking in no catalog. A new wormhole. A back door to Barrayar that no one knew about — not Cetaganda, not Komarr, not their own history.

Pioneer had been sent to find out where it led. And now she had come back — holed, frozen, and mute.

Senior Lieutenant Seyan woke on the third day and told, in the broken, too-level voice of a person who has gone beyond horror and come back by the same road, the story that by evening had been classified so deeply that its classification itself was classified.

Five jumps along the virgin chain beyond the new passage. Dead systems, then a living one: a planet with green in its spectrum. On the approach — a ship without identification, rising from behind the moon, a salvo without warning, a breach in the hull. And then — boarders.

“They weren’t human.” At this point Seyan’s level voice cracked for the first time. “They came in through the breach in vac suits… suits with six limbs. Two and a half meters. Heads… heads of mantises, my lord. Faceted eyes behind the visors. They moved like soldiers — in pairs, covering each other, clearing compartment by compartment. We fell back and torched the sections behind us. The captain and eight others held the engineering corridor while we four… while we five prepped the jump and the chambers. The last thing I saw, before the pilot threw us back down the chain, was one of them — standing over the captain’s body. It did not desecrate him. It examined him. Like a specimen.”

She raised to Miles eyes in which fourteen months of ice still floated.

“My Lord Auditor. There, behind the new door, there are aliens. And they kill people.”

II. The Ensign

Ensign Aral Alexander Vorkosigan learned of his assignment at eight thirty in the morning, forty minutes after it was decided at a meeting he had no right to know existed, and eighteen days after he had sewn onto his sleeve his first tab.

“Reconnaissance scout IJS Hound,” he read in one breath, standing in his father’s study at Vorkosigan House, because the order had caught him in the leave between the Academy and first posting. “Special cartographic mission. Sensor officer. Father… this is that ship, isn’t it? For that passage. The whole class is whispering that something entered the system out of nowhere, and then the whispering stopped all at once, which means it’s true.”

“I can neither confirm nor deny,” said Miles, which between Vorkosigans meant “yes.” He looked at his son — a meter eighty-five of calm, focused youth — and felt the father and the Auditor fighting to the death in his chest. The father had spent the night composing nine impeccable service reasons for Ensign Vorkosigan to be reassigned elsewhere. The Auditor had torn them up one by one. “Aral. You know I can pull you off that list with one call. You also know why I never will.”

“I know.” His son smiled with Ekaterin’s smile and his grandfather’s forelock. “Because afterward you’d have to live with me. And I hold grudges like Grandmother.”

“Worse. Because you are the best sensor graduate in five years and the ship needs your eyes, and I have no right to leave the Hound half-blind for the sake of my own stomach.” Miles came around the desk. At fifty-three he reached his son’s shoulder, which had long since stopped being anyone’s problem. “One thing I’ll tell you, and it isn’t fatherly, it’s professional. You’ve heard Seyan’s recording. You’ve all heard it. Forget the adjectives. Remember the data. Horror tells stories; sensors tell the truth. Your job out there is one thing: see what is, not what you’re afraid it is.”

“Yes, my Lord Auditor.” Then, more quietly: “I’ll come back, Father.”

“I know,” Miles lied with forty years of practice, embraced him hard, and let him go.

The Hound departed eleven days later: eighty meters of hull, maximum speed, minimum signature, a crew of nine. Captain Toria Vorhalad — fifteen years in deep reconnaissance, a face of granite, and a weakness for tea with rum. Jump pilot Revik — a Komarran, silent as his own implant. Engineer Lieutenant Duran, Senior Sergeant Kalo with two troopers, a comm officer, a medic — and the youngest sensor officer ever to fly off the edge of the map.

The mission order, signed by Gregor himself, condensed into one sentence: Find out what is behind the door. Let no one find out what is behind ours.

III. The Chain

The new passage swallowed them on the fourth day — young, narrow, “flickering at the edges,” as Revik put it with the rare talkativeness of a man about to thread a ship through a needle’s eye. Beyond lay Pioneer’s chain: five jumps through systems no human catalog knew.

Aral Alexander lived at the sensor station. The first system: a red dwarf and ash. The second: a binary tearing a planetary disk apart between them. The third, the fourth — rock, ice, silence. He mapped, he cataloged, and he learned the oldest discipline of deep reconnaissance: not letting the emptiness talk to you.

The fifth system had a yellow star and one planet in the habitable belt — a blue-green point that, at the first long-range scan, sent his pulse to the top of the medical monitor.

“Vegetation, Captain. Oxygen atmosphere, twenty-one percent, nitrogen buffer. Oceans. Cloud systems.” His fingers were already decomposing the spectrum, and right there, in the third window, waited the find that turned the whole mission on its head. “Captain… the chlorophyll. The absorption lines. This is chlorophyll A and B. Earth’s. To the last angstrom.” He looked up from the console. “Alien plants don’t read our botany textbooks, Captain. This biosphere isn’t foreign. It was brought here. Somebody terraformed this planet — and terraforming is not a technology of deep-space mantises. It is a technology of humans.”

Vorhalad leaned over his shoulder and studied the spectrum a long time.

“So Seyan’s ‘aliens’ breathe our air,” she said slowly. “Ensign, if you’re right, this stops being first contact and becomes something considerably dirtier. Revik, approach in the gas giant’s shadow, passive sensors, full silence. I want to see this planet before it sees u—”

The ship without identification came out from behind the gas giant’s third moon exactly as it had come out against Pioneer — and the Hound survived the next eleven minutes only because she was smaller, faster, and conned by a captain who had been expecting the ambush for three days. Vorhalad threw the scout into a maneuver that made the compensators scream, Revik squeezed out of the drives numbers beyond the rating plate, and Aral Alexander, welded to his console, called vectors on the intercepting salvos while the universe rolled over.

They got clear behind the rings with three hits aft, and a balance sheet that Engineer Duran delivered with a blackened face and blackened gloves:

“Main reactor’s hit, Captain. The magnetic bottle is holding on hand-training. Jump power we do not have and will not have — not with this reactor. The capacitors are good for one descent, and that’s all.”

Vorhalad surveyed the bridge: eight faces and one simple arithmetic. Two possibilities remained — to die in orbit, or to go down onto the enemy’s planet.

“Well,” she said, and finished her cold tea, “at least there’s air down there. Ensign Vorkosigan, find me a landing site. Preferably one where we can look convincingly dead.”

IV. The Hill

The plan for their own demise they worked out in one breath, on the way down, because the pursuer was already describing a wide arc to cut their orbit and they had exactly one atmospheric entry to spend.

“The reactor’s a corpse anyway,” said Duran. “But it’s a corpse with eight hundred megajoules still in the bottle. I separate it on a ballistic arc, it goes in ahead of us, the bottle ruptures on impact — and you get a blast, a crater, and the radiation signature of a crashed ship. Everything an investigator wants to see.”

“And we land behind the ridge line, in the radio shadow, on capacitors,” Vorhalad finished. “Ensign, find me a hill.”

Aral Alexander found them a hill — a long basalt rampart at the edge of a forested plain, with a deep dry valley behind it. They entered the atmosphere like a meteor disintegrating by the book: at eighteen kilometers’ altitude the Hound spat out her dying reactor along the computed arc; it lived out its last seconds as a comet and drove itself into the brow of the hill with a blast that vaporized a hundred meters of slope and hung a mushroom of dust and steam over the plain. Three seconds later, blind to every sensor in that storm of debris, they slid over the crest itself and lay down in the valley beyond — on capacitors, on air cushions, on a hair.

The camouflage nets were rigged before the dust settled. An hour later a flyer passed over the “crash site” — once, twice, a circle over the crater, a circle over the scattered wreckage (Duran had jettisoned three containers of genuine ship’s refuse along with the reactor — “investigators love physical evidence”). Then the flyer went away. Nobody searched the valley.

“Congratulations, ladies and gentlemen,” said Vorhalad. “We are officially dead. Let’s not squander the advantage.”

The planet received them with a warm wind, with the smell of crushed greenery — disturbingly, nostalgically Earthlike — and with a forest of trees that were not quite trees but were trying. And on the second day Sergeant Kalo’s recon pair came back from the crest at a pace faster than regulation, with a holo recording around which the crew gathered in silence.

Along the cut at the forest’s edge moved a column. Thirty-odd figures, two and a half meters tall, green-brown, six-limbed — four for walking, two folded as if in prayer. Mantis heads, exactly as Seyan had said. But Seyan, in her horror, had missed the main thing, and Aral Alexander, going through the recording frame by frame, said it aloud, because data was his job:

“They’re marching. In step. In pairs, at interval, with a point man and a rear guard.” He froze the frame. “And on the chest of each one — look — a breastplate with a mark. The same stamp, serially applied. Aliens don’t sew themselves uniforms with factory numbers, Captain. Soldiers do.”

“Whose soldiers?” asked Kalo.

The answer came on the fourth day, when they tracked the column to the valley beyond the forest and lay on the crest with the optics. Below, in an artificial lake of light, spread a compound: domes, barracks, training grounds, a landing field with two cargo ships. And on the façade of the central building, enormous and shameless beneath the alien sky, was blazoned the crest that every Barrayaran officer learned by heart in first year — the stylized bird wings of a Cetagandan ghem clan.

“Clan Raal,” said Vorhalad after a long pause, in a voice with no color in it at all. “The war party. So that’s where the vanished budgets go that Allied intelligence has argued about for ten years.” She lowered the binoculars. “There are no aliens, gentlemen. There never were. There is a ghem laboratory on a terraformed planet off every chart, making soldiers out of insects. And we are lying in its backyard with a burned-out reactor.”

V. The Hive

They named the compound the Hive, because a person needs a name for a thing before he can stop having nightmares about it, and for three nights they studied it with the patience of people whose only road home runs through the enemy’s warehouse.

The Hive lived by a schedule. In the bio-domes to the north, tanks were ripening — Aral Alexander counted through the optics forty-eight industrial replicators, each the size of a bus. On the training grounds to the south, the mantis soldiers learned: close-order drill, marksmanship with adapted carbines, assault on a mock-up — all of it under the invisible whip of something the scouts discovered on the second night, sweeping the radio spectrum: a continuous coded signal at seven hundred megahertz, beamed from the central dome’s antenna at every living thing on the training ground.

“A leash,” said Aral Alexander, showing the captain the correlation: the pauses in the signal coincided with the intervals between exercises; each command preceded the columns’ movement by two hundred milliseconds. “They aren’t trained, Captain. They are driven. Neural modification plus a carrier wave. Cut the signal…” He hesitated at the edge of the thought. “I don’t know what remains when you cut the signal. But whatever it is, it’s theirs — not the clan’s.”

“Write that thought down, Ensign,” said Vorhalad. “We may need it. Now the main thing: Duran?”

“The depot by the landing field,” said the engineer, who had spent two nights studying the Hive’s cargo traffic with the passion of a thief at a shop window. “They’re offloading standard reactor modules for their shuttles — Cetagandan Ishi-4s, fifty megawatts, I’ve seen them at trophy-equipment auctions. With an adapter frame and six hours of swearing, I can marry one to our Hound. It weighs four tons. So we need: first — a module; second — a cargo platform; third — thirty minutes during which nobody looks at the depot, at the valley, or at the sky.”

“The first and second we steal,” said Vorhalad. “The third is called a diversion. Proposals?”

There were two proposals. Sergeant Kalo proposed the fuel park — a classic, guaranteed fire, guaranteed attention. Aral Alexander proposed the other thing, and then lay awake all night listening to his own proposal grow in the dark.

“The leash antenna, Captain. The relay for the southern training ground is on a separate mast, outside the dome perimeter — reachable on foot. We don’t blow it up — a blown antenna is a one-hour diversion, then they repair it. We shift its code. The comm officer says the carrier runs on a rotating key — if we record eight hours of traffic, we can transmit the same signal phase-shifted. To the receivers in their heads that won’t be silence. It will be noise. The commands will stop meaning anything at all.” He drew a breath. “There are eight hundred soldiers on that ground right now, Captain. Eight hundred driven beings that suddenly no one will be driving. The clan will throw everything — every vehicle, every man, every sensor — into rounding up its stock before it scatters across the continent. Nobody will be counting reactor modules. Nobody will be looking at the sky.”

Vorhalad looked at him for a long time — long enough that he felt, down his back, under the uniform, the whole weight of the family name.

“And the eight hundred, Ensign? When we cut their leash — what do we do with them? Release onto this world eight hundred two-meter soldiers with no commands?”

“We release onto this world eight hundred beings who, for the first time since the hatchery, will be executing no one’s commands,” said Aral Alexander. “I don’t know what they will do, Captain. I know what we do while the signal keeps running: we leave them in slavery because it’s tidier that way.” He heard in his own voice both his father and his grandmother, and was not ashamed of either. “The planet is theirs more than the clan’s. Let them have it.”

Silence. Then Vorhalad turned to the comm officer:

“Record the traffic. We go tomorrow, in the dark watch.” And to Aral Alexander, more quietly now, with something unreadable in the granite face: “One thing is certain, Ensign. You are nobody’s son. You are your own.”

VI. The Long Night

The operation, which later entered the fleet’s closed annals under the name “the Long Night of the Hound,” lasted four hours and eleven minutes.

At zero ten the comm officer and one trooper reached the relay mast and clipped onto its power cable the box that held three days of stolen traffic and one phase loop. At zero forty Duran, Kalo, and Aral Alexander entered the depot by the landing field through the roof — three shadows on lines, between two rounds of the guard, timed to the second. A reactor module Ishi-4, four tons of sleeping sun, settled onto a stolen cargo platform — a wide flying car for ten, of the kind the Hive used for hauling between domes — and the platform crawled out of the depot a hand’s breadth above the ground, without lights, loaded with the future of nine people.

At one fifteen, when the platform was already creeping along the valley floor toward the hill, the comm officer threw the switch.

To the Hound’s sensors, listening from eight kilometers, nothing changed: the carrier at seven hundred megahertz kept transmitting, smooth and confident. But across the Hive’s southern training ground, where the night shift was drilling close order under the floodlights, a wave passed — first a stutter in the rhythm, then the dissolution of the columns, then something the observation post on the crest described in its report with a sentence copied ever after into all the analyses: “The formation stopped. Then the formation looked around.”

Eight hundred soldiers stood under the floodlights and looked around — for the first time with no command in their heads, in the deafening silence of their own thoughts, whatever those were. Then one walked. Not in step. Simply walked, toward the dark line of the forest. After it, a second. Then — all of them, in a widening wave, through the lit perimeter, through alarms erupting like hysteria, through the shouts of guards who discovered that their weapons were programmed not to fire on the clan’s own stock.

The Hive went mad exactly as one ensign on his first mission had predicted: every vehicle in the air, every floodlight on the forest, every man of Clan Raal — out, into the night, chasing the scattering property of ten years of black budget. Nobody counted the reactor modules. Nobody looked at the sky.

At two forty the platform reached the valley behind the hill. By four less ten, Duran — with six hours of swearing compressed to two and a half by pure adrenaline — was marrying the Ishi-4 to the Hound’s power bus, and the crew was folding the nets. At four eleven the scout lifted off the valley floor and rose into the radio shadow of her own funeral crater, dark, silent, dead to every sensor currently occupied with eight hundred louder targets.

Aral Alexander, at the sensor station, allowed himself one last look back through the optics. On the crest above the valley, silhouetted against the glare of the Hive’s floodlights, stood a single figure — two and a half meters, six limbs, motionless — looking up, straight at the rising dark point it could not possibly see and was looking at nonetheless. Then it raised its two forelimbs, folded as if in prayer — a gesture that was in no catalog and that he never, to the end of his life, undertook to interpret — and the forest swallowed it.

Five jumps down the return chain passed without pursuit. The young passage let them through with its “flickering at the edges,” and on one clear morning IJS Hound, declared lost twenty-nine days earlier, requested docking clearance from Barrayar orbital station — an event the duty controller celebrated by knocking over his chair.

VII. The Deadbolt

The council in the Emperor’s presence lasted six hours and involved nine people, two of them Vorkosigans — one with an Auditor’s chain, the other with an ensign’s tab and a report that Gregor Vorbarra read twice, end to end, before he spoke.

The equation on the table was simple and had no good solution. Behind the new passage, five jumps out, ghem Clan Raal was building an army in secret — one the Celestial Garden either knew nothing of, or preferred not to know of. The clan did not suspect that the chain led to Barrayar — Pioneer and Hound had died convincingly, and the farewell traffic analysis showed Raal hunting an “unidentified reconnaissance vessel, probably Allied.” That ignorance was the Imperium’s only advantage, and it was melting by the day. Public exposure meant either war with Cetaganda or — likelier and worse — Cetagandan gratitude, a purging of the clan, and the route remembered. The route to Barrayar’s back door.

“There is a third option,” said Miles, when the circle had closed for the second time. “No one guards a door he doesn’t know about. But no one passes through a door that isn’t there, either.”

He laid on the table a single photograph: an elongated eleven-gigawatt cylinder in a vault of the Imperial arsenal, under an inventory line that read “Item O-1, origin: Tarn Crater.”

“Soven’s machine,” said Gregor. It was not a question.

“Built to close Barrayar’s door,” said Miles. “I propose we let it do exactly that. Only the door will be chosen by us.”

Professor Emerita Judith Riva arrived from Komarr on the first courier, examined Item O-1 in the arsenal with a flashlight and with a mixture of disgust and tenderness of the kind reserved for gifted, badly brought-up students, and pronounced: the young passage, narrow and still unstable, was the ideal patient. “Old bells take months of singing to crack. Young ones haven’t hardened yet. This one will fall silent in three weeks.”

They mounted the machine on a barge at the passage itself, powered by two fleet reactors, aimed not by stolen calibration streams but by the full, generous, official telemetry of Argus-B. Riva tuned the resonance personally — “once in my life, to fire this thing properly” — and for twenty-two days the device of ghem-lord Soven sang to the young passage its own patient, destroying song. On the twenty-third day the five-space node in the system’s periphery shivered, contracted, and ceased to exist — quietly, cleanly, finally, observed by nothing but one barge, one professor, and two Vorkosigans on the observation deck.

“A sword reforged into a deadbolt,” said Miles, watching the screen on which there was no longer anything to watch. “Soven would have appreciated the irony. Actually, no — he would have hated it, which is even better.”

“And I can’t stop thinking about the other end,” said Aral Alexander quietly. “We locked the door on a crime scene, Father. The clan stays there, the replicators stay, the leash will be repaired. We just… turned the key and walked away.”

“Yes,” said Miles. He softened nothing, because his son had earned the right to unsoftened answers. “We turned the key and walked away, because the alternative was a war that would have cost millions of lives for the same result. The Imperium performs triage, Aral. It is the dirtiest word in the whole trade, and it is learned in only one way — by hurting.” He was silent a moment. “But remember two things. First: the file remains. Every frame, every spectrum, your report — in the archive, alive, waiting for the day the Celestial Garden itself asks Clan Raal where its money went, and then a Barrayaran courier may quite accidentally deliver the answer. Archives are patient. I have a lieutenant who taught me that.”

“And the second?”

Miles looked a moment longer at the empty screen — the door that was no longer there, and beyond it, five nonexistent jumps away, a forest under an alien sky, in which eight hundred freed soldiers were walking somewhere with no commands in their heads.

“The second is yours, not mine. You cut the leash when nothing obliged you to. Eight hundred beings are free tonight on their own planet — free for what, free for how long, unknown; but free, and by the decision of an ensign on his first mission.” He turned to his son. “The Imperium closes doors, Aral. That is its work, and sometimes it is right. You see to it that you’re one of the people who remember what stays on the other side. As long as there are such people, deadbolts are policy. When there stop being such people — they become character.”

On the way back, in the shuttle, Aral Alexander took out his notebook — paper, a present from his father on commissioning, with a single sentence already inscribed on the first page in the familiar small hand: The bell sings only for the one who listens. Beneath it, with proper ensign’s diligence, he added a second line — his own:

And the door remembers everyone it has let pass. In both directions.

The Blue Dose

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Request

The message arrived over a commercial channel, encoded with a cipher Elli Quinn had not seen in fifteen years — the private key of an old friendship — and it began without a greeting, Betan-style:

Quinn. We’ve lost a ship. The Union won’t tell you this officially, because the Union has no military fleet and is proud of it in a way that at the moment strikes me as a luxury. So I’m telling you. The survey vessel Audacity, crew of fourteen, twelve of them quaddies, disappeared seven months ago in the side branch beyond Xerxes node. Her last dispatch mentioned a system with an asteroid belt and “signs of unregistered settlement.” Then — nothing. The Union sent inquiries. The inquiries came back unopened. I am old, Quinn, but I have not gone soft in the head: an unopened inquiry is an answer. Does your fleet still take contracts, or are you just polishing the Triumph for the museum? — B.

“Bel Thorne,” said Captain Herald, reading over her shoulder. “The harbormaster of Graf Station. I thought it retired ten years ago.”

“It did. Three times. The quaddies keep bringing it back — nobody in the Union can shout at late freighter captains with such love.” Quinn reread the message and felt the old compass in her chest quiver and settle onto a heading. Twelve quaddies. The four-armed people, born for null-gee, the finest space workers humanity had ever made for itself — and had once tried to own. A people whose history began as “experimental tissue, property of a corporation,” and who had won their freedom by fleeing across half a galaxy. If someone in an uncharted belt had laid a hand on a quaddie crew…

“Herald — new course,” she said. “Xerxes node, then the side branch. The contract is private, the client is the Union of Free Habitats through an intermediary, and the intermediary is one under-slept Betan it with a bad temper. The pay will be modest.”

“Then why are we taking it, Admiral?”

“Because thirty years ago, when I was lying in a sickbay with a burned-off face, that same under-slept it read bad novels aloud to me eight hours a shift.” Quinn stood. “And because if somewhere there are quaddies with an overseer again, I intend to be the invoice that gets presented to him.”

Bel Thorne was waiting for them at the transfer station off Xerxes — seventy, silver-haired, with the Betan carriage that age had only made drier and more precise, and with a travel bag whose presence was not open to negotiation.

“I’m coming,” it said. “Don’t argue. Nicol has a grand-nephew on that ship. Leo Twelve, engineer, twenty-six years old. He played the harp for her when he was five, off-key and with all four hands at once. If you think I’m going to sit on Graf and wait for news, the years have taken your memory.”

“Come aboard,” said Quinn. “But your cover is a trade captain.”

“My dear,” Bel smiled the smile that had once sold impossible lies to three intelligence services simultaneously, “I am a born trade captain. I merely spent forty years impersonating a soldier.”

II. Anchor

System HV-742 had no name in the registries and many names among its inhabitants, but the official one, written on the main habitat in letters of ship armor, was “Anchor” — and beneath it, smaller: Core Consortium. Private property. The solvent are welcome.

They came in aboard the Magpie — a fat, battered free trader the Dendarii Fleet kept precisely for such occasions — loaded with a precision cargo: medicines, filters, tool steel, and luxuries. Quinn stood on the bridge in the skin of “Captain Rosa Vane,” a graying smuggler with a dubious past and excellent prices; Bel was the “supercargo”; Lieutenant Tira and four more of the recon platoon were crew.

The belt unfolded before them in all its ugly industry: hundreds of tagged asteroids, probe robots, tugs, and — Quinn counted them with a professional shiver — eleven heavy mass drivers on stations that had nothing to do with throwing ore toward the refineries, and everything to do with controlling the approaches. A mining tool is a gun pretending it isn’t: fifty kilograms of rock accelerated to ten kilometers a second holes a warship no worse than a plasma salvo.

“Eleven barrels, networked targeting, overlapping arcs,” she muttered. “Whoever built this wasn’t afraid of pirates. He was afraid of liberators.”

Anchor Station received them with tariffs: docking — two hundred marks; air — per head per day; water — per liter; a trading permit — at the administration’s discretion. The currency was internal, “kernels” — convertible only in the Consortium’s stores, at a rate Bel computed aloud with the intonation of an obituary. And then they saw the people.

Three strata, distinguishable at a glance, the way the decks of a slaver are distinguishable. On top — the Consortium’s men: security in gray armor with good weapons and bored eyes. In the middle — the “contract hands”: miners, dockers, techs, in old suits, with debt bracelets on their wrists counting off in red how many kernels each still owed for the air he breathed while paying off the air from before. And at the bottom…

At the bottom, on the cargo quay, six hands in a row were passing containers along a chain in the null-gee zone — two pairs of quaddies, working with that fluid, downsider-impossible grace Quinn remembered from Graf. Except that on Graf the quaddies laughed while they worked. These worked in total silence, in identical gray coveralls, with an injection port sewn into the left shoulder of each — and with eyes whose whites were tinted a pale, unmistakable, unnatural blue.

“The blued,” a contract hand with a bracelet past five digits told them that evening in the quay bar, over a glass of the genuine whiskey they’d brought. “Don’t talk to them, don’t look at them, don’t ask. The blued belong to the Director. Personal property, as the saying goes, only here nobody says it.” He drained the glass. “And let me tell you, strangers: mind you don’t run up too much debt. Because from contract hand to blued, the road is one signed ‘voluntary stabilization program.’ A port in the shoulder, a dose every three days, and you never, ever think about leaving again.”

“And if a blued misses his dose?” asked Bel with perfect commercial indifference.

The contract hand set down his glass and looked into it a long while.

“The funeral is at the Consortium’s expense,” he said at last. “The only free thing on Anchor.”

III. The Blued

The Director of the Core Consortium received them on the third day, in an office with a view of the belt and furniture shipped in from three sectors. Gedeon Valk was a large, well-kept man of indeterminate sixty, with a politician’s handshake and a moneylender’s eyes, and he spoke of his system with the intonation of a man reading a love letter to himself.

“Order, Captain Vane. That is what I sell — not ore. Order is the scarcest commodity in the galaxy. Here there is no crime, no unemployment, no politics. Everyone has a place, a debt, and a schedule.” He smiled. “I know what they say on the quays. ‘Slaves,’ ‘doses,’ dramas. The truth is boring: a labor stabilization program, entirely voluntary, with medical coverage the contract hands can only dream of. My stabilized workers are the healthiest people in the system.”

“And the four-armed ones?” Quinn asked, with well-measured greed. “I hear they’re worth a shuttle apiece. Do you sell?”

Something cold and satisfied crossed Valk’s face.

“I see you understand assets. No, Captain. Quaddies are not sold. Quaddies are depreciated — thirty years of useful life in free fall, zero expenditure on grav decks. My best investment.” He leaned forward. “They fell to me from the sky, you know. Literally. They came to survey me.”

Quinn smiled while, under the table, her nails left grooves in her palm, and raised a toast to order.

The rest, Tira’s network accomplished in six days. The crew of the Audacity — alive: eleven quaddies and both downsiders; the twelfth quaddie, the captain, killed in the taking of the ship, having “offered resistance.” They were held at refinery R-3, the best in the belt, because they were the best workers in the belt. The doses — “azure,” as the administration itself called them, with commercial poetry — were administered in the blue booths of every habitat, every seventy-two hours, on a biometric schedule that was both leash and bell: miss one, and on the fourth day come the tremors, on the sixth the metabolic crash, on the tenth the end. The chemist who had designed the molecule — Dr. Ines Vara, formerly of House Bharaputra, her biography there having ended in “disagreements regarding intellectual property” — lived in a private dome beside the laboratory. And the laboratory…

“The laboratory is mined,” Tira reported. “Thermal charges, slaved to Valk’s biomonitor and a panic code. He tells every new security officer about it, proudly, like a toast: ‘If I die — the formula dies. I am this system’s dose.’ Six hundred and eight stabilized, Admiral, including our eleven. All of them his hostages. Every day. By design.”

That same night, in the Magpie’s cargo hold, sensors dark, Bel Thorne met the prisoners’ first free envoy — because the quaddies, a people born in a prison, carried in their blood the thing no dose could reach: patient, four-handed conspiracy. The young man, who had slipped over with R-3’s repair shuttle under a borrowed name on the roster, had blue scleras, a worn face, and a smile that did not surrender.

“You’re Bel Thorne,” said Leo Twelve. “Great-aunt Nicol told me about you. She used to say: if you’re ever in trouble so deep there’s no deeper, it means either a Dendarii or a miracle is about to turn up.” He surveyed the hold with all four hands spread in a quaddie gesture that took in the ship, the fleet behind it, and the whole sudden arithmetic of hope. “Which are you?”

“Both, boy,” said Bel. “Talk.”

Leo talked. And on leaving, he left on the table, wrapped in insulating tape, what he had stolen: one full ampoule of azure and a drop of his own blood on a sample strip.

“The leash,” he said. “Take it to somebody who knows how to untie knots. We will wait. We’re good at waiting — we learn it before we learn free flight.”

IV. The Bridge

The fleet’s courier made the run to Escobar and back in nine days, carrying out the molecular analysis and one personal request, and carrying back an answer recorded by Dr. Raven Durona with rolled-up sleeves and the expression the Durona Group reserved for genuinely interesting nastiness.

“Congratulations, Admiral — your enemy hired a talented chemist,” the recording said. “The molecule is beautiful the way a bear trap is beautiful. Full detoxification without the original synthesis library will take us months — and the library is in that mined laboratory. But. There is a bridge. We cannot cure the dependency quickly. We can, however, copy the dose. An agonist substitute, synthesizable from standard precursors on any fleet med-fabricator, indistinguishable in effect from the original, safe for gradual tapering later, in a real clinic. The formula is in the packet. Make it by the ton, Admiral. Because — and this is the important part, pass it to your people as battle doctrine — the chain was never the dose. The chain is the sole supplier. Break the monopoly, and the slavery becomes merely a bad medical history.”

Quinn played the recording to the war council twice. Then she unrolled the plan that had been ripening in her since the quay bar, and it had three blades.

First: the dose. The med-fabricators of the Triumph and the hospital ship went to three shifts; in nine days the fleet produced forty thousand doses of “bridge” — sixty for every stabilized person in the system, in packaging labeled in seven languages: Free. From no one. Forever.

Second: the guns. The eleven mass drivers were terrible — and were crewed, like everything heavy and dirty on Anchor, by blued gun teams under gray guards. “Valk economized on wages,” Quinn told the council. “His artillery is loaded by the people he keeps on the leash. On the day the leash drops, his firepower is going to have opinions of its own.”

Third: the security force. Two hundred and forty mercenaries, paid in kernels and in promises. “I have been a mercenary for thirty-five years,” said Quinn. “I know what a professional thinks when he’s paid in company scrip. We are going to make them an offer at guild rates.”

And above the three blades — the clock: Valk’s panic code and the mined laboratory. That part Quinn entrusted to Tira, two sappers, and one person on the inside, who proved the easiest recruitment of Tira’s career: Dr. Ines Vara heard the offer — immunity, extraction, a job interview with the Durona Group — and answered with a single sentence: “Condition: I disarm the charges personally. Nobody touches my library in sapper’s gloves.”

They set the day: the next system-wide dose schedule, when six hundred and eight people would stand before the blue booths at once. In the Consortium’s calendar it was listed as “stabilization day.”

In the fleet’s plans it went down as Payday.

V. Payday

It began at oh eight hundred system time, when the Magpie, loaded as if for departure, instead opened her cargo hatches onto the main quay, and out of them, instead of goods, came forty Dendarii troopers in full armor — while over Anchor, on every frequency at once, sounded a voice that had spent three decades selling the impossible to half a galaxy:

“This is Admiral Elli Quinn, Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet, under contract to the Union of Free Habitats. I am addressing three audiences and I will be brief, because we all have work to do. First — to the stabilized: containers of your dose are being unloaded at every blue booth as I speak. It is identical, harmless, and free — from today, forever, from any physician on any civilized world. The monopoly is dead. Nobody owns the key to your blood anymore. Second — to the contract hands: your bracelets zero out the moment this system changes management, and it changes management today. Third — to the Consortium security force: I am a colleague. I know what you’re paid, and in what. The Dendarii Fleet guarantees every professional who grounds his weapon in the next thirty minutes safe extraction and payment of your employer’s arrears at standard guild rates, in real money. Anyone who prefers to die for company scrip — I respect the choice, but I don’t understand it. The clock is running.”

The history of the next four hours later entered the asymmetric-operations curricula of three military academies, but on Anchor itself it broke down into simple human scenes. The queue at the blue booth of Habitat B, where a white-haired miner with a twenty-year port in his shoulder held the new ampoule in both hands and read aloud, over and over, the three words on the packaging. The crew of mass driver number seven — four blued under one gray sergeant — who at nine eleven received the order to open fire on the approaching Dendarii frigate, and at nine twelve the sergeant discovered he was alone in the turret, locked out, while the gun transmitted to the frigate nothing but the coordinates of every other battery. A hundred and eighty mercenaries formed up on the quay with empty holsters and the expressions of men who had accepted the year’s best offer. And the laboratory dome, where Dr. Ines Vara, under the muzzles of two platoons and with the precision of a woman defusing her own conscience, switched off the thermal charges one by one and handed Tira the crystal with the library, saying only: “Handle it like a newborn.”

Gedeon Valk did not surrender. Valk did what people do for whom other people are line items: at the height of the collapse he ordered his personal guard to load aboard the armored tug Fist the Consortium’s most valuable movable asset — the eleven quaddies of the Audacity’s crew — and punched through the cordon toward the belt, toward the reserve jump shuttle beyond it, leaving behind him counter-fire and one last promise, screamed into the ether, that the system would burn.

“All ships,” said Quinn, on the Triumph’s bridge now, in a voice cooled to absolute zero. “The tug is not to be fired on. Our people are aboard. Pursue, intercept, board. That man does not leave this system.” A pause. “And, ladies and gentlemen: he has just taken eleven quaddies hostage and cut their gravity so they can’t move about. Eleven quaddies. In free fall.” Into her voice, very slowly, came the smile. “I want to see his face shortly.”

VI. Free Fall

Because Gedeon Valk, born and raised under gravity, had made the mistake of every downsider before him: he had assumed that weightlessness was a cage.

For eleven people conceived, born, and raised in free fall, weightlessness was home, language, and weapon. The moment the Fist’s artificial gravity went out, the eleven “immobilized” prisoners in the cargo bay stopped being cargo and became what they were: the best space workers in the galaxy, with four hands apiece, no direction called “down,” and twenty-eight months of accounts to settle. Leo Twelve opened the maintenance crawlway with a tool he had hidden there himself half a year earlier — “just in case; we’re good at waiting.” Two quaddies went along the cable shafts, where a downsider cannot even turn around, and cut the central distribution. Valk’s guards — eight men, trained, brutal, and suddenly weightless — found themselves in the dark, in free fall, among opponents who flowed along the walls like liquid and had two extra hands for every grip, every disarm, and every very personal blow.

When Quinn’s boarding shuttle docked with the Fist twenty minutes later, the lock was opened for them from inside by Leo Twelve — blue-sclered, with a bloodied lip, and with four thumbs raised in a quaddie gesture that needed no translation.

Gedeon Valk was waiting for them on the bridge, alone, unarmed, already dressed in the calm of a man moving on to his next deal. He looked Quinn over — the armor, the admiral’s insignia, the face — and spoke smoothly, untouched, eternal:

“Admiral Quinn. The Dendarii. So the Union paid… what, two million? Three? I’ll double it. Not as ransom — as a retainer. A system like mine needs a fleet like yours, and you need a client with my margins. Everyone has a price, Admiral. That is the only real law in the galaxy.”

“I know,” said Quinn. “I computed yours my first evening on Anchor.” She took a step forward. “Your price, Valk, is one dose every seventy-two hours. That is exactly what every human being in your system was worth — and by your own accounting logic, exactly what you are worth too.” She nodded to the troopers. “Take him away. Carefully — he’s material evidence.”

VII. The Tariff

The trial was held on Anchor, three months later, before the first elected assembly in the system’s history — contract hands with zeroed bracelets, freed blued with fading scleras, even a few former guards who had stayed by choice — and it ran eleven days, conducted under the Escobaran procedural code with two observers from the Union. Gedeon Valk heard six hundred and forty-one plaintiffs. The sentence, read out by a woman with a twenty-year port scar on her shoulder, was worded with a precision Quinn rated as the finest retribution of her career:

Lifelong labor in the system’s mines. Standard contract. Full tariff wages — from which, to the last kernel, would be deducted the installments to the victims’ compensation fund, computed at a sum the convict, with his accounting skills, could work out for himself: at the most conscientious labor, he would pay it off in approximately four hundred years.

“Sentenced to an honest wage,” said Bel Thorne with deep Betan satisfaction. “In debt forever, by his own arithmetic. You know, Quinn, there are fates no playwright would dare to write.”

The detoxification was already proceeding under Durona protocols — slowly, surely, with a clinic on every habitat and Dr. Vara working, under supervision, on her own repentance, molecule by molecule. The system chose itself a name — they voted for three weeks, and the winner, by a narrow margin over everything solemn, was the simple “Ours.” The Union of Free Habitats signed an assistance treaty with the new republic, and the eleven from the Audacity set out for home aboard the Triumph, because Quinn refused to entrust them to anyone else.

At Graf Station, half a concert hall’s worth of people was waiting. Quinn stood at the side of the dock and watched a small, silver-haired quaddie musician with four arms embrace her grand-nephew with all of them at once — and watched Leo Twelve, engineer, twenty-six years old, his scleras nearly white again, weep openly, shamelessly, and happily in free fall, where tears do not drop away but stay with you until you wipe them yourself.

“Nicol asks,” said Bel, appearing beside her with two glasses, “whether the fleet will stay for the concert. She says she’s composed something new. It’s called ‘The Dose of Freedom,’ and it is played, I quote, ‘with all the hands you have.’”

“The fleet will stay,” said Quinn.

Later, in her cabin, she wrote the short final report to the client, and the even shorter personal letter through four relays to an address on Barrayar — because there were things that, after thirty years, one simply did, like checking the airlock before sleep:

Miles. Thirteen of fourteen, alive, home. The latest man who believed everyone has a price is now paying his own in installments — four hundred annual ones. And write this sector’s lesson down somewhere with yours: the chain is never the dose, the gun, or the bracelet. The chain is the sole supplier. Break the monopoly, and the slave will open his own door, with however many hands he has. The ones here had four apiece. The sight was magnificent. — Q.

The reply came in seventeen hours, two sentences, and made her laugh out loud in the quiet cabin:

Filed, right between “perfection is the liar’s signature” and “the Imperium runs on buffets.” I am beginning to suspect the two of us have spent thirty years writing the same textbook — you just write it cheaper for the taxpayer.

The Triumph left Graf orbit after the concert — which was indeed played with all hands, and from which, in the crew’s general opinion, even the ship’s hydraulics emerged moved — set course for the node and the next contract, and Admiral Elli Quinn, approaching sixty with no intention whatsoever of retiring even once, let alone three times, stood on her bridge and watched the stars of the side branch slowly fall into their familiar order.

Somewhere astern, in a system named Ours, six hundred people were going to bed that night without counting the hours to a next dose. It did not fix the galaxy. But it was, Quinn thought, a perfectly decent wage for one season’s work.

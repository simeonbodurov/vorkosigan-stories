The Formula

An adventure of the Vorkosigan District Guard

I. Hassadar

Lieutenant Tamas Kozlov of the Vorkosigan District Guard had served in three places over his career and liked to say that each was a different kind of quiet. Vorbarr Sultana was the quiet of power — dense, official, expensive. The Dendarii Mountains were the quiet of stone — old, indifferent, honest. And Hassadar, the district center, was the third kind: the quiet of a place that is trying. A town that had pulled itself up in two generations from a dusty county seat into a real district capital, with a university, a hospital, a shuttleport field, and that touching, defensive pride of a province proving that it, too, can.

That was why the first bodies offended him personally.

“Third this month,” said the medical examiner, Dr. Venta, a small woman with eyes that had seen too much and refused to get used to it, which Kozlov respected. “Same again. Young, healthy, no history. The heart simply stops — not arrhythmia, not infarct, but like a switch turned off. In the blood: traces of a compound my toxicology doesn’t recognize. It’s new, Tamas. And it’s clean — synthetic, lab-made, not some mountain brew of mushrooms.”

“Name?”

“The girl called it ‘quiet,’ before she died.” Venta drew back the sheet. “The street calls it ‘silver.’ It gives you peace, Tamas — one perfect, serene hour, for the first time in the life of a kid who never had a single such minute. And then, on the third dose or the fifth, the switch just…” she snapped her fingers. “Off.”

Kozlov looked at the boy’s face — eighteen, nineteen at most, with calluses on his hands from work in the new greenhouses up north — and felt the quiet of Hassadar, the quiet of a place that is trying, crack under something foreign.

“Who’s selling it?”

“That’s your part, Lieutenant,” said Venta. “I only count.”

The trouble was that Kozlov had been trying to count for a month too, and the sums did not come out. The street dealers were small, replaceable, frightened — and all of them told the same useless story: the goods come “from above,” from people who aren’t local, who pay in cash and talk with a lowland accent, and sometimes aren’t from Barrayar at all. Nobody knew of a warehouse, a lab, a name. “Silver” appeared on the streets of Hassadar like dew — out of nowhere, overnight, everywhere.

II. Sergeant Deu

Sergeant Rosa Deu was twenty-nine, born in Hassadar, the daughter of a rebar man from the old construction sites, and she possessed the one quality Kozlov valued above experience: she could not leave a thing un-computed. She came into his office at seven in the morning without knocking, a tablet in hand and that expression he had learned to read as “I’ve found a thread and I won’t sleep until I’ve pulled the whole thing out.”

“Logistics,” she said, instead of a greeting. “Everyone’s chasing dealers. I went in from the back — from the money and the boxes. Silver is sold as powder, in identical little bags. The bags are industrial, one lot, imported. I traced the lot: purchased by a firm called Til Agro, which officially imports food preservatives for the northern greenhouses.” She set the tablet in front of him. “Til Agro imports thirty times more preservative than there are greenhouses to use it. And it pays for storage space that appears at no address of theirs.”

“Where’s the space?”

“An old building-materials warehouse, southern industrial zone, by the dry riverbed. Abandoned for eight years — a bankruptcy from back when the construction boom collapsed. Officially empty.” Deu hesitated, which was not like her. “Lieutenant, I went past it last night, from outside. The warehouse is dark. But the chimneys of the old drying shed are smoking. And the cooling fans are running. An empty warehouse doesn’t breathe.”

Kozlov felt a month of helplessness condense into something hard and useful.

“Surveillance. Discreet. I don’t want to spook—”

“Already did it,” Deu admitted. “Two days. They come and go after dark, cargo cars with no markings, a work shift of six or seven who don’t sleep in town — they arrive and leave together, like a crew. And, Lieutenant…” she produced a second tablet, “…one of them isn’t a nobody. I recognized him. Jonas Merk.”

The name dropped into the office like a stone. Jonas Merk was local — a Hassadar boy Kozlov remembered from petty thefts ten years back, gone off the radar to reappear, as the story went, “on the plains, in bad company.” Merk, who knew every alley of the town. Merk, who would know which officer passed when, and which door creaked when.

“So we have a local leading them by the hand,” said Kozlov. “And outsiders bringing the goods. Deu, this is no longer street dope. This is a channel.”

III. The Warehouse

They raided the warehouse Thursday at dawn, by the book: two teams, back and front doors, stunners, no needless noise — the Vorkosigan District Guard was not large, but Kozlov had trained it to work clean, because in the provinces every mistake is remembered for two generations.

They took six on the spot, including Jonas Merk, who tried to flee across the riverbed and was brought down by Sergeant Deu with a hold about which she said only, “My father taught me to set formwork, and a running man isn’t much different from a warped board.” And they found the laboratory — not a field brew but a real synthesis installation, compact, expensive, professional, hidden in the warehouse’s former drying shed, with reactors processing precursors into white powder with the methodical patience of a brewery.

And they found the thing that turned the whole case on its head.

“Lieutenant.” The technician disassembling the installation’s control console sounded strange. “There’s no recipe here. No recorded procedure, no chemical log, nothing a chemist would leave behind. There’s only… a receiver. The installation doesn’t run on its own program. It receives instructions. An encrypted stream, from outside, batch by batch. Someone somewhere sends the formula — the exact parameters, temperatures, times — for each brew separately, and wipes up after themselves. The locals aren’t chemists, Lieutenant. They’re just… hands. Fingers on a keyboard someone else is playing.”

Kozlov stood among the reactors and felt the case widen beyond his jurisdiction, beyond Hassadar, beyond the district, up and out, as far as cold reached.

Jonas Merk, questioned that same afternoon, proved both talkative and useless — a combination Kozlov knew: a man who wants to help but knows nothing of substance, because he was hired for exactly that.

“I only provided,” said Merk. “A place, people, quiet. Which officer when, which door, which palm to grease. The formula came from above. They paid from above. The goods went up — not all of it stayed here, Lieutenant, Hassadar was a trial, you understand? A test market. They were watching whether it would take, before they put it on the…” Merk stopped, suddenly frightened of his own words.

“On the what, Jonas?”

“On the ship,” Merk whispered. “I heard it once. One of the plains men, on the comm. He said: ‘if the district trial passes, we put the synthesis into orbit and flood half a continent by harvest.’ They aren’t selling dope, Lieutenant. They’re testing distribution. Hassadar was only… the sample.”

IV. The Count

Count Miles Vorkosigan arrived in Hassadar forty hours later, which for a man who had set out from Vorbarr Sultana on receiving the report was at the edge of decency and a little beyond it.

He came not as an Auditor — the case had not yet reached Imperial level — but as Count of the district, which on Barrayar was something older and, in a sense, more dangerous. The district was his. The dead boys were his subjects. And the channel that was killing them had come in through his door.

“Lieutenant Kozlov.” The Count shook hands, brief and firm, and sat down in the cramped office without surveying the furnishings the way capital visitors usually surveyed them. “I’ve read your report three times. It’s clean. Do you know how many district-guard reports survive three readings from an Auditor? This is the first. Especially the part by Sergeant… Deu, is it? She went in from the boxes, not the bodies. That’s the right end from which to catch someone else’s tangle.”

Deu, standing at attention by the wall, blushed to the roots of her hair and said nothing, for which Kozlov silently commended her.

“My lord,” said Kozlov. “We took the warehouse, the installation, and six men. But—”

“But you took the hands, not the head,” Miles finished. “And you know that if you stop here, the channel will open a new warehouse in another district in a month, change the lot of little bags, and carry on, while your six men serve a few years for something they didn’t even understand. You’ve caught the dealers of an interplanetary channel, Lieutenant. I congratulate you — that’s more than most planetary services manage. Now we must decide whether we want to catch the channel too, or merely to warn it that we’re here.”

“The installation receives the formula from outside,” said Deu, breaching protocol because she could not hold it in. “On an encrypted stream, my lord. If we could trace the stream back—”

“We can’t,” said Miles. “Or rather: we mustn’t. The stream is encrypted and runs through relays; tracing it would take months and would warn them. But you’ve missed something, because you think like honest people. The installation doesn’t receive a formula, Lieutenant. It answers. Every batch of powder has slightly different parameters — your technician recorded it: temperatures, times, each time a little different. Why?”

A silence fell, in which Kozlov felt he was about to learn something about his own case.

“Because they’re tuning it,” said Deu slowly. “Optimizing the recipe. Batch by batch. A test market… they’re not testing whether it’ll sell. They’re testing the formula. Hassadar is a laboratory, my lord. We are the experiment.”

“Exactly so,” said Miles quietly. “And an experiment sends data back to the experimenter. Every time the installation pulls a new formula, it first uploads the results of the previous one — how much pure powder, how much waste, how many…” he did not finish, but everyone in the room heard how many corpses. “There’s your thread, Sergeant. We don’t trace the incoming formula. We send an outgoing report the experimenter is waiting for — and make him answer us in person.”

V. The Plan

The plan Miles built over the next two days in Kozlov’s cramped office — with tea, with maps of the continent, and with a growing, reluctant admiration on the part of the entire district guard — rested on one thing: the channel did not know the warehouse had fallen.

The raid had gone quietly; the six had been detained without publicity; and most important — Jonas Merk, the only one with access to the channel’s communication rituals, had broken fast and agreed to cooperate in exchange for what Miles promised him personally, briefly, and without illusions: “Not a pardon. A difference in sentence between a man who carried quiet to my children and a man who helped stop it. That much I can do. Is it enough?” Merk had decided that it was.

And so the installation in the drying shed went on “working” — under the control of the guard’s technicians and one quiet specialist who had come with the Count and was introduced only as “Mr. Vorsu, consultant.” (Kozlov, who was not born yesterday, recognized ImpSec by the bearing and asked no questions.) The installation pulled the next formula on schedule. And then, for the first time, it uploaded a false report — drawn up by Vorsu and Deu together, with data measured to the last digit: the batch is exceptionally pure, the yield a record, the “side effects” below all expectation. In other words: the test market is a success. The formula is ready to scale.

The answer came in nineteen hours, and it was not a new formula. It was a message the channel had never sent before: a request for a personal inspection before “moving to the next phase.” The experimenter was coming to see his triumph with his own eyes — and to collect the optimized recipe in person, because such a thing is not entrusted to an encrypted stream flowing to hired hands.

“Of course he comes in person,” said Miles, with grim satisfaction. “The formula is the only real asset in the whole scheme. Warehouses are built anywhere, hands are hired anywhere, precursors are imported under a thousand names. But a synthesis recipe optimized to perfection, tested on a living market — that is the commodity you load onto a ship and flood a continent with. He won’t leave it in other people’s heads. He’s coming to take it.” The Count stood. “Lieutenant Kozlov, Sergeant Deu — this is your case and your district. I am here as a Count who covers your back and takes the interplanetary part when it comes to that. The arrest is yours. Only…” he smiled, for the first time genuinely, “don’t let him slip. This one won’t come through our door a second time.”

VI. The Inspection

It came on a rainy night, with a private shuttle set down on one of the outer fields of the Hassadar shuttleport, and a groundcar without markings that headed for the southern industrial zone by a route Jonas Merk had betrayed down to the last turn.

Kozlov had closed the zone like a fist — quietly, from the outside in, with the patience of a man who had waited more than a month for exactly this night. Deu was in the drying shed, beside the “working” installation, in a shift worker’s coveralls, a stunner under the apron and that calm Kozlov had seen in her only when something was finally coming out to a whole number.

The experimenter entered accompanied by two guards, and proved not to be what they expected. Not a plains boss, not a Jacksonian broker. A thin, middle-aged woman with glasses, an instrument bag, and the precise, economical movements of someone who has spent her life in laboratories — an appearance Kozlov would have passed on the street a thousand times. She surveyed the installation with professional approval, nodded to the “workers,” opened her bag — and when she took out the data reader to collect the optimized recipe from the console’s memory, Sergeant Rosa Deu said quietly, behind her back, in a pure Hassadar dialect:

“You’re a chemist. That’s why the boys died on exactly the third dose, not the second and not the fifth. You calculated the threshold. You optimized it — the closer to death, the ‘purer’ the peace. You counted the corpses like waste in production.” Deu stepped forward, and the stunner in her hand was already drawn, though pointed down, by regulation. “I’m telling you so that you know someone understood it. You’re under arrest.”

The woman — they later established she was a former pharmacologist from a large Escobaran company, laid off and embittered, who had sold her talent to an interplanetary syndicate for which Hassadar really had been only a spreadsheet — offered no resistance. Her guards tried; but the zone was a fist, and the Vorkosigan District Guard, though small, had been trained by a man who remembered that every mistake is remembered for two generations. In eleven minutes it was all over, without a single shot from a real weapon, without a single dead — which Kozlov, remembering Dr. Venta’s corpses, counted as the one statistic that weighed on him correctly.

Miles appeared only when it was all finished — deliberately, as Kozlov understood afterward. The Count came into the drying shed, looked at the arrested chemist, looked at the data reader in Deu’s hands — with the optimized formula, the channel’s one real asset, already in the hands of the law — and nodded slowly.

“Now my part begins,” he said quietly. “The formula will not see a court, Lieutenant. It will vanish into a very deep drawer in Vorbarr Sultana, together with everything this woman knows about the syndicate up above. And I will spend the next months pulling the thread you handed me, upward — through the relays, to the ship, to the people who meant to flood continents. It is ugly, slow, invisible work, and no one will ever write about it.” He turned to the whole room. “But I want it clear who did the important thing. You stopped the channel. I only clear up after you.”

VII. The Quiet of Hassadar

The case dragged on for months upward and weeks downward, and the two halves rarely met, as is proper.

Downward: the six from the warehouse received sentences measured by guilt — Jonas Merk the lightest, per the Count’s promise, honestly kept. The chemist vanished from the public registries into that deep drawer from which people do not come out, but from which sometimes comes information that saves other districts. The channel up above — Miles worked at it quietly all autumn, and though he never told the details, somewhere in midwinter a cargo ship was detained at Komarran customs on a tip with no sender, and a syndicate for which Hassadar had been a spreadsheet discovered that the spreadsheet had turned against it.

Upward, Hassadar learned almost nothing. But downward, in the town itself, the quiet returned — the third kind of quiet, the quiet of a place that is trying. Dr. Venta stopped counting young, healthy people with switched-off hearts. The northern greenhouses brought in their harvest. And one evening, in the station, Lieutenant Kozlov found on his desk a short note in a hand he now recognized by its vertical pressure:

Lieutenant Kozlov. I gave your report two more readings — five now. It holds. Tell Sergeant Deu that her line in the drying shed (“someone understood it”) went into my private notebook, and I admit few things there. The district is in good hands — better than the Count deserves and exactly as good as his subjects deserve. If the provinces ever bore her, ImpSec will fight over her; in your place I wouldn’t tell her. — M.V.

Kozlov read the note twice, smiled, and did not show it to Deu — partly on the Count’s advice, partly because he knew that Rosa Deu, daughter of a rebar man, born in Hassadar, had no intention whatever of going anywhere. She was already investigating the next thing — a small seed-smuggling matter, nothing special — with that expression he had learned to read as “I’ve found a thread.”

He folded the note, put it away in the drawer with the other things worth more than a view, and went back to his work — district, provincial, invisible, important. Outside, over Hassadar, it was raining quietly, on a town that was trying, and this time nothing foreign was breathing in the dark.

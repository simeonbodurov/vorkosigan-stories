The Inheritance of Earth

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Contract Without a Name

Earth always did the same thing to Elli Quinn: it made her feel at once at home and a stranger visiting her own childhood. The blue-white globe beneath the Triumph was the mother of all worlds — the cradle, the museum, the old woman of the galaxy who received her grandchildren with an equal mixture of pride and irritation. Quinn had come here young, with a face that was not yet her own, on a mission that had nearly killed a very short admiral. Twenty-eight years later she returned with admiral’s tabs, a refit bill, and a contract that had no name.

“The client insists on complete anonymity,” reported Captain Herald, which was already a violation of the second rule above Quinn’s door. “But the payment guarantee is through Banque Esaba, Escobar — the hardest escrow in the sector. The money is real, Admiral. Too real for a task described in three sentences.”

The three sentences read: A position has been stolen. Trace it to its final destination. Do not permit it to arrive.

“‘A position,’” Quinn repeated. “Not ‘a shipment.’ Not ‘a person.’ A position.” She examined the precious nebulosity of the client’s identity and felt the old prickle at the back of her neck that had twice saved the fleet and once her life. “Someone has lost something so badly they dare not name it even to the people they hire to get it back. Herald, we accept — but we investigate the client alongside the task. I don’t like being the knife without knowing whose hand it’s in.”

The first link was on Earth itself, in a name the escrow document had failed to scrub deeply enough: Transcultural Institute of Applied Genomics, Facility “Ashmore,” County of Surrey, European Union. A private laboratory with government contracts, a fence without a sign, and, according to open records, a specialty in “hereditary diagnostics for high-status clients.” Nine days earlier a fire had broken out at Ashmore — chemical, localized, burning out exactly one wing and exactly one server array. The Earth police were treating it as an accident. Quinn, who had arranged similar “accidents” in her youth, read it as the erasure of a crime scene.

“We’re putting a team down,” she decided. “Discreetly. I’m leading.”

“You?” Herald raised an eyebrow. “Admirals don’t crawl under fences, Elli.”

“This one does.” Quinn was already checking the comm on her wrist. “Besides, I have an old debt on this planet, and old friends who remember it. It’s time I paid them a visit.”

II. London

London had aged the way only truly old cities age — by adding layers without removing the previous ones. Quinn came down into the misty dusk among towers, cathedrals, and advertising holograms reflected in the river, and for a moment she seemed to see her own ghost at twenty-five, running along these very streets with a stolen face and a stolen courier pack.

Her old acquaintance awaited her in a club without a sign in Mayfair, where membership was measured in generations. Commodore Destang was long since dust, but ImpSec on Earth had no habit of dying — it merely changed its faces. The current one was called Lisa Vio, chief of the Barrayaran residency, Komarran by origin, with exquisite manners and a butcher’s eyes.

“Admiral Quinn.” Vio did not bother to feign surprise. “The Dendarii Fleet in Earth orbit is always cause for insomnia in my department. To what do we owe the honor?”

“To a laboratory in Surrey that burned down right on time.” Quinn saw no point in circling; Vio was the sort who respect the direct approach more than charm. “I’m looking for a stolen ‘position,’ Captain Vio. Not a weapon in a box. Something a private genomic institute kept locked away, and someone decided was worth an entire wing of fire to make vanish without a trace.”

Vio studied her for a long moment, weighing. Then she made a decision Quinn recognized: share a little, to see whether the other knows more.

“Ashmore didn’t do diagnostics,” she said quietly. “It did authentication. Wealthy houses from three planets sent them samples to prove — or dispute — lineages. Inheritances worth billions hang on a single chromosome, Admiral. And lately Ashmore had taken on a private commission from someone far above its usual clientele. We know this because our own source inside died in the fire, before he could tell us for whom.” She leaned forward. “If your client hired you to recover a stolen genetic sample, Admiral Quinn, then we are both chasing the same ghost. And my department would be most grateful to you not to lose it.”

Leaving the club, Quinn already knew two things. First: the weapon was not a device. It was information — or biology. Second: ImpSec on Earth feared it enough to offer cooperation to mercenaries, and ImpSec did not frighten easily.

The third thing awaited her at the hotel, in the form of Lieutenant Tira of the recon platoon, come down under cover as a student, her face gone gray from what she had dug out of the burned remains of Ashmore.

“I recovered a fragment of the lab journal, Admiral. Charred, but legible.” Tira set the tablet on the table with a hand that trembled just perceptibly. “Do you know what they authenticated in the last commission? A bloodline. One specific one. Traceable, documented, with branch points and… with a viability assessment of each branch.” She looked up. “Admiral, this isn’t a family tree. It’s an order form. Someone commissioned a report telling them which carriers of a certain blood are alive, where they are, and how difficult it would be to make them not.”

Quinn felt the room freeze around her.

“Whose blood, Lieutenant?”

“The seal of the commission is burned. But the delivery coordinates are not.” Tira turned the tablet. “The final destination of the ‘position,’ Admiral, is Barrayar.”

III. The Shape of the Weapon

Quinn convened a war council on the Triumph that same night, over a secured channel to Escobar — because there was one man in the galaxy who understood Barrayaran bloodlines and weapons tailored to a single genome, and he owed her a few favors from thirty years back.

Mark Vorkosigan’s face rose on the screen — broader and calmer than his brother’s, but with the same sharp eyes.

“Admiral Quinn.” He heard her out without interrupting, which Quinn appreciated. When she finished, Mark was silent a long while. Then he said: “Do you know what the oldest weapon on Barrayar is? Not the needler. The pedigree. A whole planet built on the question ‘whose son are you.’ Legitimacy there isn’t a document — it’s blood. And if someone has commissioned an authenticated map of an Imperial bloodline…”

“Imperial?” Quinn interrupted. “I said ‘high house,’ not—”

“Think, Admiral.” Mark’s voice was level, and the more terrible for it. “What house on Barrayar is worth an entire wing of fire and the hiring of a whole fleet? A bloodline whose authentication — or forgery — could shift the throne.” He leaned toward the camera. “Someone isn’t stealing a sample in order to kill. They’re stealing it in order to prove something. Or to fabricate it. The weapon you’re chasing doesn’t go off. It legitimizes.”

After the call Quinn stood a long while alone on the darkened bridge, assembling the shape of the thing in her head. Not a bomb. Not a poison. A genetic key — a sample and its accompanying authentication — capable of turning a pretender into a lawful heir. Or of reducing a lawful ruler to an impostor. On a planet where blood was the constitution, this was a weapon of mass destruction wrapped in a single test tube.

“Herald,” she said at last. “The trace. How far have we gotten with the ‘position’s’ route?”

“It left Earth six days ago, Admiral, on a freight run with Arista Lines — a legitimate carrier, clean profile. Transit through Escobar, then the Polian League, then…” Herald frowned. “Then the trail splits into three. Three different couriers, three different jump routes into Barrayaran space. Only one carries the real cargo.”

“Classic.” Quinn rubbed her temples. “One real, two decoys. Follow the wrong one, and we lose weeks while the ‘position’ arrives.” She looked at the globe of Earth through the viewport. “The answer is down there, Herald. Whoever organized the triple split chose the couriers from here, from Earth. Somewhere on this planet there is a person who knows which is the real one. And I’ll find him before he can board a ship.”

IV. The Hunt

The next three days were the purest old work Quinn had done in years — the kind she had thought her admiral’s tabs had deprived her of forever. The Dendarii recon team, spread across London, Geneva, and the old spaceports of equatorial Africa, assembled the mosaic piece by piece: who had hired the three couriers, who had paid, who had burned in Ashmore.

The threads gathered into one name, and it was neither Cetagandan nor Jacksonian. It was Barrayaran — old, Vor, with the patina of respectability: an agent named Horace Vorbrett, officially the trade representative of a certain countship in the Earth consortium, unofficially a man whose translations and travels over the past year drew a picture of preparation. Not for a coup with a weapon. For a claim with documents.

“Vorbrett isn’t the principal,” Quinn told Vio, at a second meeting, this time in an ImpSec safe house, because the two had already passed from cautious rivalry to cautious alliance. “He’s the hand. The hand is always here, with the couriers. But someone on Barrayar is paying Vorbrett — someone with blood ancient enough to believe an authentication could bring him closer to the throne, and a mind modern enough to hire an Earth institute and a mercenary fleet instead of brandishing a sword.”

“We have a suspect,” said Vio quietly, and for the first time Quinn saw in her eyes not a butcher but a frightened professional. “But it’s one of those names one doesn’t utter without proof. And the proof has just left the atmosphere.”

Because Vorbrett had hurried. Quinn’s intelligence caught him at a private spaceport outside old Nairobi forty minutes before departure — boarding a fast courier, a class far above a trade representative, with diplomatic immunity and a small thermal container shackled to his wrist with that particular cuff Quinn had seen too many times in her career.

“He’s gone with the real one,” she said, watching the recording. “All the feints were to keep us here while the principal received the signal that the hand was on its way. Herald, intercept course. Which of the three routes is his?”

“Checking… he’s on none of the three declared ones, Admiral. He’s taken a fourth. Direct. A diplomatic corridor through the Komarr nexus — the fastest route into Barrayaran space, open only to immunized traffic.” Herald looked up, pale. “He’ll arrive in nine days. We’re at nine and a half, if we jump now and run the Necklins into the red.”

“So we’re twelve hours late.” Quinn closed her eyes. Twelve hours. Half a day between stopping a weapon and its falling into the hands of a man with imperial ambitions. “We can’t outrun him. We can’t stop him — he has immunity, and violence against a diplomat in Komarran space would provoke exactly the crisis someone wants.” She opened her eyes, and in them already gleamed what the crew called “the admiral’s smile” — the harbinger of something at once brilliant and appalling. “So we won’t stop him. We’ll arrange for his cargo to arrive useless.”

V. The Move

The plan Quinn built over the next hour was contrary to every mercenary instinct, because it involved not a single shot, not a single boarding, not a single violation of immunity. It involved telephone calls, documents, and one very old truth about the way weapons of blood work.

“The weapon is an authentication,” she explained to the war council. “A sample plus a certificate that says ‘this is the true blood of line X.’ Its power lies in the trust placed in Ashmore as an independent, incorruptible authority. Destroy the trust, and the sample becomes a cloudy liquid in a test tube. We can’t steal the shipment. But we can poison the well.”

“How do you poison a burned-down laboratory?” asked Herald.

“By resurrecting it just enough to make it talk.” Quinn turned to Vio on the screen. “Captain Vio, your source died in the fire — but he managed to send you something beforehand, didn’t he? Otherwise you’d not have known about the private commission. Give it to me. And I’ll give you something in return: I’ll bring the whole mechanism into the light.”

The mechanism was this. The Dendarii team, still on Earth, documented with irrefutable evidence that Ashmore’s certificates from the past year — including the one in Vorbrett’s container — were issued by a compromised laboratory: a bribed director, falsified chains of custody, a fire to cover the tracks. All of it true; Quinn fabricated nothing, she merely brought the buried to the surface. Then, with Vio’s help and that of an Escobaran legal consortium, she registered this truth officially — not secretly, but publicly, in the Interplanetary Registry of Genomic Authentication, the body whose seal gave Ashmore’s certificates any value at all.

On the day Vorbrett’s courier emerged from the Komarr nexus into Barrayaran space, every certificate bearing the Ashmore mark from the last twelve months had already been officially, publicly, and irreversibly annulled as the product of proven fraud. The sample in the thermal container shackled to his wrist was still real blood. But the paper that said whose it was, and that it was authentic, was now worth no more than the test tube itself.

“He carries a key,” said Quinn, watching the tactical map, “to a lock I’ve just changed. His blood is real. But no court, no Council of Counts, no Auditor will accept it, because the authority that was to confirm it has been publicly disgraced by its own archives. The pretender receives his shipment — and finds he holds in his hands not a crown, but an indictment against himself.”

VI. The Principal

There remained the principal — the man on Barrayar whose imperial ambitions had set the whole mechanism in motion. Quinn could not arrest him; he was beyond any jurisdiction of hers, and besides she already suspected the true denouement did not belong to her. But she could name him — to the right person.

“My client,” she said to Vio, at their last meeting before the Triumph left orbit. “The whole time I investigated who hired me. A triple anonymous chain, the hardest escrow in the sector, a task described so as to name nothing. Do you know who hires mercenaries to stop a weapon, but dares not give his name even to them? Someone who knows that the investigation itself, if it goes wrong, could turn against him. Someone very close to the target.”

Vio was silent. Then she said slowly:

“You think the client and the principal—”

“Are not the same, but are in the same room.” Quinn shook her head. “I think my client is someone from the throne’s closest circle who scented the plot from within, could not be sure which precisely of his colleagues was behind it, and dared not trigger the official channels, because the official channels leak. So he hired an outside knife — me — with a task shaped so that even the knife would not know whose back it pierced.” She smiled wearily. “I did a favor to someone very high up, Captain Vio. And the best part is that I’ll never know exactly to whom — and he’ll never know exactly whom he stopped. The two of them, principal and client, may have sat at the same table yesterday. That is Barrayar.”

The denouement came on Barrayar, far from Quinn’s eyes, and she learned of it only by the absences: a countship whose representative in the Earth consortium was suddenly recalled; an old lord who just as suddenly retired “for reasons of health” to his most remote estate; a diplomatic courier named Vorbrett who never appeared in any official registry, as if he had never left Nairobi. ImpSec had taken up the ghost from the point at which Quinn had disarmed it, and had swallowed it without a sound, the way Barrayar swallowed everything that must not be spoken.

VII. The Cradle

On the night before the jump Quinn went down to Earth one last time — not on business, but on something that had no name in any contract. She stood on the bank of the Thames, where twenty-eight years before a young woman with a stranger’s face had run, frightened and alive, and watched the lights of the old city reflected in the river that had flowed here before humanity had dreamed of even one other planet.

Her comm chimed — a blind link, four relays, a voice she knew better than her own:

“I hear you’ve been for a walk about the old neighborhood.” Miles Vorkosigan sounded amused and somehow wary. “And that Barrayar has lately gone very quiet around a few names that used to make quite a noise. Quiet in that particular way.”

“I don’t know what you’re talking about,” said Quinn. “I’m a simple mercenary. I returned a lost shipment to an anonymous client and collected my fee. Doubled, by the way. The client was pleased.”

“Simple mercenaries don’t bring down succession plots with an accounting registry, Elli.”

“This one does.” She smiled at the river. “Do you know what’s funny, Miles? Twenty-eight years I’ve chased weapons across this galaxy. Needlers, warheads, viruses, fleets. And the most dangerous thing I ever stopped turned out to be a sheet of paper that says whose son you are.” She paused. “Your planet is built on the oldest weapon in history, and most of you don’t even see it.”

“Oh, we see it,” said Miles quietly. “We’ve just learned to live with a finger on the trigger. That’s why we fear so much the one who decides to pull it.” A pause. “Thank you, Quinn. Whoever you saved. Whatever you stopped.”

“Go tend your gardens, Auditor.” Her voice softened against her will. “And tell Ekaterin I’ve seen the Thames and thought of her. She’ll understand why.”

Quinn cut the connection, stood a moment longer above the old river, then raised her eyes to orbit, where the Triumph awaited her — her true home, the only one that did not ask the question “whose are you.” In the morning the fleet would jump to the next contract, the next sector, the next weapon with a new face.

But tonight Admiral Elli Quinn stood on the bank of humanity’s cradle, an old woman among grandchildren, and allowed herself a rare, quiet pride: she had returned to the planet where it all began, and had left it a little safer than she had found it. The river flowed toward the sea, indifferent and eternal, carrying the lights of the city downstream, toward the morning.

Quinn followed them with her eyes until they turned behind the towers. Then she turned her back on Earth and went up — toward the stars, which were her true homeland.

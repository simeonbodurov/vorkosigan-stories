Living Memory

An adventure of Lieutenant Danilo Vorlev

I.

The first thing Lieutenant Danilo Vorlev learned about modern Vorbarr Sultana was that nobody watched the sky anymore.

In his time — and he still thought of it as his time, whatever the calendar claimed — people looked up at every overflight. A shuttle over the capital meant news: reinforcements, refugees, a landing. Now the shuttles threaded past like trams, and the passersby never lifted their heads from their wrists, where small glowing screens talked to them, showed them things, and, as far as Vorlev could tell, did their thinking for them.

ImpSec had issued him one. He wore it the way one wears a foreign decoration — carefully, and with discomfort.

He walked from the station, because the map on his wrist offered an eleven-minute route by groundcar and he needed two hours. The city had climbed upward — towers of glass where he remembered barracks. On one square a Cetagandan cultural attaché with a painted face was handing out invitations to an exhibition, and no one was reaching for a weapon. A patrol passed him — two soldiers in Imperial green, one of them a woman — and Vorlev stopped in the middle of the pavement so abruptly he was nearly knocked down.

Fifty years, he reminded himself. You’ve read the files. Reading is one thing.

His family’s house still stood on the old street, smaller than he remembered it, the way all childhood houses shrink. The door was opened by a woman of sixty-eight, with a cane and his mother’s eyes.

“Dani,” said his sister Irena, who had been nineteen when he lifted off for Komarr. “You’re half a century late for dinner. Come in, I’ve warmed the soup.”

He held out, without weeping, almost to the end of the sentence.

Later, in the garden, she showed him the pictures: their parents, grown old and buried while he dreamed in the ice; her own wedding; her son; her grandson — a sergeant in the Imperial Service, six years older than his uncle Danilo. That last one they spent a long time trying to explain to each other, until they gave up and laughed instead, because the alternative was unbearable.

About Natalia he did not ask. Irena, with an older sister’s mercy, mentioned nothing. The file — because the first thing Vorlev had done with his new access was unforgivable and human — said: married, widowed, three grandchildren, living. The address sat in his memory like an unsent letter.

II.

ImpSec headquarters hadn’t changed at all, and that was almost comforting. The same gigantic ugly windowless box about which, in his day, they whispered that the architect had been shot — not for the ugliness, but to prevent a second one.

The change was inside. The change received him in a top-floor office and spoke with a slight, impeccable Komarran accent.

“Lieutenant Vorlev. I’m General Galeni, Chief of ImpSec.” A pause, measured to the millimeter. “Yes, a Komarran. I advise you to get through the astonishment phase now, all at once. You have thirty seconds.”

Vorlev used twenty-two of them.

“Done, sir. My apologies. In the time I come from—”

“In the time you come from, my father was throwing rocks at Barrayaran patrols. The Imperium is a strange place, Lieutenant, and getting stranger. The Empress, as you have presumably read, is Komarran.” Galeni slid a thin folder across the desk. “The question is what to do with you. Medicine has returned you to duty. The psychologists are writing reports about each other’s reports. Your skills are fifty years old. I am offering you the archives, and I won’t insult you by pretending it’s a promotion.”

“A burial with salary, sir?”

“With a good salary,” said Galeni. “You are the wealthiest lieutenant in the history of the Service. Forty-nine years of back pay, with interest. Accounting still lights candles against Lord Auditor Vorkosigan.”

So Vorlev settled two levels underground, among shelves he remembered full and that now stood half-decanted into crystal, and took up the one thing for which he was indisputably qualified: reading. By day he authenticated documents from the era of the Pretendership — the fiftieth anniversary was approaching, and the historians were scratching at the doors for declassification. By night he read everything else. The war at Vervain. The colony on Sergyar. The Regency — all of it, from first day to last, with the strange sensation of reading his own mission’s obituary. The sentence the Emperor married a Komarran he reread five times, and then went and made himself tea.

Lord Auditor Vorkosigan dropped by once a week — officially on Auditorial business, unofficially the way a man checks on a tree he has planted himself.

“There are complaints about you, Lieutenant,” he said on one of those visits, with evident satisfaction. “You’ve returned three documents to the historical commission as forgeries. The commission maintains they are priceless testimony.”

“The commission reads signatures, my lord. I knew the lieutenant colonel who supposedly signed them. He put a period after his rank. Always. His hand put it there, not his mind. The forger read the regulations, but never saw the man.”

Miles leaned forward with the expression of a terrier that has heard rustling under the floorboards.

“Say that again.”

III.

The folder Miles brought the next morning had nothing to do with history. It was a current counterintelligence case, frozen for eight months: someone was passing data from closed archives to a Jacksonian information broker. The channel had never been found. The only lead — three intercepted transmissions on a dead military band, encoded flawlessly in a protocol that appeared in no active registry.

Vorlev recognized them by the second line.

“This is Captain Negri’s courier network, my lord. The private one. I was part of it.”

“Negri has been dead for fifty years, and his protocols were declassified for internal use thirty years ago. A clever move — a channel that reads as historical noise. An archival ghost.” Miles drummed his fingers on his cane. “So the sender has read the same folders you authenticate.”

“No, my lord. The sender has read only the folders.” Vorlev laid the three printouts side by side. “The checksums are correct. All three of them.”

“Is that bad?”

“It’s impossible. Negri required the third block always to carry an error of one. Deliberate. Unwritten. The verification lived in the imperfection — perfection is the liar’s signature, he used to say. He never wrote it down anywhere. He taught it aloud, in a room with no recorders.” Vorlev looked up. “Whoever sent these knows the protocol from the documents. Not from the life.”

There followed a silence in which Miles visibly reconsidered several possibilities, including pleasant ones.

“Is there a living soul besides you who knows that rule?”

“There was one man who knew everything, my lord. A junior lieutenant on Negri’s staff, with a phenomenal memory. Illyan, his name was.”

“Simon Illyan,” said Miles, his voice gone suddenly gentle, “is ninety years old, and the memory you are speaking of burned out a quarter of a century ago. Long story. No, Lieutenant. That leaves you. One man, one unwritten error.” He smiled in a way that made Vorlev remember every briefing on hazardous operations at once. “How would you feel about lying to the archive?”

The plan was simple in the way good traps are simple. Vorlev composed a message on Negri’s network — an emergency meeting summons under Protocol Winter, old designated point, the colonnade behind the Ministry of War, third column, one hour before closing. The message was encoded flawlessly — with correct checksums in all three blocks. Any living graduate of Negri’s school would have smelled the forgery from ten kilometers and stayed away. A man who had learned the network from the folders would see only a perfectly legitimate order.

At the appointed hour, past the third column came an archivist second grade from the declassification section, with a spotless service record and a small crystal in the lining of his sleeve. In his report Vorlev later noted that the apprehension proceeded “in accordance with established procedure.” Established procedure included Armsman Roic, who at sixty-one still collided with fugitives like a dresser with a wardrobe, and a Lord Auditor watching from the shadows with the expression of a man whose evening has furnished him aesthetic satisfaction.

The archivist, already cuffed, looked at Vorlev almost with offense.

“The protocol was flawless. I verified every checksum against the original registries. Who even are you?”

“You read history, sir,” said Vorlev. “I remember it.”

IV.

The order came through a month later, signed by Galeni personally: a new position on ImpSec’s establishment — consultant in historical authentication — with an access level that made half the department triple-check the document’s date.

“Officially, you’re the first,” said Galeni. “Unofficially, you are the only one possible. Your job is to be living memory, Lieutenant. The Imperium has records enough. It turns out records lie more easily than people do.”

That same week Lady Ekaterin Vorkosigan brought him a seedling from the garden of Vorkosigan House — a young ash tree in a pot, with a note on how to plant it. Vorlev planted it in Irena’s garden, next to the soup and the photographs, and stood a while over the fresh earth.

“Trees don’t hurry,” said Ekaterin, who understood patient things. “And yet they always arrive on time.”

That evening he sat down at the comconsole, entered the address that had sat in his memory for months like an unsent letter, and this time did not delete it. Natalia answered on the third chime. What they said to each other entered no archive — and Vorlev, newly appointed keeper of living memory, found that perfectly correct.

The next day Miles found him in the archive, buried in documents for the fiftieth anniversary.

“Well, Lieutenant. Didn’t I tell you time could be outlived?”

“You did, my lord.” Vorlev stamped the latest forgery and reached for the next folder. “But the truth is otherwise. Time cannot be outflanked, and it rarely lets itself be outlived. Time can only be recruited.”

Miles laughed — and in that laugh, Vorlev thought, there sounded for a moment a very short admiral about whom the archives held not one word.

Pearls

An adventure of senior Armsman Roic — one Winterfair at Vorkosigan House

I. The Twentieth

The recruit arrived at Vorkosigan House on the first day of snow, with one canvas bag, a letter of recommendation, and the expression that senior Armsman Roic recognized point-blank, because twenty-seven years earlier it had stood on his own face: the expression of a man certain that at any moment he will be exposed as a misunderstanding and returned by post.

The letter was from Lieutenant Tamas Kozlov, Vorkosigan District Guard, Hassadar, and consisted of four sentences, which by Hassadar standards was an entire ode: “Timo Brezov, seven years’ service, three commendations. Not a hero and doesn’t want to be one, which we value here. He watches. Nothing more to add — that is the whole profession.”

“Brezov,” said Roic, setting the letter down. “Do you know how many armsmen a Count keeps?”

“Twenty, sir. By Vorloupulous’s Law.”

“Twenty. And do you know which number you are, if you last the year?” Roic did not wait for an answer. “The twentieth. Last on the roster, youngest in the mess, the man who carries what nobody wants to carry and stands where nobody wants to stand. I was the twentieth twenty-seven years ago. Now I am the first, which means I carry more and stand longer. If you’ve come for the cloak and the salute at the gate — the gate is where you came in. If you’ve come for the work — the work starts at five in the morning and is called the rounds.”

“I’ve come for the work, sir,” said Timo Brezov. Then, after a brief inner struggle that Roic followed with approval, because the boy lost it honestly: “Sir, permission for a question. You have served with the Lord Auditor on three planets. Explosions, kidnappings, frozen cities — we read about them at the academy, with the cuts left in. And I’m from the Hassadar municipal guard. I’ve watched markets and fairs. Why me?”

Roic looked at him for a second — the big Hassadar boy with the honest hands — and in that second there passed through him, as it passed more and more rarely and more and more gently with the years, one very old memory: another recruit from the municipal guard, standing in this same vestibule among armsmen-veterans of ImpSec, convinced to the marrow of his bones that he had walked through the wrong door of his life.

“Because Kozlov writes ‘he watches,’” said Roic. “And that, Brezov, is the whole profession. The rest is details. Come. I’ll show you the house.”

II. The School

The showing of the house took three weeks, because Roic showed not the rooms but the work, and the work of an armsman, as he taught it, consisted of things no academy teaches.

“You don’t watch guests’ faces — you watch their hands. The face lies professionally; the hands lie only in the very best.” Rounds of the ground floor, ten past five in the morning, their breath steaming in the dark corridors. “Doors get counted. Always. You enter a room — exits first, then windows, then people, furniture last of all. Not because something will happen. Because one day it will, and then you won’t have time to count.”

“And when is the weapon drawn, sir?”

“In the ideal career — never.” Roic stopped by the window onto the snowed-in garden, where at a quarter to six, on schedule, the light came on in the greenhouse: milady was beginning her day. “Listen to me carefully, Brezov, because this is the first real lesson and most men learn it late: an armsman is not a wall. Walls can be bought. An armsman is attention. We are the people who watch this house eighteen hours a day, year after year, until we begin to hear it like our own pulse — and then, on the day something in the pulse skips, we are the only ones who will feel it before the shot, not after. Hence the rounds. Hence the counting. Hence the boredom, boy — boredom is the raw material of the trade. Attention is brewed out of it.”

Timo Brezov nodded and — Roic checked — remembered. After the first week he remembered which third-floor window jammed in the cold, and was already opening it with the exact motion; he remembered that m’lord, when he worked at night in his study, left the door ajar a hand’s width — “not for the air, Brezov: a man who spent his childhood in hospitals doesn’t like closed doors, but you didn’t hear that from me”; he remembered that the small Lady Simone must not be offered help with her pack, and that the twins were to be believed when they said “we’re not doing anything.” The house accepted him, as it accepted everything, patiently and without ceremony, and somewhere in the middle of the third week Roic caught the recruit standing in the great vestibule at six in the morning, simply listening — head tilted, with that expression.

“Do you hear it yet?” asked Roic.

“Beginning to, sir,” said Timo. “The pulse. I understand what you mean.”

“Good. Because in nine days it is Winterfair, the house will take three hundred guests, forty hired waiters, and a mountain of gifts — and its pulse will triple.” Roic looked at the snow beyond the windows. “Winterfair, Brezov, is the favorite holiday of thieves, of spies, and of poisoners, and for one and the same reason: more of the world passes through these doors in that week than in all the rest of the year. I’m putting you on the gift log. Every box, every ribbon, every courier. Dull work.”

“The raw material of the trade, sir,” said the twentieth armsman, and Roic for the first time allowed himself to think that Kozlov might have sent him not a recruit but a successor.

III. The Fair

Winterfair at Vorkosigan House that year was double: besides the customary reception, the house was celebrating the betrothal of Lord Aral Alexander to Dr. Dana Izar — quietly announced in the autumn, now being presented to society — and the guest list read like an excerpt from a future history textbook. Roic laid out the security plan like a campaign: outer perimeter with ImpSec, inner — armsmen only; the hired staff checked through three registries; a gift log with a chemical scanner; and a roster of posts calculated so that in every room at every moment there would be at least one pair of eyes that did not drink, did not dance, and did not rest.

“Three hundred and eighty-one boxes by noon,” Timo reported on the third day of preparations, with wrapping-paper dust on his sleeves. “The scanner flagged two: one turned out to be Komarran cheese of an age beyond reason, the other a mechanical raven that Count Vorbretten is sending m’lord with a note reading ‘you know why.’ I passed the cheese and held the raven for re-inspection, because I don’t know why.”

“Correct on both counts.” Roic signed the log. “The raven is a joke between m’lord and the Count, an old one, from a case. But jokes get checked most carefully of all, Brezov: the easiest way to bring something into a house is to dress it as a jest. People inspect threats. Smiles go through without queueing.”

On the evening of the reception the whole house lit up — window after window, as it lit once a year — and swallowed its guests like a well-commanded ship: cloaks to the cloakrooms, names to the master of ceremonies, three hundred people through the halls, the kitchen at full steam, music from the gallery. Roic took his place — in the corner of the great salon, from which both doors, the staircase, and the mirror showing the third door were visible — and switched on that vision of his which twenty-seven years of service had turned from sense into habit: he did not watch the party. He watched the deviations from the party.

For the first two hours there were no deviations. M’lord circulated through the halls with a glass he never emptied, in his small whirlwind way; milady held the gravity of the evening with that calm which Roic had suspected for years was the most trained thing in the entire house; the betrothed couple was besieged by well-wishers; at the far end Lady Alys Vorpatril received homage, and beside her old Illyan dozed with his eyes open — a trick Roic envied professionally. The hired waiters moved along their rehearsed routes, forty men in identical livery, invisible as good waiters are.

Thirty-nine of them were invisible correctly.

IV. The Small Mistakes

Roic noticed him at twenty past ten, and later, in the report, had difficulty naming the first thing — because the first thing was not a thing, but a sum of nothings.

A waiter, middling height, nondescript, with a tray of glasses. The livery — exact. The documents — Roic knew — clean, passed through three registries. The route — per the agency’s schedule. All in order. Except that his shoes were half a size too wide in the toe — the shoes of a man used to standing long and starting fast, service shoes, which waiters do not wear, because waiters’ feet hurt differently. Except that the tray rode on his left palm with a balance that left the right hand free always, at every passing — not sometimes: always. Except that at every entry into the salon his eyes performed that quick, smooth, professional sweep — doors, windows, people, furniture last — which Roic taught his recruits as high hygiene and which, in a waiter, was like a foreign accent. And except that in two hours this man had not once ended up with his back to m’lord. In a crowded room where everyone turns, that is statistically impossible. Unless it is intent.

“Brezov.” Roic did not move from his corner and did not change his expression. “The waiter by the south table, with the tray of white glasses. Don’t look at him. Tell me where he is.”

A pause of four seconds — the recruit was learning to watch with the corner.

“South table… he’s moved, sir. Now by the column. Eleven meters from m’lord.” Two more seconds. “Sir, he is standing where both m’lord and the great mirror can be seen. That is… that is your position, sir. He stands like us.”

“Yes,” said Roic. “He stands like us. Go down to the agency’s office by the back stairs and ask for the personnel card of man number thirty-four on tonight’s list. Not over the comm — in person. And look at the photograph, not the name. Names lie professionally.”

Timo came back after eleven minutes with a face that was trying to be calm and almost succeeding.

“The photograph in the card is of another man, sir. The resemblance is careful — height, weight, haircut — but the ears don’t match. Ears don’t take makeup. I called the agency from the office, on the wired line: the real waiter number thirty-four has been sick for three days. Reported by comm. By voice.”

“They paid him to be sick or made him sick — the same thing for the moment.” Roic was already calculating: distances, routes, corridors. There must be no alarm: three hundred guests, a betrothal, imperial cousins through the halls — panic in such a room kills more surely than a knife. Extraction — quiet, along the waiter’s own route, into the service corridor behind the kitchen, where the trays are reloaded and where one vanished waiter makes no scene. “Now listen to the order of it, Brezov, and remember it, because this is the second half of the trade — the half after the watching. Nobody runs. Nobody shouts. You will go to old Yanko at the kitchen post and say three words to him: ‘row three, south.’ He knows. Then you stand at the service door and let no one through — with a smile, with an apology, with whatever you like. I will deliver to our man an invitation that is not declined.”

“Sir… he’s a professional. If he’s carrying something—”

“He’s carrying. That is why the order is this one and not another.” Roic straightened his cuffs with the gesture other men use to draw breath. “And, Brezov — good work with the ears. The tray and the shoes I saw myself, but the ears would have escaped me. Two watch better than one. That is why there are twenty of us.”

V. The Pantry

The invitation that is not declined Roic delivered at ten fifty-three, in the service corridor, in his deepest ceremonial voice:

“Colleague. The head chef asks for you personally — the special tray for Lady Alys. This way, if you please.”

The man went — professionals go, because refusal unmasks faster than error — and the three steps across the threshold of the cold pantry were enough for him to understand, and for Roic to act. The scuffle, if it could be called that, lasted four seconds and fitted between two shelves of goose pâté: the professional was fast, trained, and fifteen years younger, but the pantry was narrow, and Roic, besides being large, had spent twenty-seven years studying how people move in narrow spaces, and on top of that — this is what decided it — he made no attempt whatsoever to be elegant. When Timo flew in after them, the senior armsman was already holding the guest packaged in his own jacket, face to the shelving, with a professional gentleness that leaves no marks for the protocol.

“The right glove,” said Roic. “Carefully. Don’t touch it with skin.”

The glove — white, a waiter’s, indistinguishable — proved to have a double layer on the palm, and in the double layer, when it was unpicked under the lamp: a membrane with a contact agent, good for one single handshake or one touch on a bare wrist while presenting a glass. A quiet weapon for a patient man: no shot, no scene — only a Count-Auditor who would be taken ill in his own study an hour later, and a diagnosis that even good toxicology chases for a week.

The guest in the jacket kept a qualified silence, which impressed no one: Roic had seen how Galeni’s people worked, and a man with someone else’s ears in his card and a membrane in his glove was no longer a guest but material evidence with a pulse.

“ImpSec will take him out through the freight entrance at half past eleven, between the second and third course,” Roic told Timo, locking the pantry from outside. “Until then you guard him. Your first solo post, Brezov: one locked pantry and one silent professional. It’s dull. Boredom is the raw material of the trade.”

“Sir.” Timo swallowed. “Who sent him?”

“That the departments will chew for months, and they will find three shell companies and one middleman with amnesia. But if you’re asking the old armsman…” Roic looked toward the salon, from which drifted music and the laughter of three hundred people who would never know anything. “M’lord has lately stepped on several very long tails. Channels, consortiums, archives. Tails like that don’t forgive — they only wait for crowded evenings. Well — tonight the tail stayed stepped on. Stay here. Watch.”

VI. The Dessert

Roic reported to m’lord at ten past midnight, in the library, where the Count had withdrawn for three minutes of silence between toasts — an old habit of his that the armsmen guarded like a state secret.

He reported in his own way: in order, dry, ninety seconds — the waiter, the ears, the pantry, the glove, the quiet handover to Galeni’s people through the freight entrance, completed twenty minutes earlier without one head turned.

Miles Vorkosigan heard the report standing, the glass of unfinished wine in his hand, and at the end was silent a moment.

“A contact agent. At my son’s betrothal. In the house where my children sleep.” His voice was level in that way that made the Auditor’s subordinates check the exits. Then the Count set down the glass and asked only: “When did you spot him?”

“At ten twenty, m’lord. Removed at ten fifty-three.”

“Thirty-three minutes. And I learn of it at ten past midnight.”

“It was necessary not to interrupt the dessert, m’lord,” said Roic. “Panic in a full room is more dangerous than most weapons. And besides… the case was domestic. Domestic cases are our work. Your work is to raise toasts and step on tails — with all respect, m’lord, you do both superbly.”

M’lord looked at him for a long second — from below upward, as he had looked at the whole world for fifty-six years — and in the look was everything that had gone unsaid between the two of them for three decades: Earth, Kibou, the frozen city, the years.

“Roic,” he said at last. “Do you know what date it is today?”

“Winterfair, m’lord.”

“Twenty-five years, Roic. Tonight, twenty-five years ago, another professional brought into this house another poison, wrapped as a gift, and it was caught not by the security, not by ImpSec, and not by me. It was caught by a visiting sergeant — by the smell, because she watched this house with her whole being, though she had been its guest barely a week.” Miles took up the glass again. “I remember, Roic. Every year I remember. And I know that you remember otherwise. So I will say only this, as a Count and as a man: she would have been proud of tonight’s work. Your school is her school. Go. And… happy Winterfair, Armsman.”

“Happy Winterfair, m’lord,” said Roic in a level voice that cost him more than the four seconds in the pantry, bowed, and went out.

VII. The Pearls

The house emptied toward three, went to bed toward four, and by a quarter to five was breathing deep and even — three hundred guests to their homes, the family to their rooms, the dishes to the kitchen, the silence to the corridors — while in the senior armsman’s room, on the table under the lamp, lay open a small wooden box of the kind men in service carry all their lives from garrison to garrison.

In the box, on dark-blue velvet, lay pearls.

Roic did not touch them — he only sat and looked at them, as he looked at them every year on this night, ever since a Dendarii courier had delivered them with a short official letter and one unofficial note from Admiral Quinn: “She said: to him. He knows why.” A strand of pearls that had passed through one wedding, through one assassination attempt, through milady’s hands, and through eight years on the throat of the largest, fastest, gentlest woman the galaxy had ever produced and had not deserved — eight years which, for her hastened metabolism, were one whole long life, lived at full throttle, without one wasted minute, the way people live who know the price of minutes.

There was a knock at the door — quiet, by regulation. Timo Brezov, finished with his first solo post, the report on the prisoner’s handover written across his face together with all the questions his upbringing would not let him ask. Roic, for reasons he did not analyze, did not close the box.

“Come in. The post?”

“Handed over clean, sir. The General’s people signed the log.” The boy stood to attention; his gaze crossed the pearls and returned to the far wall with an effort that deserved a commendation.

“Ask,” said Roic.

“It’s not my business, sir.”

“It is. As of tonight you are an armsman of this house not by oath but by work — and the armsmen of this house are owed this story. Sit.” Roic turned the lamp so the light fell on the strand. “Twenty-five years ago, boy, I was the twentieth — junior, from the municipal guard, certain I had walked through the wrong door. At m’lord’s wedding a parcel of pearls arrived at this house — these — and in the pearls there was death, prepared for the bride. No scanner caught them. I didn’t catch them. They were caught by a guest — a sergeant of m’lord’s mercenary fleet, a soldier out of a genetics program, two and a half meters, claws like a leopard’s, and eyes such as I have not seen since. She caught them by the smell. Because she watched — with her nose, with her skin, with her whole being. She taught me the trade in one week, Brezov. And she taught me one thing more, which this is not the place for, except to say that it was the best week of my life, and that after it there were more, a few, because her measure was cut short and she knew it, and lived double.” He paused. “Her name was Taura. Sergeant Taura. When milady gave her this strand — cleansed, real — she wore it to the end. And then it came back to me, because she had so ordered. Since then, every year on this holiday, I take it out and go over the lesson. The whole lesson.”

“Which is, sir?” asked Timo very quietly.

“Which is: watch now.” Roic closed the box with both hands, carefully, the way one closes something living. “The walls, the doors, the guests’ hands — that is technique; it can be learned. But the lesson is something else: everything we guard is on a term, Brezov. The people, the house, the holidays — everything has its time cut short, only most don’t know it and live at half attention, postponed for later. She had no later, and so she watched truly — every room, every face, every second, all the way down. An armsman guards people. So an armsman watches like her: as if today’s rounds were the only ones. Do you understand me?”

“I understand you, sir,” said the twentieth armsman of House Vorkosigan.

“Then march off to sleep. Rounds are at five.” And when the boy was already at the door, Roic added, without turning, in the voice with which toasts are raised: “Brezov. To Sergeant Taura — the sharpest-hearted guard who ever passed through this house. She served it one week. She has guarded it twenty-five years.”

Outside, over Vorbarr Sultana, snow was falling — slow, fat, festive — piling on the sills of the great quiet house, on milady’s garden, on the roofs of the city, and at ten to five, ten minutes ahead of time, senior Armsman Roic went out into the corridors for the morning rounds: the doors, the windows, the pulse of the house, everything in order, all the way down, as if these rounds were the only ones.

The work was to watch. The rest was details.

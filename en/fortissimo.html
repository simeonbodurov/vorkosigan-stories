Fortissimo

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn — on holiday

I. The Retirement

The retirement of Admiral Elli Quinn took effect on Friday at noon, galactic standard time, after thirty-nine years of service, and lasted — as was later documented to the minute — forty-nine hours.

The ceremony aboard the Tuning Fork was brief at her explicit insistence: the ranks, the colors, the handing of command to Admiral Herald — “the fleet is yours, Lisbet; break it and I come back from the grave, and from a pension one comes back even more easily” — and one single tear, shed by Chief Pilot Kera Volan and denied by her to the end of time. The letter from Barrayar arrived an hour before the liner’s departure and consisted of three sentences, which Quinn read aloud to the officers’ mess, because a thing like that is not kept to oneself:

Quinn. The galaxy owes you more than it admits, and I owe you more than fits in a letter, so I will say only the main thing: retirement is an operation with an uncertain outcome and a high recurrence rate — ask my mother, she has retired four times. I bet you a case of Sergyaran red that your retirement doesn’t last a week. — M.

“It will,” Quinn informed the mess. “Out of spite.”

The destination had been chosen a year before, at a dinner on neutral ground, when Consul Lord Ivan Xav Vorpatril, flushed with homemade wine and pride, had described to her the water world where he served: the archipelagoes under the two moons, the consular residence on stilts over the lagoon, and — voice lowered, eyes gleaming — the monsters. The uplifted leviathans of Ylla: eighty-ton singers, once the whim of a ghem aesthete, today a protected community that was learning the human world with the speed of a child and the patience of an ocean.

“Come hear them,” Ivan had said. “Telling you about it is one thing. Standing on the boat while something the size of a frigate sings you a serenade from below is another.”

The serenade took place at hour forty-six of the retirement, on the first outing of the consular boat. The leviathan surfaced at two hundred meters — a back like an undersea ridge, an eye the size of a dinner plate, looking straight at her with an attention that had nothing animal about it — and began to sing. A deep, layered sound Quinn felt first with her breastbone and only then with her ears; and by the third measure she went still, because she knew the melody.

The Dendarii march. The parade march of her own fleet, sung by eighty tons underwater on a foreign world — slow, solemn, and absolutely true.

“That’s Octava,” said Padma Vorpatril, fifteen, from the stern, where he was calibrating his hydrophone. “The matriarch. She greets guests with a song from their homeworld — she dug up your march on the net last night when she heard you were coming. They collect songs, Admiral. The way humans collect faces.”

“And they know who I am?”

“They know what you are.” Padma grinned. “Octava named you — hold on, let me translate exactly… ‘she around whom the water falls into order.’ I don’t know what it means. They always know before we do.”

II. The Pearl

At hour forty-nine of the retirement, Elli Quinn lay in a bathrobe in the underwater resort habitat Pearl, at forty meters’ depth, watching through the panoramic viewport as a school of silver fish dissolved into the blue, and practicing her new principal occupation: doing nothing. The practice was going badly.

The Pearl was the pride of tourist Ylla: a nacre bubble of eight linked domes, anchored on four tether-pylons above the edge of the continental shelf, exactly where the shallows dropped into the Great Deep — a colossal undersea canyon whose floor the brochures called “unexplored” with unconcealed delight. Two hundred and eleven guests, forty staff, a spa (the booking was a gift from Tej, presented with the words “this, or a formal dinner with the governor — choose”), a panoramic restaurant, and a neighborhood no other hotel in the galaxy could boast: the protected lagoon to the north, where the leviathans brought their school pod — six calves and two adult teachers — and where Dr. Von Sarto’s station translated between the two species with the patience of a border service.

Friday evening’s forecast promised a seasonal cyclone, category three, passing a hundred and twenty kilometers to the east. The Pearl was certified for category five. The manager mentioned it at dinner in the tone in which one announces that the dessert features local fruit.

Typhoon Fortissimo — Ylla’s meteorological service had named its storms after musical terms ever since the year of the Leviathan Suite, a tradition considered charming until Saturday morning — changed category overnight. A warm deep current, anomalous, unforecast, fed it like a furnace; by dawn it was category five and turning; at eight ten it was five-plus and no longer turning anywhere, because its center was headed straight for the shelf.

At nine forty-one the first anchor line parted with a sound that went through all eight domes like a stroke on a bell.

III. Fortissimo

For the next eleven minutes the Pearl held as designed, and the design held against almost everything. The second line parted at nine fifty; the third at two minutes to ten; the fourth, the southern one, did not part — it ripped its entire anchor block out of the shelf, and the habitat, suddenly free, heavy-graceful as a torn-loose balloon, went with the storm current: south, along the shelf edge, trailing beneath it four hundred meters of cable with a fifty-ton anchor at the end, and with a slow, sinister trim toward the deep, because in the wrenching the ballast system of dome three had swallowed water and refused to give it back.

Inside, what happens happened: two hundred and eleven civilians, a tilting floor, flickering lights, and panoramic viewports showing the familiar bottom receding. The first three minutes — bewilderment; the next three — panic in the stalls.

In the seventh minute, onto the bar of the panoramic restaurant — the highest point of the central dome — climbed, in a bathrobe over a swimsuit and with a voice trained for thirty-nine years to cut through shipboard alarms, a woman of sixty-two.

“Ladies and gentlemen, attention!” The voice settled over the hall like a calm hand. “My name is Elli Quinn. Admiral, Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet, retired.” A pause, measured to the milligram. “As of two days ago. I hereby declare my retirement temporarily suspended. The situation: the habitat has broken loose and is adrift, but it is airtight, and your air will last for days — the structure guarantees that; I checked the certificate at dinner, because I am a boring person. The danger is not here and now; the danger is later and deeper, which means we have the most precious thing in the trade: time. We are going to use it. Staff — to me for assignments. Guests with medical, engineering, or maritime training — to the right of the bar. Parents with children — to dome five; it rides highest. Everyone else: sit down, breathe, and remember this morning well, because it will be the story of your lives, and the stories of lives are best told by the living. Move.”

Later the manager of the Pearl would swear that at exactly that moment the floor stopped rolling. The floor had not stopped. The hall had.

The inventory took an hour and produced a picture Quinn classified mentally as “bad, with material to work with.” Communications: dead — the surface antenna buoy had gone with the first line, and through fifty meters of water and a typhoon nothing in the emergency locker could punch. Propulsion: none — the Pearl had maneuvering thrusters for precise positioning, laughable against a storm current. Ballast: dome three unmanageable; the rest held, but the depth gauge crept: fifty-one meters, fifty-three. Course: south-southeast along the rim of the Deep, which meant the question was not whether the habitat would reach the canyon, but when — and how many meters past its certified depth.

And in dome one, at the wet dock for the tourist submersibles, waited the problem Quinn refused to call a problem: two leviathan calves from the school pod, scattered by the storm, had followed the only large familiar object in the maddened water — the habitat — and were now pressing against the dock, keening on frequencies that made the glass resonate. One was bleeding from a long gash along its flank — the anchor cable had lashed it at the breakaway.

“Open the wet dock,” Quinn ordered. “Bring the injured one inside; the dock is wide enough for a submersible, it will take a baby monster too. The other won’t fit — but it won’t leave its brother either, so it will follow us.” She watched, through the dock glass, the little one — “little,” eight meters and ten tons — trembling and singing. “And get me someone who understands the songs. Because those two are now singing at full power, and their mothers hear them at three hundred kilometers. Ladies and gentlemen, our distress signal is already out. On the oldest channel this world has.”

IV. The Network

Ashore, in the consular residence on stilts, whose shutters rattled under Fortissimo, Consul Lord Ivan Xav Vorpatril was living through the worst morning of his career, and living through it — to his own vast irritation — competently.

Ylla’s rescue service was drowning: a hundred and forty emergencies at once, half an archipelago without power, all aircraft pinned down. The Pearl — with Admiral Quinn aboard, with two hundred souls, adrift, silent — hung in the operational summary as a line without a continuation: “contact lost, position unknown, assistance impossible until frontal passage.” Meaning two days. Meaning too late.

“It is not unknown,” said Dr. Von Sarto. She had arrived at the residence soaked to the skin, translation terminal under her arm, without invitation and without wasted words — the woman who had once uplifted leviathans for another man’s vanity and had spent five years redeeming it, translating for them eighteen hours a day. “Octava knows where the Pearl is. To the meter. The calves are singing; the pod hears them; the pod sings to my buoys. Consul — the leviathans are offering us a network. Theirs. Their song goes through a typhoon the way your radio cannot: to them water is a conductor, not an obstacle. If we lay hydrophone repeaters along the line between shore and habitat, Octava will carry our words there and back. A living link.”

“Deploy buoys. In this.” Ivan looked at the window, where horizontal rain was testing the glass. “With what? Everything that flies is—” He stopped, because he was a father and because he already knew the answer, and the answer tasted like seawater. “Alys!”

His daughter appeared in the doorway in four seconds, already in a flight suit, with the expression of a person who has been eavesdropping from the next room and has agreed to everything in advance. Behind her, Padma held three hydrophone repeaters and his drone in an armful.

“The consular shuttle is storm-rated,” said Alys Vorpatril, eighteen, holding the flight certificate her father had signed with trembling and pride. “Not for five-plus, but I won’t be flying through the front — I’ll fly under it, on the wave deck. The drops follow Padma’s route; he releases them by drone from the cargo hatch; I only drive. Father. Two hundred people. Say ‘yes’ before Mother says ‘I’m coming too.’”

“I’m coming too,” said Tej from the corridor.

The flight of the consular shuttle under Fortissimo’s belly entered the family chronicles under a title Ivan never approved (“my children and my wife out for a drive”) and lasted two hours and eleven minutes. Three repeaters settled along the line with a precision Padma credited to the drone, and the drone, had it been able, would have credited to Padma. And at fourteen oh three, in the Pearl’s wet dock, where Quinn stood beside the injured calf, the enormous shadow of Octava rose out of the blue beyond the glass, laid her head against the dome — and the habitat filled with a voice: layered, deep, patiently pronouncing human words through the translation buoy with a whale’s solemnity.

“She-around-whom-the-water-falls-into-order. The shore hears you. The little one with you — does he breathe?”

“He breathes,” said Quinn into the hydrophone. Her throat, unexpectedly, was tight. “The wound is sutured. He is asleep in our dock. You guard us; we guard him. Tell the shore: we have until the rim of the Deep and not one hour more. And tell them, Octava… tell them the admiral is assuming command of the rescue operation. If the shore has no objection.”

A pause — as long as it took the song to go and return through three hundred kilometers of water.

“The shore says,” sang Octava: “‘Glory to all the demons — proceed.’ The shore says one more word the buoy does not translate. The consul is laughing and crying at once. Among humans, is that one feeling or two?”

“One,” said Quinn. “It’s called ‘family.’”

V. The Flotilla

The flotilla Elli Quinn assembled over the next ten hours, by living link through eighty tons of matriarch, was — every participant would swear to it afterward — the most motley rescue force in the history of civilized seafaring.

Five deep-water trawlers from the southern fishing grounds, with winches that had hauled everything in their lives except a hotel. The harbor tugs of Port Yasen, two of them, out in open water against regulations and with the blessing of a harbormaster who ruled that “a regulation that doesn’t save people is ballast.” The Pearl’s own supply submarine, caught ashore by the storm and loaded now with cable instead of croissants. The yacht Selena of an Escobaran magnate — sixty meters of vanity with, as it turned out, superb heavy winches and an owner who, to Quinn’s question “your ship, or a cabin in the front row of history?” answered within a second and a half. And ten adult leviathans of Octava’s pod, stationed along the drift line like living lighthouses, singing courses to the flotilla through waves in which no radar saw anything at all.

The plan Quinn dictated through Octava to everyone at once, with sung clarity:

“Nobody lifts the Pearl — she weighs as much as all of us together. Nobody needs to. The task is one: stop her before the rim and hold her over the shelf until Fortissimo passes. We do it with lines. Trawlers and tugs — galley oars: we hook on, we haul across the current, we shift the drift degree by degree toward the shallows. The submarine carries the final lines to the habitat. And there where no submarine can maneuver in this current — at the attachment points themselves, against the hull — the work goes to those who were born in water like this.” A pause. “Octava. Can yours carry a line?”

The answer came after a long, many-voiced underwater conference, which Von Sarto ashore listened to with tears in her eyes and declined to translate in full.

“We carried the nets of the storms before humans came,” sang Octava. “A line is a thin net. We will carry it. But hear me, She-around-whom-the-water-falls-into-order: hauling in water like this is not done by numbers. It is done in time. Your machines pull when they please — that way the lines will part. You will pull when the wave allows, all as one. And the beat…” — into the song, impossibly, came something like a smile — “…the beat I will give. Humans once wrote us a suite. We return the gesture.”

Thus came about the thing that the black boxes of five trawlers preserved for history and no recording ever truly conveyed: the work song of Ylla. Octava sang the beat — deep, four-count, passing through the hulls like a pulse; the ten leviathans took it up along the line; and on every fourth stroke, exactly in the phase of the wave, seven ill-assorted vessels and eighty tons of living patience heaved on the lines together, shifting twenty-two thousand tons of hotel by half a meter per measure. Half a meter per measure. Hour after hour. Through a night in which the ocean tried to tear everything out of everyone.

In the Pearl’s wet dock, beside the sleeping calf, two hundred and eleven people listened to the pulse of their own rescue passing through the hull, and the teacher of the school excursion from dome five — twenty-six children, not one of them crying anymore — began to sing with them, in time, the oldest song she knew. Octava heard it through the hull. And wove it into the work song.

VI. The Rim

The rim of the Great Deep came at four eleven in the morning, an hour ahead of the estimate, because Fortissimo, dying, flung its last storm current like a whip.

The drift leapt. Two lines parted with a sound like guns — the trawler Swallow broached and survived only because Octava put her back under its keel, which its captain told afterward in the taverns of three archipelagoes with nobody believing him. The Pearl, heavy with the water in her ballast, sagged her southern end out over the canyon — twenty-two thousand tons on the rim of four kilometers of blackness, held by five lines, six vessels, and one song.

“Depth sixty-eight,” the habitat’s engineer reported, in a voice flatter than it had any right to be. “The certificate ends at eighty. The southern ballast is flooding cell two. We’re sagging, Admiral. The lines hold the drift, but they do not hold the weight.”

Quinn stood in the wet dock, palm on the glass, beyond which the enormous eye of Octava hung in the dark water, and ran the sum that did not come out: the winches — at limit; the tugs — at limit; time until the storm current passed — forty minutes; time until eighty meters — twenty-five.

“Octava. We need a shoulder. Not pull — support. Fifteen minutes of something holding the southern end from sinking while the current dies. I know what I am asking. I know what it weighs. Tell me it’s impossible and I’ll look for another way.”

Silence in the dark water. Then Octava’s song changed — dropped into a register the hydrophones no longer rendered as speech but as decision — and out of the blue beneath the Pearl’s southern end there rose, one by one, ten backs like ten undersea ridges. The leviathans of Ylla took station beneath the keel of the human hotel — a living scaffold, a row of shoulders, eighty tons each — and took the sagging end upon themselves, thrusting with their flukes, rhythmically, in time, against all the remaining weight of the ocean.

For fifteen minutes ten monsters held two hundred and eleven people above the abyss. In the sixteenth, the storm current broke. In the eighteenth, the lines, suddenly light, drew the habitat north, toward the shallows, toward life; in the forty-fifth, the Pearl settled in the lee of Nacre Atoll, onto a sand plateau at thirty meters, and touched bottom with a sigh that passed through every dome.

In the wet dock the calf woke at the silence, asked something in three notes, and received its answer from outside — from its mother, from Octava, from the whole pod: a long, unfolding, many-voiced chord which Von Sarto ashore, after hesitating, entered in the operations log as one word, because no more were needed.

Alive.

VII. The Name

Fortissimo died over the northern latitudes two days later, spent and inglorious. The Pearl’s reckoning read: two hundred and eleven guests and forty staff — alive to the last; nineteen injuries, none grave; one leviathan calf with a sutured flank and a lifetime right, decreed by the hotel’s management in a legal document of unusual emotion, “to berth in the wet dock of the Pearl, for himself and his descendants, forever.” The habitat would be repaired by the season. The work song of Ylla, recovered from five black boxes, was performed within the month by three planetary orchestras, and not one performance, by the unanimous opinion of the eyewitnesses, was worth anything.

The ceremony ashore Quinn tried to avoid and failed, because on Ylla gratitude was a natural disaster of its own class. She received it on the quay of Port Yasen, in a bathrobe — she insisted on the bathrobe — and there, before the flotilla, the governor conferred on her the strangest post of her career: honorary admiral of the rescue flotilla of the archipelagoes, without salary, without staff, with a single obligation: “to be findable at need.” Quinn signed. She observed aloud that it was the first contract of her life whose fee was the expression on Ivan Vorpatril’s face at that moment.

And after the ceremony, when the quay had emptied, Octava surfaced by the pilings — alone — and sang. A short song, four phrases, and Von Sarto, listening at the terminal, raised to Quinn a gaze in which professional translator’s impassivity had failed once and for all.

“They are giving you a name, Admiral. A true one, their own — not a description but a name, the kind they give their own. It doesn’t translate exactly, but…” she swallowed. “Your name is a measure. Four beats, even, strong, that do not stop. A beat others can haul to. That is what you are in their language. Forever.”

Elli Quinn — sixty-two years old, thirty-nine years of service, forty-nine hours of retirement, and one rescue operation in a bathrobe — stood a long time on the quay, listening to her own name sung by eighty tons of gratitude, and said nothing, because there are moments for which even her voice had not been trained.

In the evening, on the residence terrace, she wrote two replies. The first — to Admiral Herald, on the query “when are you coming back”: I’m not. The fleet is in the best hands I ever trained. And I have accepted a new post: I command ten leviathans, one retiree’s armchair, and an unlimited supply of sunsets. The fee is a song. Kiss the Tuning Fork on the bridge for me.

The second — to Barrayar, through the four relays, brief: Miles. You owe me a case of Sergyaran red: the retirement held. Technically. The interruption was temporary and duly declared; witnesses — two hundred and eleven. P.S. An eighty-ton being here sang me a name that means “a beat others can haul to.” Thirty-nine years I’ve hunted for a definition of our trade. A whale found it. I don’t know why I’m not surprised.

The answer arrived in sixteen hours: The case is on its way. The name is exact — whales evidently read people better than Imperial honors commissions. Rest, Quinn. You held the beat long enough; let the others do the hauling now. And when the water gets stirred up again somewhere — because it will — I know whom it will fall into order around.

Quinn set down the tablet, took her glass, and looked out at the lagoon, where under Ylla’s first moon Octava’s pod was performing its evening song — and woven deep inside it, for those who knew how to listen, sounded a parade march from a distant world, slowed to a lullaby.

The water was falling into order. The beat was being given by someone else. It was, the retired admiral established with some astonishment, entirely enough.

The Survivors

An adventure of Lady Ekaterin Vorkosigan on Cetaganda

I. The Invitation

The invitation arrived at Vorkosigan House through the Cetagandan embassy, on a sheet of living paper that shifted its hue with the angle of the light, and was addressed — this was the first thing everyone noticed — not to the Lord Auditor and his wife, but to “Lady Ekaterin Vorkosigan, author of gardens,” personally, independently, and solely.

The Celestial Garden of Eta Ceta — the palace complex of the Cetagandan emperor, the most perfect cultivated space in human history, a place whose name was a synonym for haut aesthetics itself — was inviting a Barrayaran to design a garden within it.

“It’s a trap,” said Miles, reading the invitation for the third time. “Or an honor. With them, the two are one discipline.”

“It is a plot of eighteen decares in the southeast quadrant, authority to select species without restriction, and a formulation I want to read to you aloud,” said Ekaterin, who was reading the annex. “‘The Celestial Garden contains twelve thousand years of edited beauty. The commission wishes to add to the collection one space it does not possess: a garden of unedited life, arranged by a hand that respects it. The choice of author was unanimous.’” She looked up. “Miles. They want wild species. Unimproved. In the heart of the Celestial Garden. It’s as if… as if the Council of Counts commissioned a Cetagandan genetic sculpture for the chamber of Vorhartung Castle.”

“Which is exactly why it’s a trap,” said Miles. “Half the ghem court will take the invitation as an insult to haut art. The other half will take it as a move in a game we can’t see. And the whole weight of it will land on one Barrayaran woman with a trowel, alone, thirty jumps from home, in a court where even the flowers have factions.” He put down the sheet. “When do we leave?”

“We don’t,” said Ekaterin, gently and finally. “I leave. The invitation is to me, Miles. The first in my life in which nowhere, in not one line, does ‘Vorkosigan’ appear in any sense other than my signature. If an Imperial Auditor with two of their emperor’s medals stands beside me on that plot, the garden becomes diplomacy. And it must be a garden.” She smiled at his expression with twenty-five years of tenderness. “I will write every evening. And I’m taking Grandmother Vorvayne’s shears — if the court of the Celestial Garden means to test a Barrayaran, let it know that Barrayarans arrive with tools.”

The shears — heavy, hand-forged, inherited through four generations of South Continent women, their handles polished by palms — provoked at Cetagandan customs a fourteen-minute consultation on three levels, at the end of which the senior inspector returned them to their case with a bow and the formulation Ekaterin entered in her journal as the first local victory: “A tool of the craft, of sentimental value. The Empire welcomes the master.”

II. The Celestial Garden

The Celestial Garden could not be described, which had prevented no one from trying for twelve centuries. Ekaterin, led through it on her second day, gave up on descriptions by the third hectare and moved on to what she knew how to do: reading.

She read perfection. Trees whose crowns repeated mathematical series; flowers changing color in chord with the sun’s movement; lawns on which not one blade grew off the score; ponds with fish whose schools swam in calligraphy. Everything alive here had been edited, polished, signed — generations of ghem artists and haut geneticists had rewritten every genome the way one rewrites a draft — and all of it was beautiful in a way that made her eyes ache after the third hour, the way one aches from a text without a single pause.

Her plot was in the southeast quadrant, behind a wall of coral oaks: eighteen decares, leveled, sterile, the soil replaced to three meters’ depth — “a clean page,” as ghem-Lady Miara Soren, the young horticulturist assigned as her liaison, put it with a bow. No clean page, of course: Ekaterin knelt, took a handful of the delivered soil, rubbed it between her fingers, and asked how many generations of microflora had been exterminated in the sterilization. Ghem-Lady Soren blinked her painted eyelids and admitted she did not know. By evening she knew. By week’s end she carried soil under her manicure and took notes with a fervor she carefully hid from the rest of the court.

The court watched. Ekaterin felt the watching constantly — polite, layered, patient: ghem lords strolling “by chance” past the plot; a formal dinner at the house of the curator of living collections, ghem-Lord Saren Ketu, whose courtesy was so perfect it cut (“Unedited life, Lady Vorkosigan. What a… brave concept. Though a weed is also a concept, provided one has no neighbors”); and every morning, exactly at dawn, one white haut-bubble — an impenetrable sphere of force field, such as the haut ladies rode — which floated soundlessly to the edge of the plot, stood there for half an hour, and departed without a word, day after day.

“Who is she?” Ekaterin asked on the fifth morning.

“One does not ask,” whispered ghem-Lady Soren, pale under her face paint. “A bubble in the Celestial Garden can only be… one does not ask, my lady. Ever.”

Ekaterin nodded, turned to the bubble, and bowed — exactly as much as a gardener bows to a guest who has come before opening hours. The bubble stood a while. Then, just perceptibly, it inclined in reply.

III. The Survivors

Her concept Ekaterin presented to the commission on the tenth day, without holograms, with one pot in her hands.

“One hundred and ten years ago,” she said, “this planet governed mine for twenty years. I will not speak of the people; I am not a historian. I will speak of the plants. The terraforming bombardments, the burned zone around Vorbarr Sultana, the defoliants in the Dendarii Mountains — Barrayaran flora survived all of it, as it has survived everything for five hundred years: without anyone’s help. The species I will plant here are the survivors. Snag-bush, which roots in ash fields. Iron fern, which overwintered under burned forest. Crater knapweed from the shell-hole plains. Not one of them is beautiful by your standards. Not one is edited — except by hunger, cold, fire, and time, the oldest editors, who work unsigned.” She set the pot on the table. “And this is the center of the garden. A skellytum, a Barrayaran succulent. This particular plant is eighty-one years old. It was raised by my great-great-aunt, inherited through five generations of women of my family, carried to Komarr, where… where it was shattered. To pieces. Under bad circumstances.” Her voice did not waver; she had trained it. “It was rooted anew from one fragment. Everything you see grew from the piece. The garden, honored members of the commission, will be called ‘The Survivors,’ and it will assert one single thing: that there is a beauty which is not designed but earned — and that respect for life begins where the desire to correct it ends.”

A long Cetagandan silence fell — one of those in which positions are redistributed.

“The commission accepts the concept,” said the presiding haut at last, whose face expressed nothing in a way that expressed a great deal. “With one note, Lady Vorkosigan: in this garden, for twelve centuries, no one has spoken the word ‘bombardments’ in public. We observe that the sky did not fall. Proceed.”

The work flowed. The shipments from Barrayar arrived weekly — seeds, cuttings, microflora in cryo-containers, South Continent soil, over which Ekaterin fought a four-day battle with phytosanitary control and won it with paragraphs; the plot turned from clean page into draft, from draft into text. In the evenings she wrote to Miles. His replies began calm (“Proud of you. The house is boring. The cook’s cabbage is conspiring against me”) and gathered the familiar frequency with every week (“Benin owes me a favor, just say the word,” “I can be there in nine days,” “EKATERIN, ARE YOU WELL, THE FULL STOP AT THE END OF YOUR LAST LETTER STRUCK ME AS TENSE”), until she answered with one sentence that becalmed them for a month: “Darling, if you send one more encrypted offer of an evacuation plan, I will plant in your garden a full-length statue of Admiral Naismith, commissioned from a ghem sculptor.”

And on the forty-third day, Ekaterin discovered that another author was working in her garden.

IV. The Improvements

She discovered it the way she discovered everything in plants: with eyes that for twenty-five years had watched how life grows when no one corrects it.

A leaf of iron fern — perfectly symmetrical. A small thing; iron fern does not make perfect leaves, it makes enduring leaves, and endurance is asymmetrical by nature. Then the crater knapweed in the east bed bloomed — four weeks ahead of its season, in a color half a tone richer than possible. Then the snag-bush at the entrance stopped shedding its autumn bark and gleamed, smooth as a parlor plant. One by one, quietly, carefully: someone was editing her wild species. Not to kill them. To improve them.

Ekaterin sat over the seedlings half the night, until the scheme assembled itself in all its elegance, and when it assembled, her hands went cold. Nobody wanted to ruin her garden. They wanted to perfect it — secretly, gradually, irreversibly — so that on opening day, before the entire court, before the genetic analyzers the haut aesthetes carried the way Barrayarans carry knives, the “garden of unedited life” would prove edited to the last leaf. The author: a fraud or an ignoramus, at the audience’s choice. The concept: a farce. The insult to haut art: avenged — with haut art. No one would even raise a voice. Here they did not kill gardens. Here they made them perfect, which for hers was the same thing.

She told no one — not yet. She told the beds. For three days she walked the plot on her knees in the soil, leaf by leaf, stem by stem, mapping the tampering with the precision of a forensic botanist: eighteen interventions, all recent, all executed in a hand that grew more recognizable the longer she looked — confident, exquisite, incapable of restraint. On the fourth day she laid the trap: she announced to ghem-Lady Soren (and therefore, she calculated, to the entire court by evening) that the west bed of mountain lichens was “the heart of the composition, the most fragile and the most important,” and left it demonstratively unwatched.

On the sixth night a garden drone with dimmed lights settled over the west bed and set to work. On the seventh morning Ekaterin examined the lichens under the field microscope, found what she expected — and one thing more, which she did not expect, and at which she slowly, broadly smiled.

A signature. In the edited genome, woven into the non-coding regions, stood the artist’s personal marker — the vanity no ghem aesthete could overcome, because to them art without a signature was theft from oneself. Three stylized ideographs. Ekaterin copied them out carefully and went to find a man of whom her husband said he was the only person on this planet to whom he would trust his back.

V. The Signature

Ghem-General Dag Benin, chief of Imperial internal security, received Lady Vorkosigan in a windowless office, heard her out without interrupting, examined the genetic analysis, the copied signature, and her three weeks’ cartography of the interventions — and at the end did something she later described to Miles as “the most Cetagandan thing I have ever seen”: he bowed to the documentation.

“Eighteen interventions, documented with dates, samples, and chain of custody. Lady Vorkosigan, half my investigators do not work this cleanly.” He squared the sheets. “The signature belongs to ghem-Lord Saren Ketu, curator of the living collections. Which you knew, I suspect, before you came.”

“I suspected whose the style was. The signature confirmed it.” Ekaterin was silent a moment. “He is good, General. The work is loathsome in intent and superb in execution. Had he finished, no analysis in the universe would have proven the edits were not mine.”

“Yes. Ketu is perhaps the finest living editor of plant genomes outside the Star Crèche — and for eight years he has curated a collection to which he is not permitted to add a single stroke of his own, because the Celestial Garden is complete and he was born too late. Then a Barrayaran arrives, and they give her eighteen decares and full freedom.” Benin set down the documents. “The motive is clear, the evidence sufficient. The question, Lady Vorkosigan, is procedural, and by the court’s protocol it is put to the injured party: how do you wish his career to die? Quietly, before the opening — an arrest, a scandal behind curtains, your garden opening with a shadow over it. Or loudly — a charge before the court, a trial in which your garden becomes material evidence and is sealed for years. Both roads are open to you.”

“And the third?” asked Ekaterin.

“I was not aware there was a third.”

“There is.” She rose. “Touch nothing until the opening, General. Only come. And stand somewhere from which ghem-Lord Ketu can see you well.”

That evening — at dawn the next day — the white haut-bubble floated up to the plot as always, and for the first time in fifty days it spoke. The voice, filtered through the field, was female, elderly, and amused.

“Do you know,” said the bubble, “that in fifty mornings you are the first person here to bow to me as to a guest, and not as to an institution? Haut Pel Navarr. Yes, that Pel. Do not ask how a Consort slips out at dawn; the answer is unworthy of my rank.” The bubble drew nearer. “I have watched your survivors grow, Lady Vorkosigan. And I have watched what is being done to them. I know that you know — it shows in your traps. Therefore one question, before tomorrow’s theater, from a woman who has spent her life in the most edited building in the universe: your garden asserts that correction is a form of fear. Tell me — do you believe it to the end? For people too?”

Ekaterin thought — truly, at length, as the question deserved.

“Not to the end,” she said finally. “My son is alive because geneticists corrected the thing that was killing him: a disease, not a character. The difference, haut Pel, is not between correcting and not correcting. The difference is between correcting from love and correcting from fear. Love asks what hurts. Fear asks what sticks out.” She wiped the soil from her hands. “Tomorrow, in my garden, that is exactly what will be spoken of. Come, if protocol permits.”

“Protocol,” said haut Pel Navarr, “will survive one exception. It has survived me.”

VI. The Shears

The opening of the garden called The Survivors gathered in the southeast quadrant more of the court than any opening had gathered in a generation: curiosity, malice, and a presentiment of theater, mixed in a proportion Ekaterin — receiving her guests in a plain Barrayaran dress with garden shears at her belt — found to be an excellent working climate.

The garden lay before them — unfinished by haut standards and alive by hers: snag-bushes rooted into imported crater stones; iron fern under coral oaks; a meadow of crater knapweed, uneven as a real meadow; and at the center, on a low hill, an old skellytum with a crooked trunk and a plaque that read only: “Shattered 2977. Rooted anew.”

“Your excellencies,” Ekaterin began without amplification; she had learned from her mother-in-law that a quiet voice makes the hall lean in. “This garden asserts something simple: that life no one has corrected deserves to be looked at with respect, not with condescension. I know that here this is a disputed thesis. I know it,” she made a pause of a precision Miles would have been proud of, “because for the last seven weeks someone has been disputing it on site. Eighteen times.”

The silence dropped like a curtain. Ekaterin set off down the path, unhurried, and the court flowed after her.

“Here.” She stopped at the iron fern and lifted the perfectly symmetrical leaf. “Corrected. Here — the knapweed, bloomed before its season in an impossible color. Corrected. Here — the lichens of the west bed. Corrected last week, at night, by drone, with a mastery to which I take off my hat, and with a signature in the genome, because a true artist cannot do without a signature.” She looked at no one in particular. It was not necessary: ghem-General Benin stood in the third row, exactly where he ought, looking exactly at whom he ought, and the face of ghem-Lord Saren Ketu beneath its impeccable paint was slowly acquiring the color of soil before sterilization. “I will not name the author. In this garden, editors are not named — that is its entire idea. I will do something else.”

She drew Grandmother Vorvayne’s shears — heavy, forged, polished by four generations of palms — and before the eyes of the flower of the Cetagandan court, methodically, with clean professional movements, she cut out the corrected: the perfect leaf, the impossible bloom, the edited lichens, eighteen interventions, stem by stem, in a total silence broken only by a sound haut aesthetics had never once heard in its history — the sound of Barrayaran shears cutting improvements. Then she knelt, opened a canvas bag, and in every bared place planted new: living, wild, uneven, from the seed bank of the survivors.

“The garden restores itself,” she said, rising and brushing the soil from her knees with a gesture that apologized for nothing. “That is what it knows how to do. That is why it is here. I declare it open.”

In the silence that followed occurred the thing Eta Ceta speaks of to this day: the white bubble in the first row went out. The field contracted and vanished, and haut Pel Navarr — with an open face on which twelve centuries of genetics had written beauty, and eighty years of life, which is rarer, an expression — stepped onto the uneven meadow of The Survivors with bare feet, laughed aloud at the touch of it, and set off down the path: the first haut in living memory to enter a garden with no wall between herself and the growing.

After her, because precedent is the strongest force in any court, went everyone.

VII. The Cutting

The reckoning settled itself in the Cetagandan manner: noiselessly and finally. Ghem-Lord Saren Ketu resigned “for reasons of health” three days after the opening and accepted an appointment as terraforming inspector on Rho Ceta — a post whose irony the court chewed for a whole season: to the end of his career, the finest editor of his generation would be checking how unimproved lichen grows on other people’s rocks. Benin sent Ekaterin an official commendation and an unofficial note: “Your husband once resolved a case here by talking for fourteen hours. You resolved yours with eighteen cuts and two sentences. Tell him the court votes for your method.”

The garden called The Survivors was declared a permanent exhibit of the Celestial Garden — the first whose charter expressly forbade any genetic intervention — and the Star Crèche addressed to Lady Vorkosigan a request she recognized as the true price of the whole adventure: a complete collection of seed from unedited Barrayaran species, for the haut archive. The wild, entering the most ordered gene bank of humanity, through the front door, with an inventory.

Ekaterin agreed — for a fee she formulated in writing, and at which the embassy went silent: not money, not a title, but the return of the herbarium of Vorbarr Sultana University — fourteen thousand specimens of Barrayaran flora, carried off during the occupation and lying for a century in the Imperial repositories. The herbarium arrived on Barrayar two months after her, in forty-one sealed containers, and when the rector, at a loss for words, asked how she had ever obtained such a thing, Ekaterin answered with the sentence that afterward hung framed in the botany faculty:

“I asked for it in gardeners’ language, Rector. I told them the survivors were coming home. On Cetaganda they understand composition — no one dared spoil the finale.”

At home autumn waited for her, and her garden — hers, with the plaque with her name — and Miles at the gate, who embraced her silently and long, and then surveyed her luggage and observed that from eighteen decares in the Celestial Garden his wife was returning with one travel bag and one pair of shears.

“The garden stayed there,” said Ekaterin. “That is how it is with gardens, love: they are the only art you cannot take with you. You can only leave it to grow among strangers and hope they will look after it.” She hung the shears on their hook in the shed, where they had hung between the trowels for twenty years. “And the skellytum put out a cutting. I left it there, in their soil. Let the Celestial Garden have one thing no one will ever correct.”

In the evening, in the study, Miles found a gift on his desk: a small pot with a still smaller commemoration — a cutting of the cutting, third generation from the shattered one — and a note in the hand he knew better than his own:

To the most corrected man I know — from his most incorrigible wife. It grows slowly. It endures everything. It is ours. — E.

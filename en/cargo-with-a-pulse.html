Cargo with a Pulse

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Gray Ship

The dismantling of the barge at the quaddies’ wormhole took three weeks and passed under the dullest cover imaginable: “scheduled replacement of a navigation buoy.” Professor Emerita Judith Riva signed the finding personally — the throat, soothed for eight months by the quiet song of Item O-1, had “hardened,” as she put it in the intonation of a physician discharging a recovered patient: the young passage had come through its crisis and now rang clean on its own. The machine, three times aimed at wormholes and once taught to heal, was broken down into four sections, packed into industrial containers labeled “capacitor module, industrial,” and loaded — by all the rules of a craft ImpSec had mastered to perfection — onto the least remarkable ship that could be found.

The Marisa K. was a thirty-year-old medium hauler with a battered hull, an honest manifest, and a crew of eleven, plus two “escort technicians” whose military bearing had been carefully dismantled by six weeks of trained sloth. The logic was iron, and old as the trade: a convoy of warships screams “treasure” across three sectors; a rusty tramp says nothing to anyone. Grayness is the cheapest armor.

Grayness has only one flaw: it does not work on people who rob everything.

They were hit on the fourth jump, in the dead stretch between two secondary nodes — three fast hulls rising out of the shadow of an ice field with the practiced geometry of a pack that had been doing the same thing in the same places for two years. The Breakers: a name known to every insurance company in the sector and to not one court. They did not choose their victims and took no interest in manifests. Board, clear, strip everything with market value — containers, fuel, spare parts, medicines — and vanish before the patrols could drag their mass to the scene.

The crew of the Marisa K. surrendered per instructions: against thirty armed boarders, eleven civilians and two covert bodyguards had no move, and ImpSec’s instruction for such a case was unambiguous — the cargo is not worth anyone’s life. The pirates stripped the ship in four hours, methodically, with cargo exoskeletons. The four “capacitor modules” went into their flagship’s hold along with everything else.

And then, instead of leaving the crew on the drifting hull as they usually did, the pirates did something they had been doing more and more often for half a year, because the market had opened: they herded off the people as well.

“Cargo with a pulse,” said their bosun, locking the thirteen into an empty container with an air recycler. “Don’t crowd. Whoever breathes evenly travels comfortably.”

II. The Contract

The message caught the Tuning Fork on contract in the Polian League, through the four blind relays, under a classification Quinn had not seen in years: not “secret,” not “personal,” but that third thing which, between her and Miles Vorkosigan, meant drop everything.

Quinn. They’ve stolen my machine. That machine. The transport was hit nine days ago in sector Far-4; the handwriting is the Breakers’ — see the attachment, two years of exploits, zero captured. The pirates don’t know what they’re carrying: to them it’s an industrial capacitor. But if anyone with a laboratory and an imagination opens those containers… I won’t explain it to you. Officially the Imperium cannot move — any fleet operation in that region announces to the whole galaxy that there was something in the rusty tramp worth raising a fleet for. So I am raising you. The contract runs through three foundations, the fee is shameless, and the conditions are two: recover or destroy the machine, and — Quinn, this one is from me, not from the Imperium — there were thirteen people aboard. The crew was not found with the ship. Bring them back too. In the long list of things I cannot do from here, the most unbearable is that I cannot count the people as item two. You can not-do it for me. — M.

“Us again,” said Captain Hereld, finishing the read. “And with a passenger again, I’ll wager.”

The passenger came aboard the next day off an Escobaran liner, with one travel bag and a folder of classification stamps: Lieutenant Colonel Elin Venek, ImpSec, galactic operations, wearing the expression of someone no longer impressed by coincidences.

“Don’t ask,” she said instead of a greeting. “Galeni pulled me out of my leave. I bring two things, Admiral: the full signature of the containers — and the ‘Grief’ codes. The item’s anti-tamper system. If the machine cannot be recovered, the codes, delivered at close range by tight-beam, slag the core in eleven seconds. Clean, no blast, no trace of what it was.”

“The range?”

“Two hundred kilometers, Admiral. Whoever sends the code has to be close. Usually unpleasantly close.”

“Of course it’s two hundred kilometers,” said Quinn. “It was designed by an engineer, not by the person who’d have to cross them. Volan! Course for Far-4. We’re going to read some handwriting.”

III. Klin Station

They found the Marisa K. where the pack had left her — gutted, cold, drifting with torn cargo cradles and a ship’s log cut off in mid-sentence. There were no people aboard — neither living nor dead, which Quinn noted aloud as the only good news in the entire sector: dead cargo doesn’t travel.

The trail led, as all trails in that arm led, to Klin Station — a free port at the crossing of three secondary nodes, small, loud, and organized on the single principle that keeps such places standing: everything is sold, nothing is remembered. The Dendarii went down onto it as a crew on leave, and in two days did what Quinn called “archaeology of a fresh dig”: they bought drinks, lost at cards, and listened.

The station remembered more than it admitted. The Breakers had passed through eleven days earlier, had sold off the bulk haul — fuel, parts; the Marisa K.’s medicines surfaced in three station pharmacies — and had left suspiciously fast, without the customary bender. Why, a broker with a flexible conscience explained for a decent sum: because this time there had been something special in the haul.

“Four industrial containers with a Barrayaran customs signature that Vesh refused to sell here,” said the broker. “Rago Vesh, their ataman. They call him the Raven — wears a mechanical bird on his shoulder, an old ship’s talisman. He’s no fool, Vesh. He smelled that if Barrayar hauls something that gray, it’s black inside. He put a word out on the channels: ‘Imperial classified equipment, bidding on delivery.’ And a big fish bit, Admiral — a procurement clerk from the court of the governor-general of Sigma Ceta. A ghem-captain, name of Hast. He’s buying blind — doesn’t know what it is, but ‘Barrayaran and classified’ is enough for him: for a catch like that, the Celestial Garden hands out feathers. Vesh is running the four containers to the border node at Dran. Handover in…” the broker glanced at his chrono, “…sixty-odd hours.”

“And the people?” asked Quinn, very evenly. “Thirteen from the Barrayaran transport.”

“Not thirteen. Thirty-four.” The broker shrugged under her stare and hastened to add: “The pack’s been collecting for half a year — crews, miners from a broken settlement, two doctors. Vesh keeps them in a habitation container on the flagship. He was going to sell them here, but once the ghem deal came up, he decided not to get distracted. He calls them ‘the train.’ Cargo with a pulse, as the saying goes — it travels on its own and doesn’t wear out the packaging.”

Quinn left the second sum on the table, for the route and everything else, and walked out of the bar with a face that made two strangers step aside.

“Sixty hours; Dran is fifty-five for us by the straight route,” Volan reported on the bridge. “We beat them there, Admiral. Narrowly, but we beat them.”

“Then we lie in wait,” said Quinn. “At the exit point off Dran, before their last jump to the border. Venek — you’ll have your two hundred kilometers. Hereld — boarding parties on standby: aboard that flagship are thirty-four people who are party to nobody’s deal. The order of priorities for the entire fleet — log it: first the train, then the machine, then Vesh. Anyone who scrambles that answers to me.”

IV. The Choice

The Breakers rose out of the passage at Dran forty minutes behind schedule and in a formation that betrayed an experienced animal: the three hulls emerged dispersed, the flagship Raven deep, behind the two escorts. Vesh had lived two years at large not because he was lucky, but because he always came out of a jump expecting an ambush.

Expecting — but not the Tuning Fork.

The Dendarii struck by Quinn’s textbook: the first three minutes belonged to them. One escort lost its drives in the opening exchange — surgically, through the stern, no breach of habitation sections; the second, trying to wedge in, met Volan’s asynchronous maneuver and a point-blank salvo, after which its crew sensibly declared neutrality. The Raven, bigger and better armored, swung for the border, squeezing everything out of her drives — and then Rago Vesh showed why no one had caught him in two years.

From the flagship’s stern cradles a container separated. A habitation module, standard, with an autonomous recycler — and thirty-four heat signatures inside. It separated not into orbit but on a parting burn computed with vicious precision: down and outward, toward the radiation belts of the gas giant around which Dran node gravitated. Free drift, forty minutes to the outer belt. The recycler would hold. The people inside would not.

“Message from the Raven, Admiral,” said the comm officer, white-faced. “Voice. Playing it.”

“Mercenary.” Vesh’s voice was calm, businesslike, almost courteous. “You see the arithmetic, don’t you? The train is falling toward the belt. I’m carrying the capacitors to Dran. You’re fast, but you’re not two. Choose: the cargo with a pulse or the cargo with an inventory. And for what it’s worth — I’d take the inventory. A pulse replaces itself cheap.”

On the bridge of the Tuning Fork it became perfectly quiet. All the numbers hung on the tactical table and everyone could see them: intercepting the Raven before the passage — possible, at full burn, immediately. Intercepting the falling container — possible, at full burn, immediately. Both at once — impossible. The fleet’s escorts were too far, tied up with the two pirate hulls; no one else would reach the container before the belt.

“For the record,” said Captain Hereld, performing her duty in a level voice: “interception of the mission objective is achievable only by abandoning the rescue operation. The commander’s decision?”

Elli Quinn looked at the board one more second — not because she hesitated, but because she wanted the numbers to remember her.

“The decision was made thirty-five years ago,” she said. “Volan, course for the container, full burn. Boarding parties — to the locks, configuration for hard dock with a tumbling object. Venek…” she turned to the lieutenant colonel, “…your two hundred kilometers are off. I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be,” said Elin Venek. “I have a protocol for this case too. It’s called ‘we’ll think later.’”

The interception of the container entered the Dendarii curricula under the dry name “the Dran maneuver,” and the eyewitness accounts under other words. The module was tumbling about two axes, already licked by the outer tongues of the belt; Volan matched the Tuning Fork’s rotation to its own — one hundred and sixty meters of ship waltzing with a falling container — and held the hard dock for fourteen minutes in mounting radiation while the boarding parties cut, opened, and carried. Thirty-four, to the last soul: the eleven of the Marisa K. with their two bodyguards, sixteen miners, two doctors, and three children whom the broker on Klin had not mentioned, because children spoiled the price of the story. The last Dendarii came back through the lock ninety seconds before the point at which the ship’s surgeon declined to guarantee anything at all.

When the Tuning Fork tore free of the gravity well and lay back on course, the Raven was a dot at the edge of range, sinking into the passage toward Dran and the border.

“We’re losing him,” said Volan. Not as a question.

“No,” said Quinn. She was watching the screen, and there was nothing in her voice the crew had not heard before her great insanities. “We’re losing his appointment. Which, ladies and gentlemen, is an entirely different operation. Vesh gave me a choice between two cargoes and I chose. Now I am going to show him why the second choice was never cargo at all.”

V. The Border

The plan Quinn laid out to the war council, while the Tuning Fork devoured the distance to Dran, rested on three things: on one delay, on one vanity, and on two hundred kilometers.

“First: Vesh is now late for his own deal — because of us. Ghem-Captain Hast is waiting off Dran, on the Cetagandan side, with an escort and the bidding in his pocket. The buyer is nervous: he’s buying something ‘Barrayaran and classified’ blind, and every minute of delay smells like a trap to him. Second: Vesh is vain. The raven on the shoulder, the bidding, the message to me — he loves the stage. Such a man does not hand over goods by shuttle at a neutral point. Such a man will want to deliver in person, deck to deck, with a speech. And third…” Quinn nodded to Venek, “…we need only two hundred kilometers and eleven seconds. We do not need to get the machine back, ladies and gentlemen. We need Vesh to have nothing to sell, and Hast nothing to buy. We are walking into their wedding as the relative nobody invited but everyone is obliged to admit.”

“We are walking into Cetagandan border space,” Hereld specified with professional gloom.

“We are walking into a disputed border zone with unsettled navigation rights,” Venek corrected her, producing from her folder a chart with so many stamps it nearly glowed. “ImpSec’s lawyers know how to read hundred-year-old treaties. The Dran-South corridor is formally under joint control, exercised by no one for three decades. Twenty minutes inside — and no one will manage to decide exactly whose border we are violating before we’ve left.”

The handover took place twelve hours later, in the dead zone of the Dran-South corridor: the Raven, battered and hurried, and the Cetagandan border cruiser Primrose at Dawn — elegant, armed, condescending — docked side by side while cargo exoskeletons carried four industrial containers from one deck to the other, and ghem-Captain Soren Hast, in the formal face paint of procurement, inspected the purchase for which he expected to receive feathers in the Celestial Garden.

The Tuning Fork rose out of the corridor’s shadow at one hundred and eighty kilometers — not with an attack, but with an open frequency and a voice that two decks heard simultaneously:

“This is Admiral Quinn, Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet, under contract for the recovery of stolen property. Ghem-Captain Hast — I don’t know you, but you have my sympathy: I was going to tell you that you are buying stolen goods, but I see you have already paid. Rago Vesh — you I know. Twelve hours ago you explained to me that a pulse replaces itself cheap and an inventory dear. I have come to show you the bill.”

“Venek,” she said, cutting the transmitter. “Now.”

The tight-beam crossed one hundred and eighty kilometers in a fraction of a second. The Grief codes — delivered at close range, accepted by four anti-tamper nodes, confirmed — did their work in eleven seconds, quietly, without blast, without light: deep inside the four industrial containers on the deck of the Primrose at Dawn, the core of Item O-1 — the machine that cracked bells and healed wormholes — melted into eleven tons of shapeless, dead, absolutely unreadable slag.

Ghem-Captain Hast opened the first container seven minutes later, before the eyes of his own crew, of the pirate ataman, and of the one open frequency nobody had thought to close. The silence that followed transmitted beautifully.

“What…” Hast’s voice lost its condescension on the first syllable, “…what is this?”

“That,” said Quinn courteously, “is former Imperial classified equipment. I stress ‘former.’ And now the arithmetic, gentlemen: Ghem-Captain, you have paid a winning bid for eleven tons of slag, and the explanation before the governor-general’s court is entirely yours. Vesh — you have just sold a Cetagandan official deliberately devalued goods represented as sound. On Sigma Ceta that is called defrauding the crown. I am leaving now. I leave you to finish clarifying your deal. I suspect one of you has a brig.”

VI. The Getaway

Leaving proved harder than arriving, because ghem-Captain Soren Hast — having lost, on one frequency, the feathers, the bid, and his face — decided to save at least one of the three by detaining all witnesses; and the Primrose at Dawn, abandoning its dock with the Raven, lay onto an intercept course with weapons ports open, while from the border station at Dran two more hulls rose on alert.

“Three against one, the corridor is closing, eleven minutes to the node at a burn we don’t have,” Hereld summarized. “Volan?”

Kera Volan was studying the tactical table with her head tilted — a gesture the entire bridge knew, and at which some people wanted to tighten their straps and others to pray.

“The queue at the node,” she said. “Dran is a trade node, Admiral: eleven civilian ships hang before the passage, lined up to jump on schedule. Hast won’t fire through the queue — half of them are Cetagandan traders, and the other half are insured with people even the Celestial Garden doesn’t quarrel with. The queue is our wall. We only have to reach it before the intercept… and jump the line.”

“Eleven ships will object.”

“They won’t have time,” said Volan. “Because I’m going through out of turn. The passage admits one at a time, at ninety-second intervals — that’s how long the throat needs to settle after each transit. But our fan doesn’t wait for the throat to settle. We can enter on another ship’s wave.” She turned to Quinn, and for the first time since Keros there was that gleam in her eyes. “I jump in the lead ship’s wake, Admiral. Inside his ninety seconds. Nobody has ever done it with a full hull, because nobody ever needed to and nobody has our machine. The rating plate forbids it explicitly.”

“And the rating plate—”

“—lies,” said Volan, Hereld, and half the bridge in unison, and Quinn understood that her fleet had long been ready.

The getaway at Dran lasted nine minutes and is told to this day in three languages, less accurately every time. The Tuning Fork cut into the trade queue along its long axis — inside the corridor, where no interceptor dared fire — overtook eleven outraged civilian ships at distances from which their lifeboat names were legible, drew level with the fat Polian grain hauler at the head of the queue exactly as it sank into the passage… and jumped after it, in its wake, inside its ninety seconds, through a throat still ringing from another ship’s transit. The double fan sang one long, high, impossible note through the whole hull. Then — stars on the other side, a placid queue of arrivals, and one Polian captain who told to the end of his days how a mercenary cruiser had risen out of his own wake, blinked its running lights at him, and vanished.

The interceptors of Sigma Ceta remained before the passage, before eleven witnesses, and before ninety seconds that none of them knew how to jump.

VII. The Bill

The settling took a month and was conducted in that quiet register in which one settles things that officially never happened.

Rago Vesh, the ataman with the mechanical raven, never emerged from the Cetagandan brig into the free universe: Sigma Ceta tried him for defrauding the crown with provincial ruthlessness, and his two surviving crews, picked up by the Dendarii at Dran, went down the pipelines of four jurisdictions, each of which had been waiting for the Breakers for two years. Sector Far-4 was pirate-free for the first time in two years — a circumstance the insurance companies marked with a reduction in rates and not one word of thanks, which Quinn found perfectly normal.

The thirty-four went home at the contract’s expense: the crew of the Marisa K. to Barrayar, the miners to their rebuilt settlement, the two doctors back to their clinics, and the three children, who turned out to be orphans of the broken settlement, left with the mining families who, in the container, according to the boarding party, “had already quarreled over who gets them.” Quinn sent Miles the list of names before any other document, because she knew which he would read first.

His answer came late — the Auditor was busy explaining to an Imperial commission why eleven tons of slag was an excellent outcome — and it was longer than usual:

Quinn. The commission has accepted the report: the machine neutralized, the secret kept, Cetaganda in possession of a heap of vitrified mass and a provincial scandal which Benin, I suspect, reads aloud for pleasure. Riva is already at the drafting board — she says O-2 will be “better, lighter, and this time with my corrections, not a committee’s.” The Imperium will pay. Empires know how to pay for iron.

And now the real bill, for your archive. The machine: eight years of development, eleven gigawatts, reproducible — demonstrably; we are already reproducing it. Thirty-four people: irreproducible. Not one of them, on any budget, ever. You made the choice in a second, I know — but I want it recorded somewhere outside our heads, because times are coming when such calculations will be made more and more often and from higher and higher up: iron is counted in marks, people are counted in people, and the currencies do not convert. Anyone who claims otherwise is selling something.

P.S. The Polian grain hauler has filed a complaint for “hazardous maneuvering in wake.” I paid the fine personally, with pleasure, and requested a framed copy.

Quinn read the letter twice, smiled at the postscript, and carried the list of thirty-four names to her cabin, where she put it in the drawer with the other such lists — thirty-five years of such lists, some long, some short, all in one currency. Then she went up to the bridge, where Kera Volan was just taking the watch and where the next contract was already waiting on the tactical table.

“Where to, Admiral?” asked Volan.

Quinn looked at the star chart — the nodes, the corridors, the queues of patient ships before patient doors, the whole vast, imperfect, people-teeming blueprint.

“Wherever the pulse costs more than the inventory,” she said. “As always. Full ahead.”

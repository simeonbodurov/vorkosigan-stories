Foundations

An adventure on Sergyar — featuring the Vicereine, two brothers, and a city of concrete

I. The Road to School

Vicereine Cordelia Vorkosigan had postponed her retirement for so many years that on Sergyar it had become a calendar joke: “when the Vicereine retires” meant approximately “never, but with good intentions.” This morning, riding through the new boulevards of Gridgrad with two small boys bouncing with excitement, she told herself once again that next year — truly, finally, honest Betan — she would hand over the seal and become simply a grandmother, a mother, and a scandalously old woman with opinions.

“Aunt Cordelia, is it true the new school has a skeleton of a vampire balloon?” Kai, aged five, hung on his safety belt like a question mark.

“A stuffed one, sweetlings. Dead vampire balloons don’t fly.”

“And will they show us live ones?”

“Live ones we watch from a distance, like politics,” said Cordelia. “Sander, stop programming the window.”

Sander, aged seven, with his father’s stubborn lower jaw — with both his fathers’, Cordelia thought, with the usual quiet, complicated tenderness — raised an innocent gaze from the control panel, which he had already persuaded to glow purple. Oliver’s boys. Aral’s sons, born nine years after his death, of blood and love preserved against all the rules of time. She was taking them to their first day at the new “Zora” school because Oliver was in orbit for the quarterly inspection, because their sisters had left with the first car, and because no security procedure on Sergyar was stronger than one “please, Aunt Cordelia” in two voices.

The convoy turned onto Sunrise Hill Street, half a kilometer from the school. Later the inquiry would establish everything with cold precision: the kitchen container on the construction site to the left; the shaped charge inside it, built from a mining detonator and three hundred grams of industrial explosive; the sensor counting the convoy’s cars in order to hit the second one.

In the moment there was only white glare, a fist of air, and a sound Cordelia had not heard since the Escobar war and which her body recognized before her mind did.

The car spun, struck the barrier, and lay down on its side in a cloud of dust and shrieking alarms. Cordelia hung in her belt, with blood in one eye — her own? — and with two thoughts, absolutely clear, ranked in priority by forty years of command experience: first — the children; second — whoever did this will regret being born into this universe.

“Sander. Kai.” Her voice was as calm as a therapy recording. “Look at me.”

Kai was crying — loudly, healthily, at full lung, clutching his twisted left arm. A crying child is a living child. Sander was not crying. Sander was looking at his own scorched sleeve with a white, concentrated face, and said in a thin, astonished voice:

“Aunt Cordelia, it doesn’t hurt. Why doesn’t it hurt?”

“Because your body is clever and it will hurt later, when the doctors are with you,” said Cordelia, having already cut her own belt, already between the two boys, covering them with her body, while outside the guards shouted into their comms and ImpSec’s jammers murdered every frequency within a kilometer. “And now we are going to count to a hundred the Betan way. In Betan, one hundred is counted: one circle, two circles… look at me, Sander, look at me…”

They had counted sixty-four circles when the medics got them out. Nobody died on Sunrise Hill Street. That, Cordelia would say later, was the perpetrator’s only miscalculation — and the last one she intended to allow him.

II. The Brothers

Miles Vorkosigan covered the distance from Vorbarr Sultana to Sergyar in five days and eleven hours, which the courier’s pilot described as “at the edge of engineering tolerances,” and Miles as “unbearably slow.” Gregor sent him officially, with the Auditor’s chain and with an instruction dictated in person: “It’s only proper to tell you that you are far too interested for this investigation. I have no disinterested Auditor I would care to send. So I’m sending the most interested one. Use it.”

Mark arrived from Escobar eighteen hours after him. No one had sent him; no one had dared to stop him, either.

They met at Kareen Hospital — in the children’s wing, before the window of the regeneration ward, where Sander slept in the embrace of the apparatus restoring the skin of his arm, and in the next bed Kai, his elbow in a uni-cast, was dictating to his father a wish list beginning with “the vampire balloon skeleton, but all of it.”

Oliver Jole, admiral retired, had come down from orbit in six hours aboard a shuttle whose trajectory had probably also been at the edge of tolerances. He stood by the beds with the straight back of a man who will not permit himself to collapse while there is a watch to stand, and when he saw the brothers he only nodded — once, deeply, as to a formation.

“The prognosis is good,” he said in a level voice. “Kai comes out tomorrow. Sander — in a week, without scars. Children recover…” the voice wavered by a micron for the first time, “…children recover quickly.”

“And Mother?” asked Miles.

“Refused the bed, accepted the bandage, and is at this moment holding a session of the crisis staff.” Oliver paused. “Second hour. Commodore Rayne of ImpSec Sergyar emerged from the first hour visibly older.”

Cordelia received them in her office at the residence that evening — with a suture above her eyebrow, a cup of tea, and that particular calm which both her sons recognized, and which caused experienced people to leave the room. At eighty-five she sat in her chair upright as a question no one had yet answered.

“Boys.” Her gaze traveled over them — the small one and the broad one, the Auditor and the industrialist. “I’ll spare you the preamble. Someone blew up a car with two children in it in order to kill one old woman. ImpSec is doing what it can, and what it can will prove insufficient by tomorrow, because this case has a false bottom — I feel it with my skin, and my skin is Betan and does not err. So I tell you as a mother and order you as Vicereine: work together. You read people and spaces.” She nodded to Miles. “You read money and appetites.” She nodded to Mark. “Whoever did this has left tracks in both worlds. Find him.” She sipped her tea. “And, boys… find him before I do. For his own good.”

III. The Bar

The false bottom showed itself on the fifth day.

ImpSec Sergyar had worked fast and clean: the detonator — traced to a lot stolen a year earlier from a mining depot at the New Ridges; the machining of the shaped cone — to a small workshop in the Gridgrad docklands, closed for two months; the workshop’s lessee — a phantom with a false identity, but with one appearance in the light: a witness had seen him twice in the company of the regulars of the bar “The Cold Joint” — an old workingmen’s establishment on the riverbank, dug in between the cement silos, where the old guard of the Gridgrad construction sites had drunk for fifteen years.

The lead was taken up by Lieutenant Ari Mazur — twenty-nine, local, one of those Sergyaran boys for whom ImpSec was not a career but a citizenship. Mazur walked into The Cold Joint at nineteen ten on a Wednesday, in civilian clothes, under the legend of an insurance assessor.

No one ever saw him again.

“The cameras,” Commodore Rayne reported to the brothers, with the gray face of a commander losing people in peacetime, “cover the entrance and the quay. Mazur goes in. He does not come out. There are forty-one people in the bar that evening; we questioned every one: nobody remembers anything, which is statistically impossible and legally unprovable. At zero twenty the establishment closes, empty. Mazur is gone. His comm — dead since twenty-one oh-seven. And in the morning…” Rayne’s jaw tightened. “In the morning his case files are wiped. With his own codes. And eighteen thousand marks land in his personal account through a Jacksonian channel.”

“And your working theory is a traitor,” said Miles.

“My staff’s working theory, my lord. Not mine. I trained Mazur. But the facts—”

“The facts are arranged,” said Mark from the corner, where he sat with a tablet and the expression with which he read other people’s balance sheets. “Too well arranged. Eighteen thousand marks. Do you know what eighteen thousand is, Commodore? Enough to believe, and not enough to doubt. A sum chosen by a man who knows what bribes look like in the ledgers. That transfer is not a payment. That transfer is an invoice for your trust — and somebody drew it up professionally.”

Miles spent the next morning in The Cold Joint — alone, if one didn’t count ImpSec’s shadow outside — leaning on the bar over a glass of local beer he wasn’t drinking, reading the room the way other people read a deposition. The back door to the quay: a camera, yes, but at an angle leaving three meters of dead zone to the pontoon. The pontoon: a polished railing, traces of the regular mooring of a small craft the establishment officially did not have. The barman: the heavy hands of a former rebar man, and eyes that returned to the back door with a frequency of once a minute.

“Nice bar,” said Miles. “Old. Who built it?”

“Builders,” said the barman.

“It shows. Good concrete.” Miles ran a finger along the edge of the countertop, poured as a monolith. “They don’t pour it like this anymore. This is from the era of the big contracts, isn’t it? Before the biocement. Before — what was it called — ‘Ventrel Construct’?”

The barman’s hands did not stop wiping the glass. But their rhythm changed — by one eighth-note, just enough for Miles to hear how, in the silence of The Cold Joint, one word dropped to the bottom and everyone pretended not to have seen the ripples.

He left money on the counter, walked out into the sun, and called his brother.

“Mark. Tell me everything about Ventrel.”

On the other end came a short pause, then Mark’s voice — level, with that particular overtone Miles had heard from him only in front of old accounts:

“Gus Ventrel. Of course. I should have thought of him first — he’s mine.” A pause. “In the sense that I’m the one who ruined him.”

IV. The Man Who Poured the City

Mark’s account took one evening, one bottle of Sergyaran red, and one terrace overlooking the rising skyline of Gridgrad — a forest of cranes glowing in the dark like a constellation in scaffolding.

“When they moved the capital here, concrete was gold. Millions of cubic meters: the government quarter, the spaceport, the levees, the whole city from zero. And the king of concrete was named Gus Ventrel. Started with one mixer truck in Kareenburg, got to seventy percent of Gridgrad’s deliveries. Hard, fast, exactly as unscrupulous as the era permitted — and the era permitted a great deal: cartel tenders, ‘lost’ test protocols, the occasional competitor with a suddenly broken mixer. The city grew, Ventrel grew with it. He liked to say — this is documented, it’s in three interviews: ‘I poured this city. Gridgrad stands on me.’”

“And then came you.”

“Then came the biocement.” Mark turned his glass. “Enzyme bonding, developed by my group with Enrique’s lab — yes, that Enrique, his bugs eat radiation out in the wastelands, and one side-product enzyme of the program turned out to be the perfect binder. Thirty percent cheaper, four times faster to cure, a cost base against which classic cement has no mathematics. We entered the market seven years ago. Ventrel did what kings do: first he laughed, then he cut prices below cost, then he bought two deputies of the local council to block our certification. And then he made the mistake of his life: he tried to buy a third man. The Vicereine was just then pushing through the transparent-tenders law.”

“Oh,” said Miles. “Oh-h.”

“Precisely. Mother is not bought; Mother documents. The law passed, the tenders lit up, the cartel fell apart in two seasons. Ventrel was leveraged up to his cranes and was staying upright only on the padding — remove the padding, and a hole remains. The banks carved him up in a year and a half. The Imperium took nothing from him, Miles — do you understand? Nobody persecuted him. He simply lost, on an honest market, for the first time in his life, and it killed him more surely than any sentence. The last time I saw him was at the auction of his assets. He stood at the back and watched them sell his mixers. He watched me.”

“And he vanished.”

“From the registries — two years ago. Officially: insolvency, address unknown.” Mark set down his glass. “And unofficially, a man like Ventrel doesn’t vanish. A man like Ventrel pours himself in somewhere and waits to set.”

The mosaic assembled itself before Miles’s eyes with ugly clarity. The attack on the woman whose law had cut the cartel apart. The date: three days before Cordelia was to sign the decree for the last, largest package — the “Foundations” government quarter, fourteen blocks, contracts for thirty years ahead, allocated under the new rules. The Vicereine’s death meant caretaker administration, frozen decrees, reconsideration — and a gap through which the old players with new façades could return to the table. Vengeance and a balance sheet, poured in a single mold.

“That leaves the bar,” said Miles. “And Mazur.”

“The bar is his,” said Mark quietly. “I’d stake my company on it. The Cold Joint is the old canteen of Ventrel’s concrete crews — I checked the ownership: three shell layers, and beneath them a cooperative of his former foremen. Your lieutenant walked in to ask about the phantom from the workshop, in the one establishment on Sergyar where the phantom has forty pairs of loyal eyes.” A pause. “Miles. If Ventrel staged Mazur’s treason — the files, the money — then he needed Mazur alive. Codes are extracted from a man, not from a corpse. With fast-penta. The boy may still be alive.”

“Then,” said Miles, and for the first time since the hospital Admiral Naismith appeared in his voice, “let’s find him before Ventrel runs out of need for him.”

V. The Bait

The plan the brothers built over the next two days rested on one simple observation of Mark’s: “Ventrel is a builder. A builder believes in schedules. Give him a deadline, and he’ll hurry — and a man in a hurry comes out of cover.”

The deadline was served to him publicly, from two directions at once.

First: the Vicereine’s chancellery announced that the signing of the Foundations decree was not being postponed — on the contrary, it was being moved earlier, with a formal ceremony on the construction site of the quarter itself, “as a sign that Sergyar does not bend.” Cordelia approved the text personally and added one sentence that made Miles look at her with admiration: “The Vicereine will lay the first concrete.”

Second: MPVK Enterprises announced that, at the investigation’s request, it was beginning a quality audit of the old foundations in the government quarter — core samples from the blocks poured by Ventrel Construct in the cartel years. Mark gave an interview in which, with an expressionless face, he pronounced the phrase the two brothers had polished to surgical precision: “We will bring out of this city’s concrete everything that is hidden in it.”

“If those foundations hold only stolen percentages, he’ll swallow it,” Mark explained to the war council at the residence, where Commodore Rayne listened with the expression of a man having his stolen case returned to him. “But if there is something more in there — and a man who builds bombs today was no saint yesterday — then for him the audit is a second execution. He will act before the core drilling. That is: before the ceremony.”

“And the ceremony is a lure with the Vicereine inside it,” said Rayne grimly. “I don’t like this, my lord.”

“I like it,” said Cordelia from the head of the table, in the voice that caused experienced people to leave the room. “Carry on.”

The net unfolded quietly: the sector around the site — under three rings of control; every delivery for the ceremony — checked to the bolt; The Cold Joint — under circular surveillance, which within forty hours bore fruit. The rebar-man barman did two unusual things: he bought an IV kit and antiseptics at a pharmacy — and at three past midnight he took a thermal food box by boat from the nonexistent pontoon, up the river, to the mothballed batching plant “Ventrel-2” at the North Dike.

“IV lines and hot food,” said Miles, looking at the drone’s thermal record: the abandoned plant, dead for two years, with a single living point of warmth in the old dispatcher’s room. “They’re keeping someone alive. Rayne — Mazur is there. How long do you need for the assault?”

“An hour to plan, forty minutes to execute. But if we hit the plant before the ceremony, Ventrel will know his network is blown and will freeze the attack. We lose him — again, and this time for good.”

“Then the two operations happen simultaneously,” said Miles. “To the second. Mazur comes out of that plant the moment Ventrel walks into the trap.” He looked at his brother. “It remains to figure out exactly how he’ll walk in. Mark?”

Mark stood before the construction schedule of the ceremony and smiled — slowly, heavily, with the smile that on Jackson’s Whole had once meant someone would very soon be sorry.

“He’s a concrete man, Miles. He’s delivered all his life. He knows how you get onto a site, past every security detail in the universe, without a single question.” Mark tapped the schedule, the line that read 09:40 — delivery of load-bearing concrete, ceremonial placement. “With a mixer truck.”

VI. The Last Delivery

The morning of the ceremony was clear, windy, and crammed with formalities. At eight fifty Cordelia Vorkosigan arrived at the Foundations quarter site in an armored convoy, greeted the builders, took her place on the platform before the excavation of Block A, and began a speech written to last exactly as long as necessary. In the same minute, fourteen kilometers to the north, three ImpSec assault teams went to ground in the reeds around batching plant Ventrel-2 and waited for one word.

At nine thirty-one, checkpoint South passed through, on schedule, a heavy mixer truck bearing the logo of the subcontractor “Diga Stroy” — declared, expected, with a clean load code. The scanners searched it and found exactly what the document showed: eight cubic meters of fresh concrete.

“There he is,” said Mark in the command van, watching the telemetry. “The density. Eight cubes of concrete weigh nineteen tons. This one is carrying seventeen point three. There’s a cavity in the drum, brother. A big one. A charge in the heart of the load, screened by two meters of wet concrete — no scanner in the universe picks that up. He is going to pour his last delivery straight into the platform.”

“The driver?”

The checkpoint camera gave a close-up: a heavy face, graying stubble, hands on the wheel — hands that had poured concrete for forty years. Gus Ventrel had sent no one. For the last delivery of his life, the king of concrete had come in person.

“All teams,” said Miles into the net, very calmly. “Begin.”

North of the city the reeds stood up, and in forty-one seconds batching plant Ventrel-2 changed owners; Lieutenant Ari Mazur, dehydrated, drugged, and alive, was carried out of the dispatcher’s room on a stretcher, gripping the wrist of the first trooper with the words that later entered the ImpSec Sergyar training curriculum: “I recorded… everything… in my head. Ask me before I sleep.”

And on the site, Ventrel’s mixer passed the turnoff for the ceremonial placement and accelerated straight toward the platform — twenty-two tons of iron and explosive, eight hundred meters from the Vicereine, who at that moment, per the script, was descending from the podium toward the armored shelter labeled “photographers’ area,” muttering to her guards that she hated playing bait in her own trap.

“Don’t shoot at the cab,” Mark’s voice was sharp. “If there’s a deadman switch, his hand drops — everything drops.”

“Nobody is going to shoot,” said Miles. “We’ll receive him as a supplier. Mark — his route runs along the temporary road over the Block D excavation. Your excavation. Your material. What’s the roadway rated for?”

They understood each other in half a second, the way they had lately — without words, fraternally. Mark was already shouting into the construction dispatcher’s channel:

“This is Vorkosigan, MPVK! Section D-four of the temporary road — lift the plates! Yes, NOW, the hydraulics under them are mine, the code is—”

Eight hundred meters ahead of the hurtling mixer, forty meters of temporary road simply ceased to exist: the modular plates rotated onto their edges, and beneath them yawned the Block D excavation — six meters deep, flooded to the middle with freshly poured, still-liquid biocement, waiting since morning for its ceremonial curing. Ventrel hit the brakes with the whole of his forty-year reflex. He was one delivery too late.

The mixer skidded over the edge, buried its nose, and sank to the chassis in the gray slurry — twenty-two tons, swallowed by the material that had ruined their owner. The engine howled and choked. The drum made one last, meaningless rotation. And silence.

Then the cab door banged open, and Gus Ventrel — wet to the waist, gray from head to foot, unhurt and beside himself — came wading through the biocement toward the bank, shouting something the wind mercifully tore apart. He got to the middle. There the material of Mark Vorkosigan did what it had been engineered to do and what had won it every tender on Sergyar: it began to set. Four times faster than the classic.

When the arrest team reached him — cautiously, along laid ladders, with the deactivator that found and killed the deadman switch in the drum — the king of concrete stood in the center of the excavation, caught to the knees in his own element, upright, motionless, and no longer capable of falling.

“Gus Ventrel,” said Commodore Rayne, stepping off the last ladder, in the voice of a man repaying a debt. “You are under arrest for the attempted murder of the Vicereine of Sergyar, for an attack upon children of the Imperial household, for the abduction of an Imperial officer, and — per new data from the foundations of Block B, which we have yet to extract — probably for more. You have the right to remain silent. In your place I would exercise it standing, because we bring out the hand-saws only after the paperwork.”

Ventrel did not remain silent. Ventrel stared past Rayne’s shoulder, toward the platform, where a small red-haired old woman with a suture above her eyebrow stood upright in the wind, looking at him across eight hundred meters, and he screamed — hoarse, from the bottom of everything:

“I POURED THIS CITY! GRIDGRAD STANDS ON ME!”

Cordelia Vorkosigan took the offered microphone on the platform, waited for the echo to die, and answered — calmly, clearly, into six hundred cameras and one history textbook:

“Cities do not stand on men, Mr. Ventrel. Cities stand on foundations. The difference is that foundations do not ask the city to be grateful.” She set down the microphone, looked at the excavation, and added, without amplification now, for the nearest rows only: “And now excuse me. I have concrete to lay.”

And she laid the first concrete of the Foundations quarter — with a trowel, with a suture above her eyebrow, and with a hand that did not tremble.

VII. Curing

Retribution on Sergyar proved as systematic as a good build. The trial ran four months: the attack, the abduction, and — from the core samples of Block B — two more charges from fifteen years back, poured into the foundations along with the stolen cement, for which two families in Kareenburg finally received an answer, and a grave to visit. Ventrel heard his sentence standing — life, in the mines at the New Ridges, where, the Sergyaran press noted with unpedagogical satisfaction, “the convict will have the opportunity to become acquainted with the raw material from its other end.” The rebar-man barman and four others received theirs; The Cold Joint was sold and became the canteen of the river patrol.

Lieutenant Ari Mazur left the hospital after three weeks, delivered from his head, word for word, everything heard in the dispatcher’s room of Ventrel-2, received an Imperial commendation for endurance under fast-penta interrogation, and — at Commodore Rayne’s personal insistence — an official apology from the analysis section, read out before the assembled ranks. The eighteen thousand marks from the Jacksonian channel were confiscated and transferred, at Mark’s suggestion, to the children’s wing of Kareen Hospital.

And on one warm evening at the residence, after Kai had shown his uni-cast (autographed by two Auditors, one admiral, and the entire Seventh Shuttle Squadron) to everyone present twice, and after Sander — with new skin on his arm and a grammatically impeccable list of questions — had extracted from Mark the promise of an excursion to the biocement plant (“with samples!”), the two big brothers remained on the terrace above sleeping Gridgrad.

“Little brothers,” said Miles, gazing at the lights of the cranes. “At fifty. Father finds ways to surprise me even after his death.” He was silent a moment. “You know what I can’t get out of my head? Ventrel never understood. To the last second, to the sentence — he screamed about the concrete, the tenders, about you, about Mother. He never once mentioned the children. In his ledger they were… a rounding. An incidental line item.”

“I know.” Mark’s voice was quiet. “I’ve seen that arithmetic from the inside, Miles. I was made out of it. People like Ventrel — and like my makers — don’t hate people. They simply calculate without them. That’s why they lose: their equation is always missing a term.” He nodded downward, toward the city. “Mother put it better, on the platform. Foundations don’t ask for gratitude. But woe to the man who concludes from that, that they aren’t there.”

Below, in the Foundations quarter, the first poured block was setting in the night — slowly, invisibly, irreversibly — and somewhere in the east wing two small boys were asleep at the same hour for the first time in a month, and the house around them, full of old soldiers and new children, creaked cozily under the wind off the Ashlands.

The Vicereine of Sergyar postponed her retirement by one more year. This time, no one on the planet made the joke.

Winterfair

A closing story — one evening at Vorkosigan House

I. The House

The snow began over Vorbarr Sultana on the eve of Winterfair at noon — large, slow, ceremonial, as though it too had received an invitation — and by dusk Vorkosigan House shone through it window by window, the way it shone once a year, and this year earlier and wider than usual, because the occasion was double: the holiday — and twenty-five years since a certain wedding, celebrated in this same house, in this same snow.

Lady Ekaterin Vorkosigan walked her house at four in the afternoon, alone, by the old habit of a gardener before an opening: the great hall with the long table extended to its last leaf; the library with the fireplace; the winter garden, pruned and lit so that the snow on the glass worked for the composition; and the kitchen, where the cook was conducting operations with the authority of an admiral and drove her out with a ladle. On the staircase she was met by Senior Armsman Roic with the tablet of the security plan and a report consisting of one word: “Ready.”

“How many are we tonight, Roic?”

“Twenty-nine confirmed, my lady, with the children. Plus two on watch — myself and Brezov.” A pause, with the quarter-smile Roic permitted himself twice a year. “Brezov has checked the gifts three times. He is disappointed, my lady. He says that this year nobody is trying to poison us, and that the young generation doesn’t know what it’s missing.”

“Tell Brezov,” said Ekaterin, “that a boring evening is the summit of the trade. He knows whose quotation that is.”

The master of the house meanwhile stood in the library by the window, doing the hardest work of the day: nothing. Miles Vorkosigan at fifty-seven had mastered waiting to the degree that people with his metabolism master it — that is, badly — and he turned in his hands a glass of wine he was not drinking, watching the lights of the first car crawl up the drive through the snow.

“They’re coming,” he said to his wife when she entered. “Ekaterin. Twenty-five years. How did you manage it?”

“Patience, regular watering, and pruning out the deadwood,” said Lady Vorkosigan. “Same as with the roses, except that the roses don’t bring mercenary fleets home. Come along. Your mother arrives first, and she counts who meets her on the steps.”

II. The Gate

Cordelia Vorkosigan, ninety-two, Vicereine retired, got out of the car without anyone’s arm — with a cane she carried plainly as an accessory — surveyed the snow, the house, and the three generations drawn up on the steps, and announced, in the Betan intonation that sixty-eight years of Barrayar had not blunted by one degree:

“Right. Everyone alive, nobody under arrest, the house not on fire. By this family’s standards the evening is already a success.” She kissed her son on the top of his head, her daughter-in-law on both cheeks, inspected the assembled grandchildren, and came to a halt before the smallest. “Simone. That on your head is a beret.”

“Yes, Grandmama.”

“I know that beret,” said Cordelia, and added nothing more, but the look she exchanged with her son over the child’s head contained an entire archive.

Then the rest arrived, in waves, the way families arrive: Mark and Kareen from Escobar, laden with boxes, the largest of which bore the inscription “NOT BUGS (WORD OF HONOR)”; Lady Alys and Simon Illyan — she at ninety-one, straight as protocol; he at eighty-five, with the smile of a man for whom every good evening is pure profit; Helen Natalia with a folder under her arm even here (“one client, Mama, I’ll just leave it in the front hall, I swear”); Aral Alexander and Dana — the newlyweds of the spring, still with that shine on them; the consular family Vorpatril in full strength, straight off the jump ship — Ivan, Tej, the grown Alys and Padma — carrying the scent of another planet and a box that Tej brought in personally and surrendered to no one.

And the last car stopped in front of the gate — and did not move.

Miles went out onto the steps without a coat. Through the snow, by the open door of the car, stood a woman of sixty-three: upright, silver-haired, with the expression of a person who has stormed stations with less hesitation. Elli Quinn, honorary admiral of the rescue flotilla of Ylla, was looking at the house she had refused thirty years before with eight words spoken on another continent, in another life — and was not stepping forward.

Miles came down the steps, through the snow, in his stocking feet — this was retold afterward with variations, but the stocking feet were fact — stood beside her, and looked at the house together with her, from the outside.

“It’s big,” he conceded. “It’s smaller inside. It’s full of people who have wanted to meet you all their lives, and of one table with a place that has been kept for a quarter of a century. Nobody will want anything from you, Quinn. Nobody will expect anything. It’s only dinner.”

“That same sentence lied to me in ’fifty-seven, Vorkosigan, and that time it ended in a boarding action.” Quinn drew a breath, took a box of bottles from the seat — and stepped across. “Fine. I’m going in. But only because you owe me a case of Sergyaran red, and I collect my debts in person. And if your wife doesn’t like me, I’m blaming you.”

At the door Ekaterin was waiting — alone, without retinue, both hands held out.

“Admiral Quinn.” Her voice was a hostess’s, and something more. “For twenty-five years I have heard about you from a man who does not know how to retell people smaller than legends. Come in, and allow me to verify in person.”

“Lady Vorkosigan.” Quinn took her hands and held them. “The same, from this side, word for word. The verification is mutual.”

III. The Table

The table held twenty-nine people, four conversations at once, and one common theme that circled above it like a chandelier: the years.

They raised the toasts in the order that had settled in this house of its own accord. The first — the host, to his wife: brief, because Miles Vorkosigan had learned to say the important things briefly. (“Twenty-five years. She claims the secret is the same as with roses. I claim I am the best-watered plant in the Imperium. We are both right. To Ekaterin.”) The second — Cordelia, to the table: “I look at you and I count. Three generations, five planets by birth, seven by biography, two formerly enemy states, one former clone conspiracy, one former mercenary fleet, and at least four people who at various times have been declared dead. And all of it is eating roast at one table and passing the salt. Do you know what that is called in Betan? It is not called anything. On Beta such a thing does not exist. To Barrayar — the planet that lied to me about everything except the main thing.”

The third was raised by Simon Illyan, rising slowly, with a glass his hand held steady.

“To the absent,” he said simply. “I remember less than anyone at this table, and therefore those I remember, I remember whole. Aral Vorkosigan. Sergeant Bothari. Sergeant Taura. General Piotr, with whom I did not get along, and that was how you could tell we were both right. Madame Hysopi. All those whose chairs tonight are inside us.” He raised the glass. “Memory is not what makes you you. But it is what we owe them. By name.”

They drank standing. Then the table grew loud again — because this house could do that too: carry its dead lightly — and the conversations branched: Ivan telling Mark about the leviathans with gestures that endangered the glassware; Dana and Helen Natalia quarreling lovingly over jurisdictions; Kareen and Tej, having discovered each other on the doorstep, plotting something commercial of which the Imperium would learn after the fact; Aral Alexander explaining the asynchronous maneuver to Padma with the salt cellar and two forks; and Quinn, seated — by the hostess’s arrangement — at her right hand, observing all of it with the expression of a commander inspecting a superb ship not her own.

“Confess, Admiral,” said Ekaterin quietly, under the noise. “You are counting the exits.”

“I counted them on the way in, my lady. Six, not including windows.” Quinn sipped. “Now I am counting something else. Him.” She nodded across the table at Miles, who at that moment was conducting three conversations and one passed dish simultaneously. “For thirty years I remember him as a shell in flight. I look at him tonight and I can’t get used to it: the shell has landed. And it hasn’t gone out, mind you — it has landed still burning, and it burns like that, in place, for a quarter of a century. I didn’t know that was physically possible.” She set down the glass. “You are a good engineer, Lady Vorkosigan.”

“I didn’t land him,” said Ekaterin. “He landed himself. I only… saw to it that the runway was the length he needs. A little longer than it looks from outside.”

Quinn looked at her for two seconds. Then she nodded — once, like a seal, with the gesture with which she accepted good reports.

“Verified word for word,” she said. “The legend is accurate.”

IV. The Library

After dinner the house spread out through its rooms, the way good evenings spread, and in the library, by the fire, there gathered the circle that had always gathered there: the old, the young, and the notebooks.

The shelf of notebooks stood to the left of the fireplace — the family shelf, which the guests knew about and outsiders never suspected: the journals of three generations, bound, labeled, in order. Miles took one down, then a second, telling the children sprawled on the carpet — Ivan’s grandchildren and his own younger ones — the story of the shelf: here were their grandfather Aral’s field notebooks, in the handwriting that deteriorates through the wars and recovers through the peaces; here was his own old notebook — “the original is with your brother Aral Alexander; this is a copy, because an archive is an archive”; here were the new ones.

“And mine is here,” said Simone, and produced hers from a pocket: worn by now, soft at the corners, one year old. “The first page is Father’s. The second is a list. May I…” she hesitated for the first time that evening, “…may I read it? The list. Mr. Illyan said he wanted to hear it.”

“I did say so,” Simon Illyan confirmed from his armchair, “and I stand by my word. Read, young lady. Slowly.”

And Simone Vorkosigan, thirteen, standing by the fire with a beret on her head and a notebook in her hands, read aloud the list from Middlestone Pass: fourteen names, with surnames, in the order of the count. The room listened quietly. Quinn, standing by the door, watched the beret and did not move.

“Remarkable,” said Illyan at the end. “Do you know what this room has just heard, child? The best archive in the universe: a person who remembers people. For thirty years I carried in my head a machine that remembered everything — and the most important thing I learned only when I lost it: the archive is not the thing that holds the data. The archive is the one who knows what is dear. You know. Keep your notebook — and remember that it is only the backup copy. The original is you.”

“And the beret,” came Quinn’s voice from the doorway, half a tone lower than usual, “the beret, young lady, I have known since before you were born. It was worn by a certain small admiral with a weakness for impossible plans. The gray felt is Dendarii, the cut is ours, and the stain on the inside of the band — don’t look for it now — is coffee, spilled at Dagoola, and I was two meters away when it spilled.” She crossed the room and adjusted the beret on the child’s head with two fingers, precisely, by regulation. “It is worn tilted a touch to the left. Like so. The admiral would have approved of her kit, Vorkosigan,” she tossed over her shoulder. “It suits her better than it suited you.”

“Everything suits everyone better than it suits me,” said Miles benignly. “I compensate with content.”

V. The Garden

The winter garden took them in later, by twos and threes, the way winter gardens take in good evenings — for the quiet conversations the big rooms cannot hold.

Mark and Miles stood a while between the skellytum and the glass — the two brothers, the round one and the small one, fifty-one and fifty-seven — and their conversation was brief, brother-fashion: “How is it downstairs?” — “Quiet. For a year now — quiet. Kareen says I’ve stopped paying interest.” — “She was always the better accountant of the two of us.” — “Of the three of us, brother. We count the deceased.” — and both of them laughed in a way that would have puzzled any onlooker, and that closed between them the annual inspection: alive, whole, their own.

Cordelia and Quinn crossed paths by the roses — ninety-two and sixty-three, the two admiral-women of this family — and what exactly they said to each other nobody heard, but when they came out of the garden they were walking arm in arm, Cordelia declaiming: “…and so I told him: Captain, either you put down that weapon or I relieve you of duty, and he, poor dear…” — and Quinn was laughing in a voice she had not laughed in for years.

And Ekaterin found Quinn once more, later, for a minute, by the terrace door, where the admiral stood alone, watching the snow.

“He proposed to me once,” said Quinn, without preamble, because between these two women the preambles had ended at the front door. “Long ago, before you. That I become Lady Vorkosigan. I refused in three seconds, and then spent thirty years checking whether I had been right.” She turned. “Tonight I closed the file, my lady. I am not a coastline. I am the open water — that is where I belong, and Ylla sang it to me with eighty tons of bass. But he… he needed exactly a shore. This kind: with a long runway, a garden, and a shelf for notebooks. The two of you chose each other correctly. I wanted to say it aloud, once, before a witness — snow counts as a witness — and never return to the subject.”

“Received and filed,” said Ekaterin quietly, family-fashion. “And from me, for the same record, one thing: half of what I love in him was built by the open water. I only maintain the structure. Thank you, Admiral. For the building.”

The two women stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the snow. Then Quinn said, “Well. That’s enough feelings for one decade,” — and they went back inside, where Ivan had just knocked over something made of glass to general applause.

VI. The Song

The late guests arrived at a quarter to eleven, on foot, from the lower end of the drive, stamping off the snow like men accustomed to arriving without cars and without announcement: two of them, one older, one young, in plain winter coats. Roic met them at the door, looked at them — and bowed a millimeter deeper than plain coats warranted.

“Good evening,” said the older one. “We were told good workers are received here. We carry a reference.”

“Your reference hangs framed in a certain study, sir,” said Roic with a face of stone. “Come in. My lord is expecting you by the window.”

Greg and Sav — under those names they were presented to the younger children, and the younger children, raised in this house, asked nothing, while the older ones rose to their feet without a command — stayed an hour: one glass, one toast (“To the shift — in every sense of the word,” said the older one, and half the room understood, and the other half guessed), one game of salt-cellar-and-forks with Padma on the asynchronous maneuver, and ten minutes by the window with the host, quietly, shoulder to shoulder, about things that are not written down. On leaving, the older one paused before Simone, looked at the beret and the list in her lap, and said:

“Keep counting, young lady. It is the rarest profession in the Imperium. I know what I’m talking about — I spend all day counting other things.”

And after the snow had taken them back down the drive, Tej at last brought in the box she had surrendered to no one since the doorstep, and announced that Ylla’s present was to be listened to, not unwrapped. The box proved to be a projector with a recording — and the great hall went dark, its walls filled with an ocean under two moons, and out of the depth of the recording rose a sound that stopped every conversation in the middle of a word: the song of the leviathans. The work song of Ylla — the four-count one, with which ten giants and seven chance-met ships had hauled a hotel back from the edge of an abyss — and woven deep inside it, slowed to a lullaby, for those who knew how to listen: the Dendarii march.

Quinn stood in the middle of the hall, in the dark, amid the ocean, listening to her own name sung by eighty tons of gratitude — four beats, even, strong, that do not stop — and around her stood, not breathing, the entire motley, impossible, living household of the man with whom she had shared the open water, and listened with her.

When the recording ended and the lights returned, no one spoke at once. Then Cordelia Vorkosigan said into the silence, in a voice that held ninety-two years and not one to spare:

“Well. Now I have seen everything. The rest is repetition.”

VII. The Count

They dispersed after midnight, Barrayaran-slow — with returns from the threshold, with sentences finished on the steps, with boxes of leftover roast pressed by the cook upon everyone below a certain rank — and the house emptied room by room, returning to its winter pulse.

Nikolai Vorsoisson arrived at ten past twelve — straight from orbit, on a courier run delayed by the storm over the northern continent, frost still on his collar and an apology prepared three wormholes back — and was met at the door by his mother, who said nothing, only held him by the shoulders for one long second: the living muster, the mother’s count, the last line filled in. Then they fed him in the kitchen, family-fashion, and he talked about Odera and the new throats with his mouth full, while Miles sat across from him and listened with a face no Auditorial report ever received.

And the youngest made her round of the house at half past twelve — by her own route, with her own notebook — counted the guests in the rooms of leave-taking, the household on the stairs, the armsmen at their posts, Ivan’s sleeping children on the sofa (two), the cook’s cat (one, evading protocol) — and presented herself in the library, where her father was putting out the lamps, with the final report:

“Twenty-nine came, twenty-nine seen off or bedded down. Plus Nikki — thirty. Plus the posts, the household, and the cat. Everyone is counted, Father. Everyone comes out even.”

“Everyone comes out even,” repeated Miles Vorkosigan. He looked at his daughter — the beret, the notebook, the serious eyes — then at the empty great hall, where the table had already been taken down and the last log was burning low in the fireplace, and said: “You know, that is the best report I have ever received. Not tonight. Ever. Go to bed, Simone. Tomorrow is the holiday.”

Left alone, he stood a while longer in the library, by the shelf. Then — by a habit he had no intention of curing — he took down his own new notebook, sat by the dying fire, and wrote, in the small hand, the line with which the evening closed for him, and the year, and something longer than a year:

Winterfair. The house full, the table long, the snow on schedule. Quinn came through the gate — thirty years late, exactly on time. Mother saw everything and declared the rest repetition; she is wrong in one thing only: this does not repeat — this continues. I learned the difference late, and I record it for whoever shall one day read: cargoes are inventoried, legacies are counted, and people — people are loved by name. Everyone is counted. Everyone comes out even. The door stays open — by our method: by walking through it.

He closed the notebook, returned it to the shelf — between his father’s journals and the empty space left for the next ones — put out the last lamp, and went up to his wife, in the house that slept beneath the snow: full, counted, their own.

Outside, over Vorbarr Sultana, it went on snowing — large, slow, ceremonial. The snow was in no hurry. It had a whole city to tuck in, and the night was long, and warm in the way that only the coldest nights are warm: from inside.

Tuning Fork

An adventure of Admiral Elli Quinn and the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet

I. The Theft

The new flagship of the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet was the most beautiful thing Elli Quinn had ever bought with other people’s blood and her own signatures: one hundred and sixty meters of Illyrican shipbuilding poetry, hull number 47 of the brand-new Fan class, with next-generation Necklin architecture — the “double phase fan,” as the engineers called it in the intonation of clergy — paid for with twenty years of Dendarii savings, two mortgages on the Triumph, and one sold escort frigate. Six days remained to handover. Quinn had come to Illyrica in person, with half her staff, for the acceptance trials and for the christening, for which no name had yet been chosen.

Hull 47 was stolen on Thursday, at eleven oh four local time, in the middle of its own certification trial.

“Senior acceptance pilot Kera Volan,” Dock Director Maren Sol reported, gray with fury and sleeplessness, at the emergency meeting of shipyard representatives, the insurer, and one very quiet, very frightening mercenary admiral. “Eighteen years with the company. Lead acceptance pilot for the entire Fan series — she knows that hull better than the designers, because the designers computed it and she flew it. Trial number eleven: maneuvering cycle with minimum crew, automation in acceptance mode, seven aboard. At minute forty-one she declared a simulated emergency, put the six engineers into the escape capsule per protocol… and continued on course. Alone. With the ship.”

“Demands?” the insurer asked hopefully.

“None. The transponder has been dead since eleven oh six. Her course at last fix pointed to Fell-Nine node — the Jacksonian direction.”

“The Jacksonian direction,” Quinn repeated. A strange calm was spreading through her — the calm of an old hunter looking at a trail that is too clear. “Of course. Where else does one run with the most recognizable hull in the Ring.” She turned to her staff. “Hereld, Triumph ready for departure in six hours. And get me everything on Kera Volan. Not the service record — that will be immaculate. The personal file. Because service records steal money, Captain. Ships get stolen by biographies.”

II. The Question “Why”

Quinn opened the council aboard the Triumph that night with the exercise a very short admiral had taught her thirty-five years before, and which she had been passing on to every generation of officers since: investigate not the trail, but the meaning.

“Given: hull 47 is stolen. Question: what is a stolen hull 47 even good for?” She looked around the table. “To sell? To whom. It is the most photographed ship in the sector for six months, with a unique fan signature recognizable to any jump pilot a kilometer away in five-space. Jacksonians would buy it only to part it out, and parted out it’s worth a twentieth of the risk of the theft. To hide it? You can’t — see above. To fight with it? Combat complement forty; the automation lets one person fly it, but not fight it. Ransom? No demand, and every hour of silence lowers the price.” Quinn tapped the table. “Therefore the ship was not stolen to be owned. It was stolen to be used. Once. For something no other ship in the galaxy can do. So, ladies and gentlemen: what can it alone do?”

Silence. Then the navigator, slowly:

“The fan. The double phase geometry. Illyrica’s advertising calls it ‘the future of difficult passages’ — transiting throats of high variance that standard rods can’t hold…”

“Unstable wormholes,” said Hereld, already querying the registry. “Admiral, searching active emergency bulletins for destabilized nodes…” A pause, in which her face changed. “Here it is. Node XR-9, side branch beyond Illyrican space. A young passage, discovered four years ago. Two years ago a joint Illyrican-Escobaran survey expedition went through it — Project Boundary Line, forty-one people, a base on the far side. Eight months ago the passage began to decay — eighteen months ahead of forecast. The rescue commission convened three times. All three times the conclusion was the same: transiting a throat in terminal phase is beyond the rated capabilities of any available vessel. The operation is postponed ‘pending emergence of a technical possibility.’” Hereld looked up. “Forecast collapse of the passage: in about three weeks.”

“And the expedition roster?” Quinn asked, though she already knew. Meaning always fell into place with that quiet, final click.

“Jump pilot of Boundary Line: Iris Volan.” Hereld set down the tablet. “The younger sister.”

Quinn leaned back and closed her eyes for a moment. Her anger — twenty years of savings, two mortgages — stayed where it was, intact. But beside it, uninvited, sat something else, old and uncomfortable as a scar: the memory of every occasion on which she herself had broken laws, borders, and orders, because on the other side of the ledger there were people.

“Course for XR-9,” she said. “Maximum cruise. The Jacksonian trail is for the insurer and for the naive. We are not chasing a thief, ladies and gentlemen. We are chasing family with a deadline. And family with a deadline doesn’t run from anyone. It runs toward.”

III. The Race

The Triumph covered the eight jumps to the side branch in eleven days, which for a fifty-year-old hull was a feat and sounded like one: the old flagship creaked, groaned, and twice sent damage-control parties to engineering, but held the pace with the stubbornness of a veteran who has been told the campaign is his last.

Along the way they gathered Volan’s trail — because there was a trail, and it was the strangest Quinn had ever seen. At Kern transfer station the thief had loaded reactor mass and provisions, paying with an Illyrican shipwright’s letter of credit… her personal one, covered to the last mark. At Tau node she had bought eighteen escape capsules and a medical module — again paid, again with a receipt, with a letter attached for the vendor: “If I have not confirmed delivery within thirty days, apply to the law office of Selm and Daro, Illyrica, regarding the penalties.” At the third stop she had left a message addressed directly to her pursuers, registered at the public node under “for Admiral Quinn, to be called for”:

Admiral. I know what I stole, and from whom. I know what it costs. The firm of Selm and Daro holds power of attorney over my entire property, pension fund, and inheritance share — insufficient, I am aware. The remainder I will pay off for the rest of my life, in whatever measure a court, or You, determine. But my sister and forty people are dying behind a door that is closing, and Your ship is the only key in existence in the universe. The commissions deliberated for eight months. The passage has three weeks. I stopped waiting. I am sorry about the mortgages. — K. Volan.

“An honest thief,” said Hereld, rereading. “I didn’t know the breed existed.”

“It exists,” said Quinn. “It’s called ‘a desperate professional.’ The most dangerous breed of all.” She was looking at the tactical projection: node XR-9, blinking in terminal forecast, and one brand-new, stolen point parked beside it for thirty hours. “She hasn’t fled with the ship, Hereld. She has brought it to station and is waiting. Do you know what she’s waiting for?”

“A window in the throat’s flicker?”

“That too. But mostly she is waiting for us.” Quinn rose. “Because one pilot, however brilliant, cannot simultaneously fly a dying wormhole and run the evacuation of forty-one people off a planetary base. She stole the ship knowing we would catch up. She is counting on us arriving angry… and staying as crew.” Quinn shook her head, somewhere between anger and its neighbor, admiration. “That, ladies and gentlemen, is the most brazen recruitment plan in the history of mercenary work. Ready my shuttle.”

She went aboard hull 47 alone, unarmed — what was the woman going to do, steal her? — and found on the bridge a woman of forty-odd, with Illyrican cheekbones, eyes ringed with sleeplessness, and hands that even at rest lay on the console like on the keyboard of an instrument. Kera Volan stood and came to attention — the gesture of a person surrendering command.

“Admiral Quinn. The ship is yours. It was always yours.” A pause, a single one, in which her whole composure hung by a thread. “The mission… the mission requests to be discussed.”

Quinn looked at her a long time. Then she looked around — the bridge of her new flagship, perfect, untouched, the smell of new ship that no technology had ever reproduced — and sat down in the command chair she was formally sitting in for the first time.

“It has been discussed,” she said. “It is approved. The ship is mine, Pilot Volan. The mission, from this moment, is ours. And the accounts — the accounts we will settle afterward, and I assure you they will be more terrible than any court.” She keyed the link. “Hereld? Transfer me the boarding party, the engineering reserve, and the surgeon. Then take the old man back to Tau node for backstop. We’re going in.”

IV. Between the Strokes

The packet from Professor Emerita Judith Riva — to whom Quinn had sent XR-9’s parameters by courier chain on day three, because there was one person in the galaxy who understood both dying wormholes and impossible deadlines — arrived with the last relay and began without a greeting, Komarran-style:

Admiral Quinn. I have examined your patient. Diagnosis: terminal phase, collapse in 120 hours, plus or minus the flicker, and the flicker is the whole question. The throat is no longer a door — it is a bell that is ringing itself, faster and faster and more and more unevenly. Let a standard ship enter such a resonance and it comes apart along the seams of its field. Your double fan, however, can do something its own builders advertise without having fully understood it: it can CHANGE phase in motion. In other words — your ship can pass not THROUGH the strokes of the bell, but BETWEEN them. Enclosed: a rhythmogram of the flicker and the computed windows. Warning the first: every transit eats about thirty hours of the passage’s life. You have one crossing out and one back. There is no third. Warning the second: the rhythm will accelerate. On the return leg your windows will be half as wide. Warning the third, personal: if you succeed, I want the telemetry. All of it. No one has ever recorded the death of a wormhole from inside. Make an old theoretician happy. — Riva.

The transit took forty minutes, which the crew of hull 47 remembered forever and always described badly, because there were no good words for it. Volan flew. Quinn, in the chair beside her, watched the woman and the ship stop being two things: Riva’s rhythmogram ran down the main screen like a musical score, the throat ahead contracted and released in a ragged, accelerating pulse, and in the pauses — in the narrow, black-velvet pauses between the strokes of the dying bell — Volan threaded one hundred and sixty meters of ship with movements as small as breathing. The double fan sang at frequencies that made the crew’s bones resonate. Once, a window closed half a second early, and Volan changed phase in motion, without an order, without a breath, and the ship passed through the stroke as through a curtain; only the lights blinked, and somewhere far down the hull something moaned.

Then — stars. Foreign ones, arranged in unknown constellations, on the other side of the door.

“Transit complete,” said Kera Volan in a voice worn down to a thread. Her hands on the console were trembling finely, only now. “Welcome to the Boundary Line system, Admiral.”

“Well flown, Pilot.” Quinn rose. “Rest forty minutes, then descent. Riva’s clock is running: ninety hours to the last possible return window. Let’s go collect your people.”

V. The Other Side

The Boundary Line base clung to a rocky world with a thin, unbreathable atmosphere — three linked domes, a solar field half-buried by a dust storm, and forty-one people who for eight months had watched their sky prepare to lock itself forever. The base reactor was at eighty percent decay, the recyclers on manual respiration; they had endured with the discipline of a good expedition and with that particular quiet despair of people who every evening calculate which will run out first — the water, the air, or the door.

Hull 47’s shuttles landed between two squalls. Quinn came down with the first and found drawn up at the airlock forty-one people — gaunt, bearded, in patched suits — and one small woman with Illyrican cheekbones and a jump pilot’s tabs, who was looking past the admiral, toward the second shuttle, from which her sister was just emerging.

The meeting of the Volan sisters lasted nine seconds and included not one word. Quinn turned away, because there are embraces that are not for strangers’ eyes, and busied herself with what she knew: an evacuation schedule.

The schedule was brutal and simple. People — all of them, no exceptions. Data — all of it: two years of observations of a young wormhole in decay, a treasure for which Riva would have sold the faculty’s soul. Equipment — nothing but the medical. There were no arguments; the people of Boundary Line were scientists and knew how to read clocks. The single complication came from the sky: the great dust storm circling the planet brought its front back twelve hours early, and the last two shuttle runs lifted off into brown darkness, on instruments, flown by pilots who afterward stood under the showers of hull 47 for a long time and said nothing.

At hour forty from touchdown, the last person of Boundary Line — the expedition chief, who by old sailors’ custom left the base last and locked its airlock with a code no one would ever come to open — stepped onto the deck of the ship without a name. At hour forty-one, hull 47 lay onto a course for the passage.

And then the bell accelerated.

“The flicker is leaving the forecast,” Volan reported from the console, Riva’s rhythmogram before her, the live data lying across it more and more askew. “The period is dropping. The windows are shortening… Admiral, at this dynamic the last usable return window is not in forty hours. It’s in eleven. Maybe ten.”

“Do we make it?”

“We make it to the throat in nine and a half.” Volan looked up, and in her eyes Quinn saw the pure, naked arithmetic. “But the windows there will already be half the width of the outbound ones. The fan’s rating plate doesn’t cover phase changes like that. No rating plate covers them.”

“And you?”

Kera Volan looked at the console — at the ship she had test-flown for eighteen months, bolt by bolt, field by field, lie by lie in the advertising brochures.

“I built this machine, Admiral. The rating plate lies twenty percent low. I know exactly where.”

VI. The Closing Door

The final transit of node XR-9 lasted twenty-two minutes and entered the history of shiphandling under the name an Escobaran journalist later gave it, which stuck forever: the race with the closing door.

The throat met them transformed — a bell in its death-ring, a pulse ragged to arrhythmia, windows narrow as the gap under a door. Volan fed the fan one hundred and twenty percent, and it sang a new sound, high and pure, on the very edge of coming apart. Quinn, strapped into the chair beside her, watched the pilot stop breathing in rhythm with the windows and start breathing in the rhythm between them; watched the ship take the first three strokes by Riva’s score, the fourth by a correction computed live, the fifth by something that was neither mathematics nor instinct, but eighteen months of marriage between a human and a machine.

On the sixth stroke, the bell lied. The window, computed at four seconds, began closing at two, and in the span of one human tremor Kera Volan did the impossible: she shifted the phase of the two fans separately, asynchronously — a maneuver that existed in no manual, because no designer had ever imagined it would be needed — and slid the ship through the closing slit sideways, like a sheet of paper under a falling guillotine. The port fan screamed and died — overloaded, slagged, heroic. The starboard one carried them out.

They emerged into the normal space of the side branch with one living fan, forty-one rescued, two tons of priceless data, and a view astern that everyone aboard remembered to the end of their days: node XR-9, invisible to the eye and deafening to the instruments, struck one last, long, descending note — and ceased to exist. The door closed forty seconds behind them.

In the silence that fell over the bridge, Kera Volan took her hands off the console, folded them in her lap, and wept — soundlessly, motionlessly, with open eyes still fixed on the screen. From the sensor station, Iris Volan crossed half the bridge to her and embraced her from behind, over the seat back, with both arms and all her weight.

“Dendarii,” said Admiral Elli Quinn on the all-hands channel, in a voice that held perfectly and that almost everyone believed, “log the following. Today this ship did something no ship before it has ever done. It left port as stolen property. It is coming home a legend.” A pause. “Course for Tau node. Let’s go reassure an insurer.”

VII. The Christening

The settling of accounts took a month and a half and went more easily than it had any right to, for three reasons.

The first was called telemetry. The record of the two transits — the only data in history from inside a dying wormhole, including the asynchronous maneuver, which Illyrica’s engineers watched on replay with the expressions of votaries — proved to have a market value exceeding the damages, the theft, and the slagged fan combined. The shipyard withdrew all charges in exchange for exclusive rights, fitted a new port fan free of charge, and — at the insistence of its marketing department, which Director Sol conveyed through her teeth — requested permission to use in the series’ advertising the phrase “tested beyond a closing door.”

The second was called the insurer. The assessor from Lloyd-Tau, come to inventory losses, went home having inventoried a miracle, and hull 47’s policy was re-signed with a rider Quinn had never seen in a forty-year career: a discount for demonstrated survivability.

The third was called the sentence — because a sentence there nonetheless had to be, and Quinn pronounced it personally, on the bridge, before the assembled ranks, with the full weight of her admiral’s authority:

“Kera Volan. For the theft of a flagship, two wrecked mortgages, and one staff aged ten years — you are appointed chief pilot of the Dendarii Free Mercenary Fleet, with a probationary period extending to the end of your natural life. Your pay is garnished at fifty percent until the insurance deductible is covered, which by my calculation will take eleven years.” A pause. “Welcome to the fleet, Pilot. Here we steal more rarely, but we fly the same way.”

“Yes, Admiral,” said Volan, her eyes suspiciously bright above the regulation bearing. “One correction only. Eleven years and four months. I’ve done the sums.”

The christening took place in orbit off Tau node, before the drawn-up crews of two ships — the new one, perfect, with one brand-new and one heroically scorched mounting seam on the port fan, and the old Triumph, standing alongside like a white-muzzled wolf beside a yearling. Quinn broke the bottle on hull 47’s bow herself, and pronounced the name she had chosen during the twenty-two minutes of the transit, and which no one in the fleet disputed:

“I name you Tuning Fork. Because you passed through a dying bell and caught its pitch. Bear it well.”

And in the evening, in her cabin aboard the new flagship — the new-ship smell still there, but mixed now with coffee, ozone, and life — she wrote the customary short letter through the four relays:

Miles. The fleet has a new flagship, a new chief pilot, and a new legend, and I have eleven years of guaranteed pay garnishments, which accounting has been celebrating for two days. The short version: my ship was stolen to save forty-one people; I ran the thief down and completed the theft personally. The long version requires a glass of something strong. The lesson for your textbook: the best way to have your ship stolen is for the thief to be right. P.S. Illyrica is already building hull 48, same class, for an outside buyer. I asked, as a joke, whether they had changed the acceptance codes after our case. They did not laugh fast enough. If you ever hear that one has vanished too — remember that there is exactly one ship in the galaxy that can catch it, and it is mine. — Q.

The reply came in sixteen hours and consisted of one sentence, which Quinn read twice and decided to show no one, because the crew was superstitious enough as it was:

Quinn, dear — after thirty-five years’ acquaintance, you ought to know that such things are not to be written out loud.

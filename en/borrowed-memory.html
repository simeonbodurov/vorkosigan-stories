Borrowed Memory

An adventure of Lord Auditor Miles Vorkosigan at the Imperial palace

I. The Leak

The East Chamber of the Imperial Residence was the most secure room on Barrayar and the surrounding sixteen worlds: windowless, with active acoustic armor, swept for listening devices twice daily by two independent teams who checked each other, with controlled air, controlled light, and a controlled list of participants, certified personally by the chief of ImpSec. In the East Chamber, Emperor Gregor Vorbarra held the sessions of the Council for Interstellar Infrastructure — the body that decided where the Imperium would build jump stations, which wormholes it would develop, and which billion-mark contracts it would sign over the coming decade.

And the East Chamber leaked.

“Three cases in eleven months,” reported General Duv Galeni, chief of ImpSec, with the face of a man who had been sleeping badly for eleven months. “First: the Cetagandan trade delegation at the Komarr-South corridor negotiations arrives with a position matching our fallback strategy word for word — a strategy discussed only in the East Chamber. Second: a Jacksonian consortium preempts the tender for Station Zero with a bid tailored to our ceiling within half a percent. Third, a month ago: the route of the border-buoy inspection, approved in the East Chamber on Wednesday, is known to a smuggling network by Friday.” Galeni set down the tablet. “Sire, my Lord Auditor: the room is clean. I swear it on my career — clean. There is no device, no emission, no recording. We checked the participants under fast-penta — all of them, down to the last stenographer, voluntarily, twice. All clean. Nobody is lying. And nevertheless every word walks out.”

“So we have a room that doesn’t leak, and people who don’t lie,” said Gregor. The Emperor at fifty-eight was grayer and quieter than Miles remembered from their youth, and far more frightening in his quiet. “And the information leaks. That, gentlemen, is not a contradiction. That is a wrongly posed question.” He turned to Miles. “Which is why I called you. You have fed on wrongly posed questions all your life. Find the right one.”

Miles sat late over the transcripts that night, in his study at Vorkosigan House, with tea cooling on its established schedule and with the sensation — old, familiar, tickling at the back of his neck — that he was watching a conjuring trick whose mechanism stood in plain sight. A room that doesn’t leak. People who don’t lie. Fast-penta that clears everyone.

“Nobody is lying,” he wrote in his notebook, on paper, in the family way. And beneath it, slowly: “But fast-penta doesn’t ask what you know. It asks what you hide. And if someone is carrying it out without hiding anything? If the leak… doesn’t know it’s leaking?”

He looked at what he had written and felt, across thirty years of service, across half a lifetime, another investigation looking back at him — another man, and another chip — and he put down the pen, because his hand was not entirely steady.

II. The Intersection

The method was as old as the trade, and Miles applied it with the patience of a man who has been told not to hurry, which always made him hurry twice as fast.

First: the intersection. The three leaked topics had been discussed at sessions with different, rotating attendance; the intersection of the three lists gave seven people — four ministerial, two military, one permanent secretary. Second: the marking. With Gregor’s blessing, the next session included a false annex — “preliminary coordinates of Station Delta,” a nonexistent project — and each of the seven received his own version, with microscopically different figures. A classic, called in the textbooks a canary trap: whichever version sings its way out points to the man.

The canary sang within nineteen days. A Jacksonian info-broker offered a Cetagandan middleman “the Delta coordinates” — and the figures in the offer matched, to the last digit, the version handed to Lord Cassian Vorin, permanent secretary of the Council, forty-nine years old, twenty-six years of blameless service.

“Have we detained him?” asked Galeni.

“We have tested him,” said Miles. “A third time under fast-penta, this morning, under the pretext of routine recertification. Galeni, I stood behind the glass. The man is clean. No evasion, no block, no conditioning — your technicians swore to it, and I believe them, because I was watching his face. We asked him, under a full dose: ‘Have you ever passed information from the sessions?’ He said ‘no’ — and the fast-penta said he was telling the truth.” Miles paced the office, three steps out, three back, an old habit from small bridges. “So, General, we possess a paradox fit for a textbook: a man who leaks — proven; the canary does not lie — and who is not lying when he says he does not leak. Both at once. Which leaves exactly one possibility, and I don’t like it, because I know it personally.” He stopped. “The information does not pass through his knowledge. It passes through him. Lord Vorin is not a traitor, Galeni. Lord Vorin is a carrier.”

III. The Interview

Miles arranged a conversation with Vorin under the most natural pretext in the world — an Auditorial review of the Council’s archival practice — and spent two hours with him in his office: a bright room lined with folders in pedantic order, with holos of two daughters on the desk, and with a teapot from which the permanent secretary poured two cups of a golden, mountain-scented tea before they began.

Lord Cassian Vorin proved to be one of those officials on whom empires rest and about whom no songs are written: precise, modest, in love with his work with the quiet, deep love of a man who has found his place. And with a memory that, twenty minutes into the conversation, made the hair on the back of Miles’s neck stand up.

“The session of the eighth, last month?” Vorin did not even reach for a folder. “Of course. Item three, remarks by Minister Vorloupulous, I quote: ‘The Komarr-South corridor is not an infrastructure project but’ — here he paused and tapped the table twice — ‘a political one, and whoever claims otherwise has not read his own budget.’ General Metz objected in the words…” and Vorin recited the objection — forty seconds of speech, with the pauses, with one of the general’s ’um’s in the middle, with the footnote.

“Impressive,” said Miles, perfectly casual. “A stenographer’s memory. Innate?”

“Oh no, my lord, if only!” Vorin laughed, openly, with the pride of a man sharing a small private victory. “Until forty-five I remembered like everyone — that is, badly. Then, four years ago, I had my back operated on Escobar — a disc correction, the Callisto Institute; it was recommended to me, an excellent clinic. And there, during recovery, one of the doctors prescribed me this.” He raised the cup of golden tea. “Vardia. A mountain herb, Escobaran, drunk as tea, two cups a day. ‘Supports memory consolidation,’ the doctor said, and I confess I smirked. And half a year later I caught myself remembering sessions verbatim. Verbatim, my lord! My wife jokes that I have drunk an entire meadow. I have ordered it regularly ever since — there is a teahouse here in Vorbarr Sultana that imports it, the Green Stone, by the south gate. Without it, frankly, I’d be afraid — at my post, memory is half the service.”

“And your dreams?” Miles asked. The question came out on its own, ahead of thought, from a place he would recognize only that evening.

Vorin blinked.

“Strange that you should ask. I don’t dream. That is — surely I dream, everyone dreams, but I haven’t remembered a single dream since…” he considered, and for the first time a shadow of puzzlement crossed his face, “…since the operation, in fact. Funny. A man with my memory — and my dreams slip away, every one. My wife says I’ve sold my dreams for meeting minutes.” He smiled and finished his tea. “I sold them cheap, my lord, between us. Nobody misses the minutes.”

Miles took his leave with all available charm, descended the ministry stairs at an even pace, got into the groundcar — and only there, behind the tinted glass, allowed himself to close his eyes. A verbatim memory, appearing at forty-five. After a procedure on another planet. With a convenient, beloved, everyday explanation supplied by the operators themselves. And vanished dreams.

He had seen a memory like that. For thirty years, every day, across one desk at ImpSec: the memory of Simon Illyan, the man with the chip. And he knew — from the most personal, worst case of his career — what happens when such a memory turns out to be somebody else’s property.

“Drive,” he said to the driver. “Not home. To Uncle Simon’s.”

IV. Simon

Simon Illyan, retired for a quarter of a century, lived with Lady Alys in a house with a garden in the old southern suburb, where the afternoons were long and the past was arranged on shelves nobody reached for without need. At eighty-four the former chief of ImpSec had grown thin and translucent; his memory of the chip years was in rags — the organic brain, having relied for decades on the crystal, had archived almost nothing of its own — but the mind in the gaps was the same: quiet, sharp, terrifying.

He heard Miles out to the end without interrupting, a cup of tea cooling in his hand by the old habit of a man for whom tea had always been a prop.

“A passive variant,” he said at last. “My chip was interfaced: I knew; I called up the data consciously; I lived with a second archive in my head. This is something else, simpler and viler: a recording implant without an interface, hung on the hippocampus. The carrier knows nothing — but the side effect is unavoidable: his memory consolidation is reinforced, because the chip doubles the recording. Hence the ‘photographic memory.’ And the herb…” Illyan smiled with a quarter of his mouth. “The herb is beautiful work, Miles. Professional. A man with a suddenly perfect memory asks questions. A man with a miracle tea does not. They gave him an explanation he loves, maintains, and defends himself. The best cover story is the one the agent tells himself with pleasure.”

“And the dreams?”

“Dreams are consolidation. The chip steals it — intercepts the stream before the brain can dream it out.” Illyan set down the cup, and his hand, Miles noticed, was quite steady; only his voice had dropped half a tone. “I did not dream for thirty years. Nobody warned me that was the price. Your Lord Vorin at least has slept peacefully — he doesn’t know what he’s missing.” A pause. “The extraction, Miles. When you get there. The surgery is different now, the risks are small, but his memory afterward will be human again — that is, bad; that is, his own. He will grieve. People think we grieve for what we’ve lost; the truth is we grieve for what we believed was ours. I want to be there when they tell him. I am the only living man on this world who knows what that silence sounds like from the inside.”

From the doorway Lady Alys, ninety years old and straight as protocol, saw them off with a single sentence, addressed to Miles with the familiar cool tenderness:

“Bring his life back to him whole, boy. Simon’s, in his day, you returned to him in parts — and I counted the parts.”

V. The Confirmation

They confirmed quietly, because the operators of a four-year asset must sense nothing until the final hour.

The chip was found at a scheduled preventive checkup, in which the neurological scanner had been “calibrated” by an ImpSec technician: a thread of crystal, thin as a hair and long as a fingernail, laid beside the brainstem with a surgery that made the Imperial physicians fall silent with professional envy. A miniaturized descendant of the eidetic chips of Illyan’s era — no interface, no emissions, pure recording, a buffer good for months.

The exfiltration channel they found in a week of surveillance, and it possessed the elegance of the good traps — hidden inside the cover story itself. The Green Stone teahouse by the south gate: a quiet establishment, fifteen years at the same address, with a steady clientele and one steady customer who came every first Friday of the month for a packet of vardia and stayed for a cup of “the new harvest” — always at the same table, by the wall, in the most comfortable armchair. In the chair’s backrest, under the upholstery, Galeni’s technicians found an induction plate: forty minutes of tasting — and the chip’s buffer poured itself into the receiver to the last second of recording. Lord Vorin had been delivering the Imperium’s state secrets every first Friday, over a cup of his favorite tea, with a clean conscience and a loyalty card.

The thread upward Venek and her team unwound in nine days: the teahouse was a node of a network trading under the name that the reports began to capitalize — the Archive. Jacksonian in financing, Escobaran in surgery, galactic in ambition: the Callisto Institute had for years been implanting “passive archives” in carefully selected patients — promising mid-career officials, the kind who five years later sit down at the important tables — and then patiently waiting for the assets to ripen. The list, extracted from one poorly defended accounting base, held thirty-one names from eleven polities. The buyers of the recordings included three intelligence services, two syndicates, and one ghem faction whose name made Galeni pour himself a second cup.

“Thirty-one people,” said Gregor very quietly, when Miles reported. “Thirty-one innocents, each with a favorite herb.” The Emperor studied the map of the Nexus for a while, then said: “First, our man. Then, the same day — the list goes to Escobar, Komarr, Pol, and every polity affected, through the channels of good will: a thing like this is not hoarded for leverage, Miles; a thing like this is extinguished everywhere at once, or it has not been extinguished. As for the clinic and the Archive…” Gregor rarely finished such sentences, and did not finish this one. “Galeni knows the procedure. Go to Vorin. And, Miles — take Illyan. Imperial grace has limits of competence; what awaits that man lies beyond them.”

VI. The Conversation

They told him at Vorkosigan House, in the library, because Miles refused to let it happen in an office with a protocol: Lord Cassian Vorin, summoned “for consultation,” found waiting there the Auditor, the chief of ImpSec, one very old gentleman whom he recognized with a gasp — and the Emperor, which brought him to attention and buckled his knees at the same time.

Miles told it — slowly, in order, without one softening, because his mother had taught him that stories with the cuts left in are more dangerous than enemies. The operation. The chip. The leak. The canary. The armchair at the Green Stone. Vorin listened standing, whitening in layers, and when Miles finished, he asked only one question, in a voice that made Illyan look away:

“The three leaked sessions… the border-buoy coordinates… The smuggling network knew the route. Is there… did anyone die because of what went out through me?”

“No,” said Galeni. “It has been verified. Financial damage, diplomatic — yes. No blood.”

“No blood,” Vorin repeated. His legs gave way after all, and he sat, and for a while he looked at his hands. “Twenty-six years I have served. I have sworn twice — to the old Emperor and to you, Sire. And all that time… I was the device. They swept the chamber twice a day, and they should have swept me.” He raised his head. “Sire. Whatever is required. Extraction, resignation, trial — whatever is required, only tell me what.”

“Trial — no, because your innocence is proven in the hardest currency of the Imperium: three rounds of fast-penta and one Auditorial investigation,” said Gregor. “Resignation — no, because the Imperium does not throw away servants like you; that is final. As for what is required — I will tell you, but I have no right to order it, so I will only ask. The chip in you is still recording, Lord Vorin. The operators do not know that we know. One last first Friday. One cup of tea. And one recording, composed by us — with a bait the Archive will reach for, and burn with all its buyers at once. Afterward — extraction, by my best surgeons.” Gregor paused. “I can send a double. I can end all of it today. But the trap is surest with the true carrier, and the true carrier is you — and so is the choice.”

Lord Cassian Vorin, permanent secretary, one of the men on whom empires rest, was silent exactly long enough to draw one breath.

“The first Friday is in eleven days,” he said. “I shall order from the new harvest.”

The last first Friday went off flawlessly: forty minutes of tasting, one buffer full of carefully prepared poison — a “preliminary protocol” for a nonexistent Imperial tender, cut so that any buyer who moved on it described himself — and one network that over the following three weeks burned from Vorbarr Sultana to Escobar: the teahouse, the couriers, the nodes, the Callisto Institute (closed by the Escobaran authorities with a formulation in which the word “medicine” did not appear), thirty-one carriers on eleven worlds — found, informed, freed, each from his own beloved cover story. The ghem faction on the buyers’ list sank into a silence that the ImpSec digests described with the by-now-traditional term.

VII. The Herb

The extraction was performed at the Imperial Military Hospital, in forty minutes, with a surgery about which Illyan, watching from the gallery, said only: “You’re a quarter of a century late with this technique, colleagues. But who’s counting.”

He was at the bedside when Vorin woke — at Vorin’s own request, made before the operation in the words Illyan carried with him afterward: “You are the only one who has been where I am coming back from.” And he stayed through the following weeks — an elderly gentleman with a tattered memory and a whole heart, coming for tea — while Lord Cassian Vorin learned anew to live with a human memory: that is, a bad one; that is, his own. Vorin grieved, exactly as Illyan had foretold — quietly, with an apologetic smile, reaching for recollections that no longer came verbatim — and Illyan told him, again and again, patiently, with the weight of a man who had paid everything for the knowledge:

“Memory is not what makes you you, Cassian. I lost thirty years of recordings and remained — furrowed, but myself. You are losing the transcript, but you keep the main thing, and the main thing was never in the chip: it is that for twenty-six years nobody ever had to check your word. Even when someone else’s will ran through you — yours did not move. The Imperium remembers that.” A pause, and a quarter of a smile. “And the Imperium, take it from an old archivist, remembers worse than you do even now.”

His first day back at work, Lord Vorin began with a cup of vardia — genuine, bought from a new importer vetted by ImpSec to the seventh generation. The herb, the Imperial laboratory established, did in fact have a mildly beneficial effect on memory: “within statistical error, but with a superb flavor.” Vorin no longer drank it for his memory. He drank it because he loved it, and because a man has the right to keep at least one of his cover stories — on his own side of the table, with his eyes open.

And Miles, a week later, finished his report to the Emperor in the study at Vorkosigan House, in the late hour when the house grew still, and by old habit added to the private notebook — the new one, begun after he had passed the old one to his son — the line with which the case closed for him:

Chambers can be armored, channels encrypted, devices found. People are not devices — and precisely for that reason they are at once the most vulnerable node and the only one worth defending. Sweep the rooms. But guard the people: their memory is borrowed only when someone steals it; returned, it becomes the one thing no chip ever records — trust.

He set down the pen, drank off the cooled tea — ordinary, Vorbarran, no miracles — and turned out the lamp. In the dark, somewhere across the Nexus, thirty-one people on eleven worlds were dreaming again that night. Miles did not know what they dreamed. He knew only that the dreams were their own — and that this, in the end, was the entire sentence handed down in the case.
